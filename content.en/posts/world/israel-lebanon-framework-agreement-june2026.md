## Core Summary

Israel and Lebanon signed a framework peace agreement on June 26, mediated by US Secretary of State Rubio, who called it "the first step toward peace." This marks the first such diplomatic document between the two nations through American mediation, representing a significant breakthrough in Middle East peace efforts. However, Hezbollah is not a party to the agreement, and previous ceasefires between Israel and Lebanon have still seen near-daily cross-border strikes.

## Event Details

According to AP News, the framework agreement was signed following trilateral talks hosted at the State Department. Secretary of State Rubio stated at the signing ceremony that this was "the first step toward peace" and pledged continued US engagement in subsequent negotiations.

The agreement's core provisions include establishing military liaison mechanisms, a principles framework for maritime boundary delineation, and a joint supervisory committee. However, full details have not been publicly released.

Notably, Hezbollah did not participate in signing the agreement. As Lebanon's most powerful armed force, controlling southern Lebanon, Hezbollah's confrontation with Israel lies at the heart of the Lebanon-Israel conflict. Multiple previous ceasefire agreements between Israel and the Lebanese government failed due to Hezbollah's non-compliance.

The timing is significant — falling on the same day as the US-Iran military escalation. Analysts suggest the US may be attempting to stabilize the broader Middle East situation through the Lebanon-Israel peace deal, preventing multiple fronts from spiraling out of control simultaneously.

## Panoramic Analysis

The signing of the Israel-Lebanon framework agreement represents one of the few positive signals in Middle East peace processes in recent years. Since the 2006 Lebanon War, the two countries have remained in a "no war, no peace" stalemate. The fact that the US could bring both sides to the negotiating table is itself a milestone.

However, the agreement's sustainability faces multiple challenges. First, Hezbollah's absence means the agreement lacks actual enforcement power over security in southern Lebanon. Hezbollah has significantly expanded its arsenal since 2006, estimated to possess over 100,000 rockets and missiles — military capability far exceeding the Lebanese national army. Any security arrangement excluding Hezbollah cannot fundamentally alter the Lebanon-Israel border security dynamic.

Second, the agreement's conclusion contrasts sharply with deteriorating US-Iran relations. If the US attempts to use "Lebanon-Israel reconciliation" as leverage against Iran, it may prove counterproductive — Iran could increase support for Hezbollah to undermine implementation.

More broadly, Middle East peace processes are shifting toward a "transactional" diplomatic model. The Trump administration favors bilateral agreements over multilateral frameworks for resolving regional disputes — a strategy that showed results with the Abraham Accords. But bilateral agreements' limitation lies in their inability to address structural roots of regional conflict.

## Multiple Perspectives

**US Perspective**: Rubio described the agreement as "a historic first step," emphasizing America's irreplaceable mediating role in Middle East peace. The White House said it would push for substantive follow-up negotiations.

**Israel's Position**: The Israeli PM's office welcomed the agreement but emphasized Israel retains "complete freedom to respond to any security threat."

**Lebanon's Response**: Lebanon's president cautiously welcomed the agreement but acknowledged Hezbollah's absence "creates uncertainty about implementation."

**Analysts**: Most Middle East experts believe the agreement is more symbolic than substantive — the real test will be whether effective implementation mechanisms can be established.
