## Core Summary

President Trump announced what he called the "most crushing economic operation ever" against Iran on August 20, threatening "tremendous economic consequences" for any country continuing to assist Tehran. The statement marks a dramatic escalation of the maximum pressure campaign, upgrading from unilateral sanctions to multilateral economic encirclement.

The move coordinates with the UAE's earlier decision to sever all trade with Iran, threatening to close off Tehran's last major economic corridor. The Wall Street Journal analysis notes the entire plan's success hinges on whether Dubai — Iran's most important re-export hub — can be cut off.

## Key Details

### Trump's "Economic Nuclear Option"

Trump used exceptionally strong language, describing the upcoming measures as the "most crushing economic operation" ever deployed. According to White House officials, new measures will include: full escalation of secondary sanctions on Iranian oil exports, cutting off evasion channels through third countries, and imposing penalties on foreign companies doing business with Iran.

Trump specifically emphasized that any country helping Iran evade sanctions will face "tremendous economic consequences," widely interpreted as targeting Iran's remaining key trade partners.

### UAE Trade Severance Ripple Effects

The UAE's decision to suspend all commercial dealings with Iran carries symbolic weight. Dubai has long served as Iran's primary transit point for evading Western sanctions, with Iranian companies conducting extensive re-export trade and financial flows through Dubai's free trade zones. The UAE's cooperation means Iran's most vital economic lifeline faces being severed.

### Allied Anxiety in South Korea

South Korean political circles are deeply concerned about the US-Iran escalation. South Korea maintains a 72-year alliance relationship with Iran and still has substantial frozen corporate funds there. The escalation may force Seoul to choose between alliance obligations and economic self-interest.

## Panoramic Analysis

This economic warfare escalation will have far-reaching impacts on global energy markets and geopolitical dynamics. First, if Iran's approximately 1.5 million barrels per day of oil exports are genuinely cut off, global crude supply will tighten by roughly 1.5%, potentially driving significant oil price volatility given current Middle East tensions. Second, America's threat of "collateral punishment" against third-country trading partners essentially weaponizes dollar hegemony to its extreme, accelerating de-dollarization — nations will have greater incentive to establish trade settlement mechanisms independent of the dollar system.

From a geopolitical perspective, Iran is not waiting passively. Reports indicate Tehran is turning to Russia for military and financial support, potentially forging a new "anti-sanctions axis." If Iran fully pivots toward the China-Russia economic sphere, US strategic influence in the Middle East will be further diluted. The ultimate outcome of this economic war may not be Iran's submission, but rather an accelerated bifurcation of the global trading system into two parallel architectures.

## Multiple Perspectives

**US Position**: The White House views economic strangulation as the best alternative to military conflict, using maximum pressure to force Iran back to negotiations.

**Iran Position**: Iranian officials call the sanctions "illegal and ineffective," claiming years of sanctions have been successfully weathered through trade diversification and pivoting to Eastern markets.

**European Allies**: The EU has expressed concern about unilateral escalation, worried it will destroy what remains of the 2015 JCPOA diplomatic framework.

**Gulf States**: The UAE's active cooperation shows Gulf Arab nations aligning with US-Israel positions against Iran, but also worry about potential Iranian retaliation.

**Asian Trade Partners**: Countries like South Korea and India with historical trade ties to Iran face a dilemma — complying with US sanctions harms their economic interests, while resisting risks secondary sanctions.
