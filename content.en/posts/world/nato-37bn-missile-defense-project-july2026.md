# NATO Announces £37 Billion Missile Defense Project in Major European Defense Integration Step

## Core Summary

NATO allies have announced a £37 billion joint missile defense project at a summit in Ankara, Turkey. UK Prime Minister Keir Starmer chaired the meeting of approximately a dozen leaders, marking a substantive phase in European defense integration. The project aims to develop and deploy next-generation air and missile defense systems covering threats from short-range rockets to medium-range ballistic missiles. It is being hailed as one of the largest defense investments in Europe since the Cold War.

## Event Details

According to BBC reporting, UK Prime Minister Starmer convened approximately a dozen leaders in Ankara to discuss and announce this ambitious missile defense initiative. The £37 billion investment will fund research, development, and deployment of a new generation of air and missile defense systems, providing layered protection against a range of threats.

The summit comes as European security faces multiple challenges. The ongoing conflict in Ukraine continues, with frequent Russian missile strikes on Ukrainian infrastructure and some missiles landing in NATO member states' airspace. Meanwhile, instability in the Middle East persists, with the Iran nuclear issue unresolved and risks of regional conflict spillover. These security threats have prompted NATO members to accelerate the integration of defense resources and enhance collective defense capabilities.

Starmer stated after the meeting that the project embodies the principle of "shared burden, shared benefit" among NATO members, significantly enhancing Europe's air defense capabilities. He emphasized that in the current security environment, NATO must maintain technological superiority and rapid response capabilities to address security challenges from all directions.

## Panoramic Perspective

The announcement of NATO's missile defense project marks a profound transformation in the European defense landscape. From a strategic perspective, the significance of this project extends far beyond mere military investment—it is reshaping transatlantic security architecture and Europe's strategic autonomy process.

First, the project reflects a major shift in NATO's perception of current security threats. Traditionally, NATO's defense focus has been on deterrence and collective defense, but recent missile threats from Russia, instability in the Middle East, and challenges from emerging technologies have forced NATO toward a more proactive and layered defense strategy. The £37 billion investment scale indicates that member states recognize existing systems are insufficient to address future threats, necessitating joint R&D and scaled deployment to reduce costs and improve effectiveness.

Second, the project has dual implications for Europe's strategic autonomy. On one hand, building a joint missile defense system will enhance Europe's independent defense capabilities and reduce reliance on the United States, aligning with France's long-advocated "European strategic autonomy" concept. On the other hand, the project remains within the NATO framework, emphasizing transatlantic cooperation, indicating that European countries value the alliance with the United States while pursuing strategic autonomy. This balance reflects Europe's strategic choices in an era of great power competition.

Third, from an industrial and economic perspective, the project will inject strong momentum into Europe's defense industry. Missile defense system R&D involves radar technology, interceptor missile manufacturing, command and control systems, and other high-tech fields, driving related industrial chain development and creating numerous high-skilled jobs. The joint R&D model also helps avoid duplication, improve capital efficiency, and promote integration and standardization of Europe's defense industry.

However, the project faces numerous challenges. Technically, building an all-European layered defense system requires solving complex issues of system compatibility, data sharing, and command coordination. Politically, disagreements may arise among countries regarding procurement shares, technical approaches, and benefit distribution. Financially, the £37 billion investment requires long-term defense budget commitments from member states, which is not easy amid current economic pressures.

## Multiple Perspectives

**Supportive View**: UK Prime Minister Starmer emphasized that the project demonstrates NATO's unity and adaptability, significantly enhancing European air defense capabilities as a necessary response to current security challenges. Poland and other Eastern European countries strongly support the project, believing it will strengthen deterrence against Russian threats.

**Cautious View**: Some analysts point out that the project faces significant risks of timeline and cost overruns. Historically, European joint defense projects have frequently experienced delays and budget overruns. Additionally, balancing national interests and ensuring fair technology sharing are challenges that must be addressed during implementation.

**Critical View**: Some peace organizations and left-wing parties criticize the plan as embodying "militarized thinking," arguing that security threats should be resolved through diplomatic means rather than increased military investment. They worry this will exacerbate confrontation with Russia and trigger a new arms race.

**US Position**: The United States welcomed the project, viewing it as demonstrating European allies' willingness to take on more defense responsibility, helping to alleviate the US burden. However, the US is also concerned about whether the project will weaken the market share of US defense industry in Europe.
