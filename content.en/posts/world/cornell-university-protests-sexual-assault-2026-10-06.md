**[Core Summary]**
Cornell University, one of America's most prominent Ivy League institutions, has been rocked by protests over its handling of sexual assault allegations. Activists spray-painted "Justice for Jane Doe" on Day Hall and broke windows; the university subsequently announced it would pause disciplinary actions against the seven male students previously sanctioned.

**[Event Details]**
- **On the Ground**: Roughly 200 students gathered outside Day Hall on the Cornell campus, spray-painting red and black graffiti on the building's facade and breaking several windowpanes; campus police did not directly intervene.
- **Core Demands**: Protesters are demanding that the school release all disciplinary documents related to the "gang-rape allegations" against the seven men and are questioning the independence of New York Attorney General Letitia James, who has been serving as special prosecutor in the case.
- **University Response**: Cornell President Martha Pollack issued a statement announcing a pause on the prior disciplinary actions against the students and the launch of an independent external review.
- **Legal Complication**: The U.S. Attorney's Office for the Southern District of New York has already filed federal criminal charges against six of the students, and the outcome of those proceedings may set a precedent for how U.S. universities handle campus sexual assault cases.

**[Panoramic Perspective]**
**First**, the scale of the protests shows that "how universities handle sexual assault" has become a defining governance issue on American campuses. Students are no longer satisfied with the traditional model of "internal adjudication" and are demanding greater transparency and external oversight — a continuation of the MeToo movement's extension into campus life.

**Second**, the case involves overlapping federal criminal prosecution and state-level special-prosecutor jurisdiction, highlighting how legally complex campus sexual-assault cases have become. When multiple levels of the justice system assert parallel jurisdiction over the same matter, procedural fairness and the rights of the accused become contested terrain.

**Third**, because the protest unfolded at an Ivy League institution, its symbolic weight extends far beyond the case itself. How Cornell ultimately balances procedural justice with victim protection will be studied as a template by other U.S. universities and is likely to shape the trajectory of campus sexual-assault governance for the next decade.

**Multiple Perspectives**
- **Cornell President Pollack**: Acknowledged that the campus justice system has room for improvement in handling assault allegations, and pledged that an external independent review will rebuild trust.
- **Student Protest Leaders**: Insisted "silence is no longer an option," demanding full disclosure of all disciplinary files and the removal of James from the special-prosecutor role.
- **Spokesperson for James's Office**: Rejected the "conflict of interest" allegations, saying her role as special prosecutor complies with New York State legal procedures.
- **U.S. Attorney's Office**: Stated that federal criminal proceedings will continue independently of the university's internal process and will not be altered by campus protests.
- **Association of American Universities (AAU)**: Urged member institutions to use "transparency plus independent review" as guiding principles for similar cases, avoiding the twin extremes of "internal cover-up" and "excessive punishment."
