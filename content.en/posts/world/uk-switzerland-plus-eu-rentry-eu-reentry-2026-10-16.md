**[Summary]**

A senior Member of the European Parliament said on October 16 that the United Kingdom could quickly re-enter the European Union through a "Switzerland Plus" arrangement, combining selective access to the EU single market, the customs union and key regulatory cooperation. The proposal lands as UK domestic debate over the economic cost of Brexit, trade frictions and regulatory divergence continues to intensify.

**[Event Details]**

- **Core of the proposal**: The "Switzerland Plus" framework is built on the existing Switzerland–EU relationship but adds deeper commitments in goods trade, services, persona mobility and mutual recognition, potentially covering financial services, REACH, agricultural inspections and energy market coupling.
- **EU position**: The MEP stressed that any such deal would require the UK to accept the three "Swiss formula" pillars — free movement of persons, jurisdiction of the European Court of Justice and contributions to the EU budget — and that the EU will not redesign its core architecture for "soft Brexit".
- **UK government reaction**: The Prime Minister's Office responded cautiously, saying the TCA is still the foundation of post-Brexit trade policy, while acknowledging growing industry demand for regulatory coordination in certain sectors.
- **Economic backdrop**: UK Treasury analysis shows UK–EU goods trade is roughly 15% below counterfactual projections since Brexit, with services cross-border activity shrinking in parallel; industry groups continue to push for a fresh assessment of the relationship.
- **Party lines**: Ruling-party backbenchers remain wary of closer EU ties; opposition voices, business associations and some senior parliamentarians argue for a full review of the post-Brexit roadmap.

**[Panoramic Perspective]**

The "Switzerland Plus" proposal reflects the mid-term recalibration pressure facing the UK trade architecture after Brexit. In the immediate post-referendum years, the UK chose the Trade and Cooperation Agreement to retain control over tariffs and quotas; five years on, border checks, duplicative regulatory certification and services market-access barriers have translated into a quantifiable economic cost. The sovereignty gained from "hard Brexit" has not delivered the expected boost to industrial competitiveness, and new friction costs in high-value-added sectors are gradually opening political space to revisit the relationship with the EU.

The deeper tension lies in the structural tug-of-war between UK sovereignty preferences and economic realities. Voters in 2016 and subsequent elections have consistently shown strong sensitivity on immigration control and judicial sovereignty, while financial services, professional services and high-end manufacturing lobby groups continue to press the government. Any "Switzerland Plus" arrangement will struggle to advance without triggering renewed political backlash. Expect a patchwork pattern — sector-by-sector breakthroughs in chemicals, energy and visas — rather than a single sweeping agreement.

From the EU side, France, the Netherlands and Nordic states remain firm on immigration and budget principles, while Poland, Hungary and other Central European members are wary of cherry-picking. Any "soft re-entry" by the UK is likely to be required to assume obligations comparable to Switzerland's, otherwise consensus will be hard to form inside the EU. Discussions may oscillate between launch and suspension for years before any substantive outcome emerges.
