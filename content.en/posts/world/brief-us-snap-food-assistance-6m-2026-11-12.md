## Key Facts

- **The proposal**: A reform of the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program put forward by the Trump administration.
- **The official estimate**: A USDA analysis finds more than six million people could lose benefits.
- **What the programme is**: SNAP is the largest federal food assistance programme in the United States, serving low-income households.
- **The stage**: The proposal remains at the drafting stage, and the figure comes from an administrative impact assessment.
- **Why it matters**: This is the first official quantification of the reach of the reform.

## Wider Perspective

The weight of the six-million figure needs two reference points. The first is the scale of the programme itself: SNAP is the largest federal food assistance programme in the country, covering tens of millions of low-income households, and after several years of rising food prices it functions not only as a benefit but as a pillar of the food budget for those households. The second is the direction of travel, which has not changed: this administration's moves on immigration and benefits have been consistent, and what is new is not the policy direction but the first official quantification of its reach.

The estimate matters because it pulls a political argument back to a checkable number. The dispute over whether eligibility thresholds should tighten, and whether work requirements should be attached, has run for years without resolution. With a USDA figure of six million, opponents no longer have to argue that many people are affected; they only have to ask who those six million are. Once that question is established, the character of the debate changes from an argument about principles to an argument about a list, and in an election cycle a list is far easier to rally around than a principle.

Fiscal and moral arguments converge here. Supporters will emphasise administrative savings and correcting eligibility mismatches. Opponents will point to the multiplier effect of food assistance spending: for recipient households the money goes overwhelmingly into basic groceries, and during a period of rising prices it directly determines how much they can actually consume. Neither claim is easy to falsify, so the near-term outcome will probably turn on implementation detail. Exemptions, transition arrangements and state-level enforcement standards are likely to decide how many of those six million actually lose assistance, more than the headline of the proposal will.
