## Core Summary

The Hawaiian Islands face their most severe weather threat in decades — Category 1 Hurricane Lala could become the first hurricane to directly hit Hawaii in 34 years. Weather officials warn that in a worst-case scenario, the hurricane could dump up to 25 inches (about 63cm) of rain on the Big Island, accompanied by destructive winds and massive waves. Hawaii's governor has declared a state of emergency, schools and businesses have closed early, and residents are urgently stockpiling supplies and preparing for the storm.

## Event Details

According to the BBC, Hurricane Lala is currently moving toward the Hawaiian Islands across the Pacific, with weather models showing its path could directly cross the main Hawaiian islands. This would be the first direct hurricane landfall in Hawaii since Hurricane Iniki in 1992.

The National Weather Service has issued hurricane watches and tropical storm warnings for multiple Hawaiian islands. Meteorological departments predict that Lala's sustained winds near the center are approximately 120 km/h, meeting Category 1 hurricane standards. However, the heavy rainfall and storm surge it brings could cause serious damage.

The Big Island and Maui are expected to be most severely affected. Preventive evacuations have begun in low-lying coastal areas, and some shelters have opened for evacuated residents. The Hawaii National Guard has been deployed to assist with evacuation and emergency preparations.

Local power companies are reinforcing critical transmission facilities to prevent widespread outages from strong winds. Water authorities have urged residents to store at least three days' worth of drinking water and household water. Supermarkets and gas stations have seen panic buying as residents stockpile food, water, and fuel.

## Panoramic Perspective

Hawaii facing a direct hurricane threat highlights the far-reaching impact of extreme weather events on Pacific Islands in the context of climate change. Although Hawaii is located in the central tropical Pacific, direct hurricane landfalls have been extremely rare due to the special characteristics of surrounding ocean temperature distribution and atmospheric circulation patterns. However, in recent years, continuously rising Pacific ocean temperatures have provided more favorable conditions for hurricane formation and intensification.

From an infrastructure perspective, Hawaii's building codes and disaster prevention systems are primarily designed for earthquakes and tsunamis, with relatively limited hurricane defense capabilities. Many older buildings may not withstand Category 1 hurricane winds, and power and communication networks face severe tests. The painful lessons from the 2023 Maui wildfires remain fresh, and the strong winds from this hurricane could further exacerbate fire risk.

From an economic analysis perspective, Hawaii is highly dependent on tourism, and a hurricane strike could lead to massive flight cancellations and tourist stranding, dealing a major blow to the local economy. The insurance industry will also face huge claims pressure, especially considering Hawaii's previous wildfire disasters — some insurance companies' willingness to underwrite may further decrease.

This event has also sparked broader discussion about Pacific Islands' climate adaptation capabilities. As ocean temperatures continue to rise, regions traditionally considered "safe" may face new weather threats, requiring disaster prevention planning and infrastructure construction to keep pace with the times.

## Multiple Perspectives

**Meteorological experts** point out that although Hurricane Lala is currently a Category 1 hurricane and not the highest intensity, there is uncertainty regarding its movement path and speed. If the storm intensifies or slows down as it approaches Hawaii, the impact could far exceed current predictions. They urge residents to prepare for the worst.

**Hawaii state government** emphasizes that emergency response mechanisms have been fully activated, and residents should strictly comply with evacuation orders, staying away from coastlines and low-lying areas. The governor stated that all available resources have been deployed and will do everything possible to ensure resident safety.

**Tourism industry workers** expressed deep concern. Massive bookings face cancellation, and hotels and airlines are developing emergency plans. Some tourists have begun leaving the islands early, creating passenger peaks at airports.

**Long-term residents** say that while Hawaii regularly experiences tropical storms and heavy rainfall, the threat of a direct hurricane landfall is unprecedented. Many still have vivid memories of the destruction caused by Hurricane Iniki in 1992, and this warning has revived the tension of that time.
