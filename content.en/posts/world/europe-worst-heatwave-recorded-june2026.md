## Core Summary

Researchers have confirmed that the heatwave currently sweeping across Europe is the worst ever recorded, according to The Washington Post. Temperatures have shattered historical records across multiple countries, with heat-related deaths continuing to rise. Scientists warn that as global warming intensifies, such extreme heat events will become far more frequent in coming decades.

## Event Details

The heatwave has persisted for days, affecting much of Western, Southern, and Central Europe. Weather stations in Spain, France, Italy, and Germany have all recorded record-breaking temperatures. Parts of Spain exceeded 45 degrees Celsius, while southern France surpassed 42 degrees in multiple locations.

Governments have activated emergency responses. Several Spanish cities issued the highest-level red heat alerts, closing schools and canceling outdoor events. France's health authorities launched their "Heat Health Plan," focusing on elderly and outdoor workers. Italian tourist cities including Rome and Florence issued warnings advising visitors to avoid going out during midday hours.

Preliminary figures indicate the heatwave has already caused hundreds of heat-related deaths across European countries, predominantly among elderly people and those with underlying conditions. Medical systems face enormous pressure with emergency room admissions surging.

Researchers note that the heatwave's intensity and duration have exceeded climate model expectations. World Meteorological Organization experts say climate change is making heatwaves more intense and prolonged, while human infrastructure and public health systems remain inadequately prepared.

## Panoramic Analysis

This European super-heatwave is not merely an extreme weather event — it stands as a vivid testament to the accelerating progression of global climate change. Scientists using "attribution analysis" methods have confirmed that without human-caused climate change, a heatwave of this intensity would be virtually impossible under natural conditions.

From a public health perspective, heatwaves are known as "silent killers." Unlike sudden disasters such as hurricanes or floods, heatwave impacts are gradual, yet their death toll often exceeds that of other natural disasters combined. The 2003 European heatwave killed approximately 70,000 people, and this event may match or exceed that figure. While Europe's public health systems have improved in heat warnings, existing "heat health plans" remain inadequate for increasingly frequent extreme temperatures.

Economically, the heatwave has comprehensively impacted European agriculture, energy, and transportation. Southern European crops are failing across wide areas due to heat and drought, river levels have dropped causing sharp reductions in hydropower generation, and railway tracks have been speed-restricted due to heat deformation. These cascading effects highlight European economic systems' vulnerability regarding climate resilience.

More profoundly, this heatwave may catalyze accelerated EU climate policy. Facing increasingly irreversible climate reality, European governments will face greater domestic pressure to adopt more aggressive action on carbon reduction targets, energy transition speed, and adaptation infrastructure.

## Multiple Perspectives

**Scientific Community**: Multiple climate scientists note this heatwave's intensity exceeded existing climate model predictions, suggesting climate change may be progressing faster than expected. The WMO has called for urgent enhancement of climate adaptation capabilities.

**Governments**: European Commission President announced an emergency summit to discuss response measures. France's President called for accelerating energy transition and reducing fossil fuel dependence.

**Public Health Experts**: WHO's European Regional Office recommends countries integrate heatwaves into national public health emergency systems and establish long-term heat health protection mechanisms.

**Public Response**: Across social media, citizens shared extreme heat living conditions. Air conditioning sales have surged, with some countries experiencing electricity supply strain.
