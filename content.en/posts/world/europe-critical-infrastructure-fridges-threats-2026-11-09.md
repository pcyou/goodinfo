## Key Facts

- **A coordinated refrigeration sweep**: On Monday, supermarkets in Belgium, Luxembourg, Sweden and the Netherlands found refrigeration systems set as high as 50 degrees Celsius, affecting retailers including Ahold Delhaize, IKEA, Colruyt Group and Carrefour and forcing them to discard spoiled food.
- **An earlier incident at a medical storage facility**: Refrigeration run by the French transfusion service was subsequently targeted in what appeared to be a similar incident, while an October cyberattack on hundreds of French supermarkets caused fires, overheated fridges and food to spoil.
- **Flights and newsrooms**: A wave of suspected hoax security threats disrupted dozens of flights across the continent, forcing diversions and, in some cases, fighter-jet escorts. Similar threats also targeted European news outlets, including the UK's Sky News and Irish public broadcaster RTE.
- **Attribution is unconfirmed**: Authorities have not established who was behind the attacks or whether they were connected. Russia denied any involvement, calling the accusations stupid and baseless.
- **The technical barrier is low**: Cybersecurity expert Fredrik Blix said the attacks required very little technical sophistication because vulnerable refrigeration control systems were connected directly to the public internet, with some devices protected only by default passwords or easily guessed credentials. Anyone can do it, and it does not require any resources from a country or anything like that, he said.
- **A wider backdrop**: Suspected sabotage and hybrid threats across Europe include damage to undersea cables in the Baltic Sea, death threats against European defence executives, and a suspected explosive device placed on a DHL aircraft in 2024. Weeks of nationwide protests, civil disturbances and strikes in France, unrest over living costs in Germany, Italy, Portugal and Albania, and an attempt by protesters to enter a Microsoft data centre in Amsterdam on Friday have widened the space in which adversaries can operate.
- **Divergent preparedness**: Finland and Sweden maintain well-developed civilian defence programmes reflecting their Cold War experience, while infrastructure further west was built around economic efficiency rather than defence needs. Along the Atlantic coast, preparation against hybrid attacks and against missile strikes of the kind seen in Ukraine is far less advanced, according to RUSI's Kaushal.
- **Responsibility is fragmented**: Blix noted that responsibility is split among product manufacturers, maintenance providers and grocery chains, with each pointing to the others. Maintenance companies sometimes prioritise convenience by enabling remote access despite security guidance, while retailers may fail to enforce adequate cybersecurity standards among suppliers.

## Competing Views

**The state-actor reading and the opportunist reading answer different questions.** Sidharth Kaushal, a research fellow at the Royal United Services Institute, said that if Russia were responsible, such attacks could be intended to demonstrate the costs of Europe's continued support for Ukraine. The goal, in his words, is cost imposition, convincing the European political leadership that the costs of a war can come home to them. The Kremlin's Dmitry Peskov denied involvement through emailed comments, calling the accusations stupid and baseless. Blix, while allowing that a state or state-backed actor was possible, judged the more likely scenario to be someone testing the boundaries and having some fun. None of these accounts contradicts another. What was done is established; what it was for is not, and the gap between those two statements is exactly where policy has to work.

**The most consequential reframing came from the analyst who refused the peace-and-war binary.** Oana Popescu-Zamfir, a senior RUSI fellow specialising in European security, said the incidents showed Europe had lagged in preparing for something it already knew would be coming. Europe still tends to conceptually divide between peace and wartime, she said, and that is no longer the case because the continent is not at war but is also not at peace time. The observation is analytically uncomfortable because it moves the question away from who did this and toward the standard against which Europe is preparing, which is a question the existing security vocabulary and the budget lines built around it are not well equipped to answer.

**Attribution risk cuts in both directions.** Alexander Anderson, president and director of policy at the Centre for International Security and Economic Strategy, cautioned against dismissing legitimate domestic grievances as the product of foreign interference, because doing so risks misunderstanding both the causes of political instability and the methods hostile actors use to exploit it. Weeks of protests, riots and strikes have increased pressure on the French government, and in Spain a housing-related parliamentary impasse has pushed Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez to call a snap election for November 29, with the far-right Vox party expected to gain. Russian and potentially Chinese influence operations may work to amplify such divisions, but treating every grievance as an externally caused plot would produce its own misreading of what is actually driving European politics.

**The technical assessment is the least flattering to Europe's defenders.** Blix said the supermarket attacks required almost no specialised knowledge because vulnerable refrigeration controllers were exposed directly to the public internet, and warned that the publicity could encourage copycat attacks. His broader criticism was of the response rather than the attacks: what worries me, he said, is that we don't really do anything, adding that we are shuffling a lot of papers and doing a lot of risk assessments while failing to take meaningful action. That is the practical summary of a supply chain in which each party has a contractual reason to point at the next.

## Panoramic Analysis

The choice of target is the story. Refrigeration is neither a military installation nor a symbolic monument. It is a mundane control system with a hard physical deadline attached to it. Driving a cold chain to 50 degrees does not destroy the building and does not kill anyone; it destroys perishable goods, empties shelves, and produces a visible, local, photographable absence. That is precisely the profile of a low-capability denial-of-service action against everyday life: minimal technical requirement, maximal visibility, and no attributable actor. The medical-storage refrigeration case raises the stakes considerably, because a public health stock carries a different expiry curve from a supermarket chiller, and the difference between spoilt milk and unusable therapeutic stock is the difference between an inconvenience and a failure of the health system.

The second thing worth understanding is that this class of threat was always available and cheap, and the reason it is now visible is not that it became more sophisticated but that it became more attractive. What changed is the ambient environment: a decade of hybrid activity, a European security debate shaped by undersea cable damage and death threats against defence executives, and a wave of domestic unrest across several member states. A capability with no attributable signature is far cheaper to deploy in such an environment because the response is expensive and the attribution is unproven. In that sense the fridge attacks are not a new vulnerability so much as a new application of an old one, applied at the moment the political cost of using it fell.

The third is the accountability gap, and it is the part most amenable to being fixed. Security responsibility for industrial control systems is distributed across a long chain: the manufacturer that ships a device, the maintenance firm that keeps remote access alive because it is convenient, and the retailer that contracts both without auditing either. Each link can point at the next. This is not primarily a moral failure, it is a contractual one, and the cost of that diffusion lands on the least powerful actor in the chain, the store that discards its stock and answers to a customer. Until a purchaser of refrigeration controllers can demand the state of their network exposure the way one demands a fire certificate, the cheapest defence remains a document nobody verifies.

The most useful observable indicators are unusually concrete. Whether any member state introduces an enforceable requirement on the network exposure of industrial control systems rather than another advisory risk assessment. Whether insurers begin pricing this exposure, since insurance is where unpriced risk usually surfaces first. Whether copycat attempts cluster in the weeks following media coverage, which Blix explicitly flagged as a possibility. And whether a public, quantified inventory of critical infrastructure resilience becomes something a government can be held to rather than a classified annex. On the diplomatic side, the more telling signal would be whether European governments treat the retail and logistics sector as a resilience domain with the same seriousness they now give undersea cables.
