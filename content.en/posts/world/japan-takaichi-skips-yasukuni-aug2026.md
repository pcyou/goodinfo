## Core Summary

Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi chose not to visit Yasukuni Shrine on August 15, the anniversary of Japan's surrender in World War II. The decision broke expectations that she might visit, and is widely interpreted as a cautious posture by the Japanese government amid diplomatic tensions in East Asia. Yasukuni Shrine honors 14 Class-A war criminals among the war dead, and has long been a flashpoint for diplomatic friction between Japan and countries including China and South Korea.

## Event Details

According to the Associated Press, Takaichi did not visit Yasukuni Shrine on August 15, instead choosing other forms of commemoration. This decision contrasts with the approach of previous Japanese prime ministers, several of whom visited the shrine in either private or official capacity, each time triggering strong protests from China and South Korea.

Since taking office, Takaichi has maintained a relatively cautious stance on historical issues. During her campaign, she stated she would "commemorate war dead in an appropriate manner" but did not explicitly promise to visit Yasukuni. This latest decision is seen as a balancing act under multiple diplomatic pressures.

The current East Asian geopolitical landscape is at a delicate juncture. China-US relations remain tense, the Taiwan Strait situation is sensitive, and the Korean Peninsula nuclear issue remains unresolved. Against this backdrop, a prime ministerial visit to Yasukuni would likely trigger a serious diplomatic crisis with China and South Korea, potentially affecting trilateral trade cooperation and security dialogue.

Japan's Chief Cabinet Secretary stated at a regular press conference that the prime minister "made this decision after comprehensively considering various domestic and international factors," without specifying which factors were considered. Neither the South Korean presidential office nor Japan's Foreign Ministry issued formal comments on Takaichi's decision, but diplomatic observers generally believe both sides hold a "cautiously welcome" attitude.

## Panoramic Perspective

Takaichi's decision to skip the Yasukuni visit reflects Japan's long-standing dilemma between historical perception and real-world diplomacy. The Yasukuni issue is not merely a domestic political matter for Japan, but a structural contradiction in East Asian international relations.

From the perspective of Japanese domestic politics, conservative voters and bereaved families have long viewed Yasukuni visits as a patriotic duty, and any prime minister's absence would be interpreted as weakness on historical issues. However, Japan's deep economic dependence on Chinese and South Korean markets means that deteriorating diplomatic relations would directly impact Japanese business interests. In recent years, trilateral trade among China, Japan, and South Korea has exceeded 800 billion dollars, and any diplomatic friction could disrupt supply chain and industrial cooperation.

From a regional security perspective, Takaichi's decision also creates space for future summit diplomacy. If China-Japan and Japan-South Korea relations can be eased by this gesture, it could lay the foundation for trilateral coordination on issues such as Korean Peninsula denuclearization and regional economic cooperation. However, resolving historical issues cannot happen overnight, and public dissatisfaction in China and South Korea regarding Japan's historical perception persists.

Notably, this decision has also triggered different reactions within Japan. Some conservative lawmakers criticized Takaichi for bowing to diplomatic pressure, arguing the prime minister should have the right to visit Yasukuni based on her own judgment. Meanwhile, moderates and the business community generally supported the decision, believing that maintaining stable relations with neighbors is more important given the current international situation.

## Multiple Perspectives

**Japanese Conservative Position**: Some conservative LDP lawmakers expressed disappointment, believing the prime minister should fulfill her responsibility to commemorate war dead uninfluenced by external pressure. The Japan War-Bereaved Families Association has also shown internal divisions, with some members expecting a visit while others understand the diplomatic considerations.

**Chinese and South Korean Official Reactions**: China's Foreign Ministry spokesperson said they noted the relevant reports and called on Japan to approach historical issues with a responsible attitude. South Korea's Foreign Ministry did not issue a formal statement, but sources revealed the South Korean side holds a positive attitude toward Takaichi's decision.

**Japanese Business and Moderates**: The Japan Business Federation (Keidanren) issued a statement emphasizing that stable East Asian relations are crucial for Japan's economy. Multiple Japanese business leaders privately expressed that Takaichi's decision helps ease recent tensions in China-Japan relations.

**International Observers**: Several East Asian studies scholars pointed out that while Takaichi's decision carries symbolic significance, substantively resolving historical issues still requires Japan to take more proactive stances on specific matters such as textbooks, comfort women, and forced labor. Merely avoiding shrine visits is insufficient to fundamentally improve relations with neighbors.
