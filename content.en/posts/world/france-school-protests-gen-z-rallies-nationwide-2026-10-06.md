**[Core Summary]**
A wave of student protests has swept across France, spreading to more than 40 cities and drawing over 250,000 participants. Police deployed tear gas and water cannons in Paris, Lyon and Marseille, exposing deep structural tensions over resource distribution, policing and Gen Z political demands. The Élysée Palace announced a cross-ministerial mechanism, and UNESCO has urged dialogue to ease tensions.

**[Event Details]**
- **Scale**: Over 250,000 students and young people participated, with protests concentrated in Paris, Lyon, Marseille, Toulouse, Bordeaux and other university-intensive cities.
- **Core demands**: Increased education funding, improved campus infrastructure, expanded scholarships, relief from student housing shortages and a reassessment of tuition reform plans.
- **Police response**: Riot police fired tear gas and deployed water cannons. Clashes, damage to storefronts and transit infrastructure were reported, with several central Paris metro stations temporarily closed.
- **Government response**: The Élysée Palace issued a statement announcing a "cross-ministerial education emergency mechanism", calling for restraint. The Education Minister pledged a campus safety upgrade plan within two weeks.
- **International reaction**: UNESCO issued a statement calling on all sides to resolve differences through dialogue. The European Commission is monitoring the situation and stressed that member state education sovereignty must be respected.

**[Panoramic Perspective]**
**First** This is the largest youth mobilization in France in years and reflects the acceleration of Gen Z's political engagement. The new generation is no longer confined to traditional party politics; instead, street mobilization, social media organizing and short-video dissemination form a new mode of political expression. This contrasts sharply with traditional protests led by unions and student federations, posing new governance challenges for European governments.

**Second** The protests have fully exposed structural problems in France's education resource allocation. Although France's education outlay as a share of GDP ranks middle-to-upper in the EU, regional gaps, underinvestment in campus infrastructure and chronic student housing shortages have been ignored for years. When economic pressure and rising youth unemployment intersect, these "latent contradictions" erupt as large-scale social movements.

**Third** The events will have a ripple effect on European politics. France is a core EU member state, and its governance difficulties will spark attention from other member states, particularly in Southern Europe, to issues of educational equity and youth job guarantees. Political discourse in European Parliament elections and local elections will be recalibrated.

**Fourth** Over the medium to long term, the protests will become an important catalyst for French education policy and policing reform. How the government balances the right to protest, the maintenance of public order and the response to youth demands will test the Macron administration's governing capacity for the rest of its term.
