## Core Summary

According to The New York Times and Al Jazeera, Japan has formally established its first national intelligence agency since the end of World War II, with a budget of $407 million and a personnel strength of 33,000. This historic move marks a major turning point in Japan's security policy, aimed at addressing increasingly complex regional security threats and ending Japan's longstanding embarrassment as what the intelligence community has called a "spy paradise."

## Event Details

According to multiple international media reports, Japan's newly established intelligence agency will integrate scattered intelligence resources across government departments to build a unified intelligence collection, analysis, and operations system. The agency's annual budget stands at $407 million with 33,000 personnel, making it one of the most significant intelligence forces in the Asia-Pacific region.

The New York Times reports that the Japanese government has invited Western allied intelligence experts to provide technical support and training to help the new agency rapidly establish modern intelligence capabilities. This cooperative arrangement demonstrates that Japan's intelligence reform has received active support from US and European partners.

Japan's Prime Minister stated in a Diet speech that the new agency's establishment is a "necessary measure for maintaining national security," emphasizing that Japan's security environment has undergone fundamental changes. He noted that North Korea's missile threats, China's military expansion, and Russia's regional activities all require Japan to possess stronger intelligence early-warning and response capabilities.

## Panoramic Analysis

Japan's establishment of a national intelligence agency represents an important milestone in the evolution of post-war Japanese security policy. The deeper backdrop is the profound transformation of East Asian geopolitical dynamics and Japan's repositioning of its security role.

From a historical perspective, Japan was stripped of the right to establish formal intelligence agencies after WWII as part of Allied occupation demilitarization policies. However, the Cold War's emergence and the Korean War forced the US to acquiesce to Japan building limited intelligence capabilities. Yet intelligence work has long been scattered across the Foreign Ministry, Defense Agency, and police systems without unified coordination, leading to multiple major security early-warning failures.

From a regional security perspective, the new agency will significantly enhance Japan's intelligence collection and analysis capabilities. Facing North Korean nuclear-missile threats and Chinese military modernization, Japan needs more timely and accurate intelligence support. Additionally, as Japan plays a more active regional security role—including through the Quad security dialogue with the US, Australia, and India—a unified intelligence agency will become an important tool for Japan's participation in multilateral security cooperation.

From an institutional design perspective, how Japan builds strong intelligence capabilities while ensuring democratic oversight and civil liberty protection will be an important test. Western allies' experience shows that intelligence agency powers must be subject to strict legal constraints and parliamentary oversight, otherwise they risk repeating abuses of power.

## Multiple Perspectives

The Japanese government emphasizes that the new agency's functions are strictly limited to national security domains and will not infringe on citizen rights. The Prime Minister's office stated that independent oversight mechanisms will be established to ensure intelligence activities' legality and transparency.

The US and some European nations have welcomed Japan's move, believing it will strengthen intelligence sharing and cooperation among allies. A US Defense Department spokesperson stated that a Japan with stronger intelligence capabilities "serves the security interests of the Indo-Pacific region."

However, Japanese pacifists and some opposition parties have expressed concerns about the new agency. They believe it could become a first step toward Japan becoming a "military power" and may provoke vigilance and backlash from neighboring countries. China's Foreign Ministry has stated it is "closely watching" and called on Japan to "handle security policy adjustments prudently."

Historians note that Japan's intelligence agency establishment represents not just security policy adjustment but a deeper shift in national identity. Over seventy years post-war, Japan has sought balance between pacifism and realistic security needs—and the new agency marks this balance tilting toward the latter.
