## US Justice Department Defends White House Media Ban, Urges Court to Reject Lawsuit

**[Core Summary]** On September 23, 2026, the US Department of Justice formally defended the White House's policy of barring several major news organizations from press access, asking a federal judge to dismiss a lawsuit filed by those outlets. The DOJ argues that the president retains broad authority to determine which journalists receive White House credentials, particularly when national security concerns are invoked. The legal dispute marks the most significant confrontation of this administration between executive power and the constitutional protections of a free press.

The DOJ brief, filed in the US District Court for the District of Columbia, asserts that the issuance and revocation of White House press passes falls within the president's constitutional authority over the operations of the executive branch. Government lawyers argue that news organizations do not have a legally enforceable right to access areas controlled by the White House, especially spaces where sensitive national security discussions may occur.

The plaintiffs include CNN, MS NOW (formerly MSNBC), and Politico. Their joint lawsuit alleges that the credentialing decisions were made in retaliation for unfavorable coverage and therefore violate the First Amendment's protections for speech and press freedom. The outlets seek reinstatement of their credentials and a court order preventing future arbitrary exclusions.

White House officials have stated separately that the credentialing decisions were based on operational and security assessments rather than on the content of any specific reporting. Press officials emphasized that the White House continues to maintain an active press pool and has not imposed a blanket ban on journalism, but rather adjusted the composition of access lists.

**[Panorama]** Beyond the immediate legal questions, this case touches a structural fault line in American constitutional doctrine: how courts should weigh executive branch assertions of national security against long-standing First Amendment protections. Historically, the Supreme Court has applied heightened scrutiny to laws that restrict speech or press activity, requiring the government to demonstrate a compelling interest and to use the least restrictive means to achieve it. However, the specific question of whether the president may condition physical access to the executive compound on security criteria has not been definitively resolved, leaving this case to potentially set new precedent.

The dispute also reflects the broader deterioration of trust between political institutions and the press over the past decade. Polling consistently shows declining confidence in mainstream media across partisan lines, while political leaders across the spectrum have adopted increasingly adversarial stances toward critical coverage. Against this backdrop, the use of credentialing decisions as a tool of press management has become a flashpoint that resonates far beyond Washington.

Internationally, the case is being watched closely by journalists and legal advocates who note that governments in other democracies frequently cite US executive practices when justifying their own restrictions on foreign or critical media. The downstream impact of this litigation could therefore influence the global climate for independent reporting for years to come.

**[Multi-perspective Comparison]** Supporters of the White House position, including several administrative law scholars and conservative commentators, argue that the president must retain discretion over who enters the working spaces of the executive branch. They contend that any external standard imposed by courts would impair the president's ability to safeguard classified discussions and protect staff from potential security threats.

Critics, led by press freedom advocates, civil liberties organizations, and First Amendment scholars, counter that even when security justifications are invoked, decisions about press access risk becoming a form of viewpoint-based retaliation when not subject to independent review. They argue that the appearance of selective enforcement undermines public confidence in government communication and erodes the informational foundation on which democratic accountability depends.

Professional journalism organizations, including the White House Correspondents' Association and the National Press Photographers Association, have issued statements calling for any restrictions to be applied through transparent, non-discriminatory procedures. The American Civil Liberties Union has indicated it may file an amicus brief supporting the media plaintiffs.
