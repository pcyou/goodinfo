## Core Summary

Reuters reported on September 13 that voting in Sweden's parliamentary election concluded in the evening, with initial counts showing a sharp rise for the far-right Sweden Democrats and the party poised to enter a governing coalition led by the Moderates for the first time. Several major European outlets described the vote as an important signal of a further rightward shift in the European political map, with the outcome likely to have far-reaching implications for immigration policy, EU relations, energy transition and public safety. Preliminary tallies showed the centre-right bloc holding a relative majority of the 349 seats in the Riksdag, but internal disagreements remain significant and coalition negotiations are expected to last several weeks.

## Event Details

**Voting and preliminary counts**. Voting in Sweden's parliamentary election began at 8 a.m. local time and closed at 8 p.m. on September 13, with around seven million registered voters taking part. The turnout reached a multi-year high. Preliminary counts showed the centre-right bloc, made up of the Moderates, the Christian Democrats and the Liberals, on around 49 percent of the vote, while the centre-left grouping of the Social Democrats, the Green Party, the Left Party and the Centre Party stood at around 48 percent, a margin tighter than most pre-election polls had predicted.

**A historic breakthrough for the Sweden Democrats**. The far-right Sweden Democrats are projected to win the second-largest party share in the Riksdag in this election, with their vote share rising significantly above any previous result. Founded in the 1990s, the party spent decades on the political margins but has over the past ten years used its hard line on immigration, crime and Nordic identity to move steadily into the mainstream. After the Moderates' leader publicly signalled a willingness to work with the party, the Sweden Democrats now have a realistic path into government for the first time.

**The Moderates' strategic pivot**. The Moderates stated clearly during the campaign that they wished to invite the Sweden Democrats to form a government together after a victory and to take concrete ministerial portfolios. That statement broke the long-standing two-party consensus in Swedish politics that had treated the Sweden Democrats as a party with whom cooperation was unacceptable. The Moderates' leader argued repeatedly in the final phase of the campaign that Sweden faces structural challenges in migrant integration, public safety and social trust that demand a policy response that crosses traditional bloc lines.

**Key variables in coalition negotiations**. After the preliminary result, several political analysts noted that the shape of the new government is still uncertain. Possible scenarios include a four-party coalition, a Moderates-led minority government supported from outside, or an arrangement in which portfolios are distributed within the existing bloc without formally bringing the Sweden Democrats into the cabinet. A number of Moderates' own MPs continue to have reservations about formally inviting the far right into government, and those divisions are likely to surface repeatedly during the coming weeks of negotiations.

**Significant international attention**. Politicians and media in France, Germany, Italy and the Netherlands followed the Swedish vote closely. Multiple commentaries noted that Sweden has long been regarded as a model of Nordic social democracy, giving its political trajectory an outsized indicator value for the broader European political climate. Senior officials from the European Commission, the European Parliament and the Nordic Council issued separate statements after the preliminary results, emphasising that they will continue to work with the new Swedish government while watching its positions on EU policy, migration quotas and defence spending.

## Wider Perspective

Sweden's preliminary election result is not an isolated national political event. It is a concentrated expression of the further collapse of the long-held "cordon sanitaire" consensus among mainstream European parties, and its impact will reach well beyond the Scandinavian peninsula, with ripple effects across the European political climate, EU internal policy coordination, migration governance models and transatlantic relations.

**The long-standing cordon-sanitaire strategy of mainstream European parties is systematically failing**. In Germany, Finland, the Netherlands, Italy and Portugal, mainstream centre-right parties have already broken, one by one, the political taboo against cooperating with far-right parties. Sweden has long been regarded as one of the last bastions of the Nordic social-democratic tradition. The Moderates' public readiness to invite the Sweden Democrats into government means that the once-rigid boundary between mainstream European parties and far-right movements is moving from local softening toward an overall collapse. This shift will set an example for coalition-building, agenda-setting and party realignment across Europe in the years ahead.

**Immigration and public safety are replacing traditional economic issues as the central axis of European electoral politics**. In this Swedish election, the Sweden Democrats concentrated the campaign tightly on migrant integration, violent crime and public safety, drawing large numbers of centrist voters who had previously backed the Moderates or the Social Democrats. Multiple post-election analyses argue that the core concerns of European voters have moved beyond jobs and welfare alone to include urban security, cross-border crime, cultural identity and the sense of safety in public spaces. This structural change in the issue agenda means traditional left-wing parties must reshape their policy narrative or they will continue to be squeezed at the ballot box across Europe.

**The EU's internal capacity for unified action is likely to weaken further**. Sweden is a founding member of the European Union and has long played a constructive role on climate policy, foreign aid and military neutrality. If the new Swedish government takes a more nationalist line on Russia sanctions, migration quotas, defence spending and the EU budget, it could deepen internal divisions on several key EU dossiers. Several MEPs have warned that when the EU takes critical decisions on migration, defence and energy transition, it will increasingly have to face a more dispersed, more diverse and more negotiating-prone membership.

**The Nordic welfare-state model is under structural pressure to make policy choices again**. Sweden has long been defined by high taxes, generous welfare provision and highly accessible public services. The Sweden Democrats made clear during the campaign that they oppose parts of the existing welfare framework and want public resources redirected toward retirement security, native families and public safety. If the party ends up in government, structural adjustments in welfare policy, the tax mix, housing provision and migrant social integration could follow. Those adjustments will not only shape Sweden's domestic political balance, but will also have spillover effects on policy directions in Denmark, Norway and Finland.

**NATO and defence questions in the transatlantic relationship will be a high-priority test for the new government**. Sweden has recently become a full NATO member and has lifted defence spending above two percent of gross domestic product. Under the new political setup, the government's stance on transatlantic relations, aid to Ukraine, Arctic security and tariff negotiations with Washington is likely to differ noticeably from previous centre-left or centre-right governments. Several analysts have noted that the new government's priorities are likely to lean further toward national interests, NATO burden-sharing and transatlantic military cooperation, a pattern that partly echoes the current political trajectory in the United States.

## Comparison of Multiple Viewpoints

**The Moderates and the broader centre-right bloc** tend to read the result as a sign of political maturity in Sweden, arguing that cross-bloc cooperation is a pragmatic response to voter concerns and that the new government will deliver a stable majority on the rule of law, migrant integration, economic growth and defence.

**The Sweden Democrats**, after the preliminary result, stressed that their core agenda will focus on tightening immigration policy, strengthening policing, restricting labour inflows from outside the European Union and steering cultural policy in public institutions back toward a native-Swedish identity, and said they are willing to take concrete ministerial portfolios to deliver on that agenda.

**The Social Democrats and the Green Party** criticised the Moderates for unilaterally dismantling the cordon sanitaire, warning that the choice will push Sweden into a period of instability and widen social divisions. Several opposition MPs warned that the new government may visibly retreat on EU policy, climate policy and the welfare framework.

**The EU and Sweden's Nordic neighbours** have reacted with caution. Senior EU officials said they will continue to work with Sweden, but stressed that the EU depends heavily on member-state positions in the migration, defence and energy dossiers, and that the new government's specific policy line will become clearer in ministerial meetings and multilateral consultations over the coming months.

**Swedish business and labour** are watching closely for the impact of coalition talks on labour law, tax policy and energy prices. Industry statements emphasised the hope that the new government will preserve Sweden's economic competitiveness while keeping the core elements of the Nordic welfare model and avoiding shocks to foreign investment and industrial policy.

**International media and research institutes** widely read the result as part of a continuing rightward shift in the European political map, and noted that political trajectories in Denmark, Norway and Finland over the coming years are likely to be influenced by Sweden's coalition negotiations and by the actual policies the new government puts in place.
