## Key Facts

- President Donald Trump said on Tuesday that his administration is terminating a Biden-era rule aimed at cutting methane emissions from oil and gas drilling.
- Speaking at a campaign rally in Lubbock, Texas, he said the change would save companies and consumers billions of dollars and unlock 210 million barrels of oil, equivalent to about ten days of United States petroleum consumption.
- Trump said the rule crushed small operators with crippling regulations, and said he was officially ending those expensive and unnecessary regulations to save 45 billion dollars in costs.
- The move comes as the administration faces criticism over high fuel prices amid the war with Iran.
- Methane is responsible for about a third of planet-warming greenhouse gas emissions, according to scientists, and oil and gas operations are the largest industrial source.
- Methane is far more potent than carbon dioxide in the short term, and the invisible gas escapes from equipment or is burned off as a waste product.
- Environmental Protection Agency administrator Lee Zeldin previewed the changes last month, saying they would eliminate the super emitter program requiring companies to investigate and repair major releases, and would set less restrictive standards for small oil and gas wells.
- The wider move fits a pattern of dismantling climate policies enacted under the previous administration.

## Competing Views

The supporting argument rests on cost and supply. Compliance costs have squeezed the viability of marginal production, and removing the rule is said to release supply at a time when prices are high, with the cost passed to consumers. The figure announced at the rally is part of that argument rather than an incidental figure attached to it.

The opposing argument is about the nature of the gas. Methane is a short-lived but potent pollutant, and because it does not accumulate for centuries the way carbon dioxide does, curbing it slows the rate of warming within the current decade. Removing the rule therefore adds a measurable increment to the near-term rate of warming, and that increment cannot be recovered later by cutting carbon dioxide instead.

A third reading from energy market observers concerns the asymmetry of the rule itself. Where field equipment is old, marginal abatement is generally easier and cheaper than a wholesale replacement of plant, so the practical effect on total emissions may be smaller than the political dispute implies. What this group watches instead is regulatory uncertainty itself, because companies defer investment until the direction of the rules settles.

## Panoramic Analysis

The real weight of this decision is not the tonnage it changes. It is that it unsettles one of the few places where global climate governance still had consensus. Progress on carbon has been slow and the major emitters disagree sharply about long-term targets, but methane was different: it acts quickly, costs relatively little to control, and connects directly to industrial safety, so almost every major economy was moving on it. Unilaterally withdrawing a measure of that kind sends a signal far wider than its coverage.

The second layer is the time scale. Methane stays in the atmosphere for little more than a decade, which is fundamentally unlike carbon dioxide, whose effect accumulates over centuries. That means the acceleration in warming caused by dropping the rule is not a diffuse cost deferred to the next century but a real increment that will show up in observations within the next five to ten years. Read the other way round, that also makes methane one of the few levers where unilateral action produces immediate results, which is exactly why a unilateral withdrawal is conspicuous.

The third layer is the lag in industrial investment. Oil and gas producers plan equipment purchases on a multi-year horizon, and an uncertain rule direction causes decisions to be deferred. The administrator previewed the change last month and the president announced it at a rally, which means companies have already been waiting for a signal. If the final text allows a long transition, the damage is contained. If the transition is compressed, the industry faces a costly and largely passive equipment replacement, and that cost eventually returns to consumers and to state budgets, at which point the question of who pays stops being purely an environmental one.
