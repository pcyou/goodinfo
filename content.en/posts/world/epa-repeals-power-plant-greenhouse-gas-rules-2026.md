## [Brief] EPA Formally Repeals Greenhouse Gas Emission Limits for Power Plants, Sparking Sharp Climate-Policy Debate

US media reported on September 14 that the Environmental Protection Agency has formally repealed its greenhouse gas emission limits for power plants. The rule had been one of the most substantive climate-policy levers in the US power sector, covering coal, gas and certain steam-generating facilities, and had been a primary federal driver of emission reductions in the US electricity industry over the past decade. Its repeal means that, for several years to come, the US electricity industry will no longer face mandatory federal emission constraints.

Federal and state reactions have diverged sharply. Several Democratic governors said in statements that they will continue to push for power-sector decarbonisation through state climate and clean-energy legislation, and may launch new multi-state coordination mechanisms. Several Republican governors welcomed the federal repeal, arguing that it will lower electricity costs, improve energy security and reduce the regulatory uncertainty that weighs on US manufacturing.

The energy industry is also split. Clean-energy trade associations warned that the repeal will weaken investment incentives for wind, solar, storage, nuclear power and grid upgrades, putting the United States at a disadvantage in the international race for clean-energy technology. Fossil-fuel industry groups called the repeal an important step back toward "common sense and the rule of law" that will support the stability and affordability of the US power system.

Several environmental organisations have already announced plans to file lawsuits. Multiple law firms and NGOs have said they will challenge the repeal on grounds including whether the procedure was lawful, whether adequate environmental review was carried out and whether the move is compatible with the existing Clean Air Act. These cases are expected to proceed through the federal appellate courts, adding a new layer of uncertainty to the rule's practical implementation.

## Full Perspective

The EPA's repeal of greenhouse gas emission limits for power plants is one of the largest rollbacks of US climate regulation in more than a decade, with deep implications for the US energy transition, federal-state policy coordination and the US position in international climate negotiations.

**A major shift in investment signals for the power sector.** The repeal means that US coal and gas plants will not face mandatory federal emission constraints for several years, and capital allocation by utilities will tilt toward short-term returns rather than long-cycle decarbonisation. The impact on wind and solar projects that already have long-term power purchase agreements will be limited, but the move is expected to put significant pressure on planned clean-energy capacity, storage deployments and grid upgrades, and may delay or redirect some projects.

**New challenges for federal-state climate policy coordination.** In the absence of federal mandates, California, New York, Washington and other Democratic-led states will continue to advance their own clean-energy and power-sector legislation, and could form new interstate coalitions. But the federal-state divide will leave multinational companies facing a highly fragmented compliance landscape across different states, raising their operating costs and strategic uncertainty.

**Widening divisions in international climate negotiations.** As the world's second-largest greenhouse gas emitter, a major shift in US power-sector policy will directly affect the upcoming round of international climate talks. Climate negotiators from Europe and Asia have already expressed concern that the federal-level US rollback could slow global decarbonisation and weaken the political will of developed countries to provide climate finance and technology transfers to developing countries.

## Multi-perspective Comparison

The **EPA** argues that the repeal is an important return to the rule of law and common sense, and that the new regulatory framework will be built around "common sense, transparency and energy security", avoiding mandatory constraints on the power industry that go beyond statutory authorisation. **Democratic governors and lawmakers** warn that the repeal will weaken the long-term competitiveness of the US power sector and have pledged to defend the decarbonisation agenda through state legislation and the courts. **Clean-energy industry groups** say the move will weaken investment incentives for wind, solar, storage, nuclear and grid upgrades and put the United States at a disadvantage internationally. **Fossil-fuel industry groups** say the repeal will support the stability and affordability of the US power system. **Environmental organisations and law firms** plan lawsuits challenging whether the repeal was lawful and consistent with the Clean Air Act.
