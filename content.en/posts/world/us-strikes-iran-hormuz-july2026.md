## Executive Summary

The United States military has launched strikes against targets inside Iran in response to an attack on a civilian vessel in the Strait of Hormuz. The escalation marks a significant deterioration in US-Iran relations and threatens global energy security as Iran announces closure of the critical waterway.

## Event Details

According to the Associated Press, the US military announced on July 12 that it has conducted strikes against multiple military targets inside Iran, responding to Iran's earlier attack on a Cyprus-flagged civilian vessel in the Strait of Hormuz. US Central Command stated the operation aimed to "protect international shipping" and "maintain regional stability."

BBC reports that Iran has announced the closure of the Strait of Hormuz in retaliation. Approximately 20% of the world's oil passes through this strategic waterway, and its closure would have severe implications for global energy markets.

The immediate trigger for the military conflict was Iran's attack on a civilian vessel. US officials stated the ship was traveling normally through the Strait of Hormuz when it was attacked by Iranian forces, resulting in crew casualties. The US emphasized the military strikes were a "necessary and proportionate act of self-defense."

Iran's Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei had previously called for revenge for his father's death, vowing tough action against the United States and Israel. Analysts suggest the strait attack may have been part of Iran's new leadership's effort to demonstrate a hardline stance.

## Strategic Analysis

The US military strikes on Iran represent a major turning point in Middle Eastern geopolitics. The far-reaching consequences extend well beyond bilateral relations, systematically impacting global energy security, international shipping order, and regional power balance.

From an energy security perspective, the Strait of Hormuz holds an irreplaceable strategic position. Approximately 21 million barrels of crude oil pass through this narrow waterway daily, accounting for one-third of global seaborne oil trade. Iran's announcement of strait closure, even if short-term, will trigger violent fluctuations in international oil prices. For Asian economies dependent on Middle Eastern energy—China, Japan, South Korea, India—the risk of supply chain disruption will force them to accelerate energy diversification strategies.

From an international law perspective, military attacks on civilian vessels seriously violate the basic principles of freedom of navigation under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea. While the US retaliatory action claims to be based on the right of self-defense, unilateral military action without UN Security Council authorization remains controversial under international law. This "force begets force" pattern could set a dangerous precedent for other regional disputes.

From a regional power balance analysis, Iran's hardline approach is closely related to its new Supreme Leader's need to consolidate power. Mojtaba Khamenei needs to demonstrate a tough external stance to rally domestic support and show resolve to hardline factions like the Iranian Revolutionary Guard Corps. However, this strategy may push Iran toward strategic isolation—Gulf Arab states may further strengthen military cooperation with the United States and even consider normalizing relations with Israel.

At the global economic level, soaring energy prices will intensify inflationary pressures, posing challenges to central banks' monetary policies. European and Asian manufacturing nations will face rising production costs, potentially delaying post-pandemic economic recovery. Meanwhile, insurance market premium adjustments for vessels transiting the Strait of Hormuz will push up global trade costs.

The prospects for diplomatic resolution are not optimistic. The lack of direct communication channels between the US and Iran, combined with domestic political climates in both countries unfavorable to compromise, creates significant barriers. While Russia and China call for restraint, their influence remains limited. The UN Security Council may struggle to form effective resolutions due to great power divisions. Against this backdrop, the risk of escalation is rising.
