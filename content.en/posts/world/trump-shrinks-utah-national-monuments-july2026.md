## Core Summary

President Trump has signed an executive order significantly reducing the protected area of two national monuments in Utah. This marks the second time during his tenure that such action has been taken in the region, drawing strong condemnation from environmental organizations, Native American tribes, and Democratic lawmakers. The Guardian reports that these monuments hold sacred significance for indigenous tribes, and the reduction threatens cultural heritage preservation.

## Event Details

According to The Washington Post, President Trump has once again used executive authority to dramatically cut the protected area of two national monuments in Utah. The New York Times notes this is the second time Trump has taken similar action against Utah's national monuments, having previously attempted to reduce the sizes of Grand Staircase-Escalante and Bears Ears National Monuments.

The Associated Press reports that Trump's executive order reduces the protected area by a substantial percentage, reopening previously federally protected land to mining, oil extraction, and commercial development. The Salt Lake Tribune describes this as "another dramatic cut to Utah's national monuments."

The Guardian emphasizes that these monuments are considered "sacred lands" by Native American tribes, containing numerous archaeological sites and cultural heritage. Reducing protection means these valuable cultural resources face destruction risks. Environmental organizations and indigenous rights groups have announced they will immediately file legal challenges against the executive order's legality.

## Panoramic Perspective

Trump's reduction of national monuments reflects the deep conflict between resource development and environmental protection in the United States. Under the Antiquities Act, presidents have authority to establish national monuments, but the law does not clearly specify whether presidents can reduce their size—this legal gray area is the focus of recurring disputes.

From an economic perspective, the pro-development stance argues that releasing land resources can promote employment and increase tax revenue, particularly in energy-dependent regions like Utah. However, environmental experts point out that these monuments possess unique geological and ecological value, and once destroyed, the damage is irreversible. More importantly, Native American tribes' cultural rights have long been neglected, and their voices are often marginalized in policy-making processes.

From a political game analysis, this move is a strategic action for Trump to consolidate his conservative voter base in the West. Utah, as a Republican stronghold, has high appeal for energy development issues among its voters. However, with the awakening of indigenous rights awareness and the growth of environmental movements, the social costs of such policies are rising.

## Multiple Perspectives

**Supporters**: The energy industry and conservative think tanks argue that reducing monument sizes helps unlock economic potential, create jobs, and reduce excessive federal government regulation of land use.

**Opponents**: Environmental organizations like the Sierra Club, Native American tribal alliances, and Democratic lawmakers strongly condemn the action, viewing it as destruction of cultural heritage and natural ecology, violating the spirit of federal protection laws.

**Legal Community**: Constitutional scholars point out that presidential authority to reduce national monuments remains legally contested. The Supreme Court has never made a clear ruling on this issue, and this lawsuit could become a landmark precedent.
