## Core Summary

The military standoff between the United States and Iran over control of the Strait of Hormuz continues to escalate. Both sides claim authority over the strategic waterway and have conducted fresh rounds of airstrikes in recent hours. According to The Washington Post, Tehran has declared peace negotiations to be in a "crisis phase," signaling the collapse of ceasefire agreements. International oil prices have surged in response, while AI-related stocks have declined broadly.

## Event Details

According to the Associated Press, both the United States and Iran have asserted control over the Strait of Hormuz, engaging in renewed military confrontations near the world's most critical oil transit route. The US military has previously struck multiple Iranian military targets, with Iran taking retaliatory measures.

The Washington Post, citing sources, reports that ceasefire agreements are disintegrating following an "intense weekend of airstrikes." Iranian officials have publicly stated that peace negotiations have entered a "crisis phase," suggesting diminishing prospects for diplomatic breakthroughs.

President Trump is reportedly considering a plan for the US to take over Strait of Hormuz shipping security and impose a 20 percent fee on cargo shipments. This proposal has drawn widespread international attention and controversy.

Oil prices have risen notably due to Middle East tensions. Meanwhile, investor risk aversion has intensified, with technology stocks—particularly AI concept shares—under broad pressure. CNBC reports that markets fear conflict expansion could disrupt global energy supply chains, thereby raising inflation expectations.

## Panoramic Analysis

The US-Iran standoff over the Strait of Hormuz has entered a dangerous "gray zone." Both sides claim control, yet neither can fully seal the waterway—doing so would have catastrophic consequences for global energy markets, including devastating impacts on Iran's own oil exports.

From a strategic perspective, Trump's proposal to "take over the strait and charge transit fees" essentially attempts to convert military advantage into economic revenue. However, this plan lacks basis in international law and could provoke strong opposition from global shipping nations, including US allies. For Asian economies dependent on Middle Eastern crude—China, Japan, South Korea, and India—any disruption to strait transit would pose direct economic security threats.

From an energy market perspective, the Strait of Hormuz carries approximately 21 million barrels of crude oil daily, one-third of global seaborne petroleum trade. Even if conflict does not lead to complete strait closure, soaring insurance costs and shipping route diversions will significantly elevate global energy expenses. For central banks at critical inflation-taming junctures, this represents a troubling policy challenge.

## Multiple Perspectives

US officials emphasize that military operations aim to "protect international shipping safety" and "maintain regional stability," viewing Iran's attacks on civilian vessels as unacceptable provocations.

Iran maintains that US strikes constitute sovereignty violations and has hinted at further countermeasures. Tehran's characterization of the situation as a "crisis phase" indicates declining confidence in diplomatic resolution.

International energy market analysts broadly worry that sustained conflict will drive oil prices higher. Some analysts note that if strait transit faces substantive disruption, prices could breach the psychological $100-per-barrel threshold, posing major obstacles to global economic recovery.
