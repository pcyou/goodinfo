**[Core Summary]**
The Guardian and the New York Times reported that María del Carmen Abascal, the 87-year-old woman whose forced eviction ignited Spain’s national housing protests, has died in Madrid. Last month she was carried out on a stretcher from the apartment she had lived in for more than 70 years, and the fallout forced the government to adjust housing policy.

**[Event Details]**
- **Background**: María del Carmen Abascal had lived in her Madrid apartment for more than 70 years and faced eviction due to a dispute between her landlord and an investment fund.
- **Eviction footage**: In September, video of her being carried out by law-enforcement officers on a stretcher went viral, igniting Spanish public anger over the housing crisis.
- **Nationwide protests**: The incident triggered protests involving hundreds of thousands of people in Madrid, Barcelona and other cities, opposing real-estate speculation and the expansion of short-term rental platforms.
- **Policy response**: Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez’s government subsequently announced a series of housing-protection measures, including restrictions on large investment funds acquiring residential property in key cities.
- **Death**: Abascal died at a Madrid hospital within weeks of the incident, with unions issuing statements of condolence and emphasizing her symbolic significance.
- **Legacy**: Her case has become a microcosm of Spain’s housing inequality, population aging and urban-renewal conflicts, prompting reflection on protections for vulnerable groups.

**[Panoramic Analysis]**
Abascal’s death offers a concrete and weighty footnote to Spain’s housing crisis. Why her personal experience triggered nationwide protests is rooted in the fact that, after the bursting of Spain’s real-estate bubble, the housing issue has evolved from a market topic into a generational topic: young people struggle to afford rent, while the elderly face the risk of being evicted by investment funds. The Abascal episode accelerated the Sánchez government’s housing policy adjustments, but whether it can fundamentally reshape Spain’s housing supply and protection system will still depend on fiscal space, legislative efficiency and local execution. Across Europe, Spain is not alone: Portugal, Italy, Ireland and others face similar tensions between investment-fund inflows and the marginalization of local residents, and Abascal’s death is likely to heat up policy debates in those countries as well.

**[Comparative Perspectives]**
- **Spanish government**: Housing-policy changes are seen as a response to public pressure, but supply and rent-affordability pressures remain unresolved.
- **Housing advocates**: Say the case exposes gaps in protections for older people and low-income tenants.
- **Residents and unions**: Call for stronger supervision of eviction procedures and stronger information rights for long-term residents.
- **Real-estate investors**: Argue that the crisis requires more supply and investment rather than simple limits on capital; parties still differ over enforcement.
- **European observers**: Say Spain’s experience may shape debates over short-term rentals, investment funds and vulnerable-group protection in Portugal, Italy and other countries.
