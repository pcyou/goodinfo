## Core Summary

New Zealand's ruling coalition has fallen one seat short of securing a parliamentary majority in the official vote count. Prime Minister Christopher Luxon's National Party and its allies need the support of the centrist Opportunity Party to continue governing. If an agreement cannot be reached, the country may be forced to hold another general election.

## Event Details

According to the official vote count released on November 27, New Zealand's ruling coalition failed to secure a majority of the 130 seats in parliament, falling just one seat short. This result stems from special votes (cast by overseas citizens or voters who were outside their registered electorate on election day), which typically lean toward left-wing parties, causing the ruling coalition to lose two seats.

The National Party, led by incumbent Prime Minister Christopher Luxon, secured 40 seats, while its coalition partners ACT and New Zealand First won 13 and 12 seats respectively, totaling 65 seats—just one short of the 66 needed for a majority.

### Seat Distribution by Party

- **National Party**: 40 seats
- **Labour Party**: 36 seats
- **Green Party**: 17 seats
- **ACT**: 13 seats
- **New Zealand First**: 12 seats
- **Te Pāti Māori**: 5 seats
- **Opportunity Party**: 7 seats

Notably, although Labour won the popular vote, its bloc cannot form a government even with the support of the Opportunity Party. The Opportunity Party has emerged as the "kingmaker" in this election, with its leader Qiulae Wong stating that her party will negotiate with the National Party but all options remain open.

### Reactions from Various Parties

Luxon acknowledged on Friday that negotiations "may be challenging" and will take some time. He told reporters: "Kiwis won't thank us for rushing to an agreement that falls apart or for sending them back to the polls."

Labour leader Chris Hipkins stated that his party "ran an incredible campaign" but acknowledged lacking negotiating power despite winning the popular vote. He said: "More New Zealanders voted for change at this election than for the status quo."

Opportunity Party leader Qiulae Wong told reporters that her party would negotiate with the National Party but all options were on the table. She said: "There are seven parties in parliament and everyone is responsible for making sure we do everything we can to deliver a stable government for New Zealand. It's not just up to us."

### Policy Platform Comparison

The National Party's key campaign promises include lower taxes, cheaper energy costs, increased renewable energy generation, and strengthened defense capabilities.

Labour, meanwhile, pledged to cut healthcare, public transport, and fuel costs, and provide more government-supported social housing.

## Comprehensive Perspective

This election result reflects profound changes in New Zealand's political landscape. The Mixed Member Proportional (MMP) representation system makes it difficult for a single party to secure an absolute majority, making coalition governments the norm. However, the ruling coalition's narrow loss of majority status highlights New Zealand voters' dissatisfaction with the current government's economic policies.

From an international perspective, New Zealand, as an important economy in the Asia-Pacific region, has significant implications for regional cooperation and trade relations through its political stability. Political deadlock may lead to policy uncertainty, affecting investor confidence and international partnerships.

Furthermore, the rise of the Opportunity Party as a new political force reflects the dissatisfaction of centrist voters with both traditional left and right-wing parties. This phenomenon is evident globally, indicating that voters are increasingly seeking pragmatic, moderate policy solutions rather than ideologically driven extreme positions.
