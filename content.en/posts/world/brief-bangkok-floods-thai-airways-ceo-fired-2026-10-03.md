The Guardian reports that Bangkok, the capital of Thailand, is suffering severe flooding, with streets turned into stagnant lakes and city operations stretched to breaking point. Reportage shows water depths on some major arteries sufficient to block normal vehicle traffic, severely disrupting residents' movement and forcing parts of the metro and commuter rail networks to adjust schedules or temporarily suspend service to avoid safety incidents. This ranks among the most serious inner-city flooding episodes to hit Bangkok in recent years and underlines the chronic flood exposure facing Southeast Asian megacities in the era of climate change.

The knock-on impact has reached corporate governance: according to Reuters, the CEO of Thai Airways publicly disclosed that he had been fired by the board over the flood crisis response. This illustrates how the emergency-response capabilities of large infrastructure operators, on metrics such as customer experience, baggage handling, and flight punctuality, are increasingly central to how boards and capital markets judge management. Thai Airways has also pledged to properly handle baggage claims and compensation for stranded passengers.

The episode highlights three deeper issues: first, climate change is raising both the frequency and intensity of extreme rainfall events, requiring fresh assessment of urban planning and drainage-system design; second, business-continuity management at major public-transport and aviation hubs during disasters needs more deliberate redundancy design and cross-agency coordination; third, the path to repairing a city's brand and tourism image after a disaster event will be an important joint agenda for government and industry going forward.
