## Key Facts

Research published in Fortune argues that the federal emergency management agency's hundred-year flood maps mark a far smaller flood exposure than actually exists. By one estimate, about six million additional homes face flood risk, putting the total number of properties at risk at 14.6 million, and the official maps are widely outdated, with some dating back two decades.

The study does not merely dispute where the maps draw their boundaries. It questions the basic granularity of risk assessment. The maps answer one question: is water likely to reach my neighbourhood. They do not assess the risk of an individual home, or what the impacts are likely to be when the water rises. Understanding the real impact requires more data, including the probable flood depth at the home, the building's age and construction quality, and insurance coverage.

The researchers combined four types of information to build a detailed flood risk score for nearly one million homes, and found that scores differed more from house to house within a neighbourhood than from one neighbourhood to the next.

## Event Details

- **The scale of the undercount**: about six million homes face flood risk without being marked on the official maps, with one estimate putting the total number of properties at risk at 14.6 million.
- **Age of the documents**: the official maps are commonly outdated, and some are two decades old.
- **What the maps actually answer**: whether water is likely to reach a neighbourhood, not the risk or the likely consequences for a particular home.
- **The granularity of the research**: four categories of information were combined into a detailed risk score covering nearly one million homes.
- **First, flood depth**: the probable depth of water at the property during a hundred-year flood event, defined as having a one percent chance of occurrence in any given year, taken from a national flood model's parcel-level depth map and recorded for each residence in Harris County.
- **Second, the building itself**: how old the home is and how well it was built and maintained, since older and poorly repaired structures suffer more damage when water enters, with data drawn from county appraisal records and expert assessors.
- **Third, road access**: typical traffic within roughly half a mile and within three miles, since busier surrounding roads mean flooding disrupts trips to work, school and hospitals and make it harder for first responders to reach trapped residents.
- **Fourth, recovery cost**: what it would cost to repair the home and what share of nearby homes carry flood insurance, neither of which keeps water out but both of which reflect the cost of recovery.
- **How the scores are distributed**: vulnerability scores differ more between houses within a neighbourhood than between neighbourhoods, so a community that looks safe on average may contain the county's riskiest homes.
- **The Houston precedent**: the team's earlier work there found that more than half of 100,000 documented flood-prone buildings currently lie outside official flood boundaries, and that these overlooked buildings are more likely to be in poor condition and uninsured.
- **The insurance gap**: the majority of homeowners' policies do not cover flood damage, so the gap between risk and protection falls directly on households.
- **What owners can check themselves**: ask longtime neighbours or the city's flood plain office whether the home or street has flooded before, look up the home's age in local property records and have an inspector assess its condition, check whether routes to work and school flood easily, and confirm whether flood insurance is in place.

## Comparison of Viewpoints

**The core problem on the federal side is stale data, and the cost of that staleness falls on households.** The research links outdated maps directly to the six million unmarked homes: the official documents are not wrong because they were drawn badly, but because they have not been updated in years. Because the vast majority of homeowners' policies exclude flood damage, the cost of that lag is not spread across an insurance pool but lands directly on families whose map says they are safe.

**Finding that differences within a neighbourhood exceed differences between neighbourhoods is in effect a challenge to allocating disaster resources by administrative area.** When the score shows a new build and a dilapidated house on the same street facing very different consequences, yet official classification puts both in the same risk zone, then flood funding and insurance pricing cut by geography are systematically mismatched. The value of the work is that it turns flood risk from a map into a quantity that can be attached to a specific address.

**The researchers' own earlier work validates the new study.** The Houston finding, that more than half of documented flood-prone buildings lie outside official boundaries and are more likely to be in poor condition and uninsured, means the homes the maps omit have never been a random sample. They have consistently been the ones with the highest exposure and the weakest coverage.

**This sits in structural tension with how the American disaster and insurance system works.** The maps determine who must buy flood insurance, who qualifies for federal relief and where flood protection is worth building. When the underlying document is both stale and conceals individual variation behind community averages, the bias of the system is reproduced at all three points at once.

**The self-service guidance also reveals how much is left to individuals.** Every step the research recommends, asking neighbours, checking property records, hiring an inspector, reviewing routes, verifying cover, is something a household can do unaided. Each step works but costs money and effort, which is in effect a way of using private labour to fill a gap in the public data product.

## Panoramic Analysis

On its surface this research is about an inaccurate map. What it actually exposes is a mismatch in the basic unit of risk governance. Flood maps descend from a mid-twentieth-century product built to support flood control engineering, and their logic answers where to build a levee rather than whose house will flood. The two goals coincide most of the time, but once they separate, the map stops working. And the evidence here says they have separated: risk varies more between houses on the same street than between neighbourhoods, which means administrative-area risk classification is losing its protective value for individual households faster than most official discussions acknowledge.

The characteristics of the omitted group deserve as much attention as the size of the omission. The earlier Houston work already showed that more than half of documented flood-prone buildings sit outside official boundaries, in worse condition and with less insurance. That closes a self-reinforcing loop: because the maps omit them, they are not required to carry insurance; because they carry no insurance, nobody invests in hardening; because they are not hardened, flood losses there are larger; and larger losses reinforce the belief that the area is not a risk zone. Redrawing boundaries without breaking that loop only postpones the next failure.

The amplification through institutions matters just as much. The same map is the input to three separate systems: eligibility for federal relief, the pricing of flood insurance and the siting of protection projects. When one input document is both stale and flattened to community averages, the error propagates into all three in the same direction, so the people who are undercounted receive less aid, are charged more and receive less protection. That is how a technical map-update problem becomes a political question about affordability and distributive fairness.

For readers, the useful conclusion is that household flood risk is shifting from being a property of a region to a property of a building. Two homes on one street can differ several-fold, which means risk assessment, insurance decisions and hardening priorities all need to descend to the level of the individual property. That demands something specific from data infrastructure: combining parcel-level flood depth, building condition records and road access data into a single score attached to an individual address. Nothing in that requires new technology. It requires realigning three scattered public datasets at the scale of property ownership, and then making the result directly available to residents. As long as flood information is published only as a community average, its consumer is the government; only when it is published as a score for a single home is its consumer the person actually carrying the risk.
