## Key Facts

Drone technology and artificial intelligence are rapidly reshaping modern warfare, but the latest results from the largest American and European defence firms show conventional equipment continuing to drive growth. Lockheed Martin reported a 24% jump in revenue from its missile business in the third quarter, RTX secured multibillion-dollar contracts for established missile systems, and German supplier Renk saw military vehicle orders surge 46% in the first nine months of the year.

- **Optronics growth came from legacy platforms**: German defence electronics firm Hensoldt reported a 52% jump in optronics revenue, with chief financial officer Christian Ladurner saying much of that growth came from conventional equipment, including tanks and armoured vehicles.
- **Air defence was the fastest-growing segment**: Rheinmetall's air defence business, which includes counter-drone systems, posted 63% revenue growth, even as the wider company cut its full-year outlook because of project delays.
- **NATO's counter-drone commitment**: Secretary General Mark Rutte warned that drones have fundamentally transformed modern warfare, and the alliance plans to invest more than $40 billion in counter-drone capabilities over the next five years.
- **The bottleneck is high-end interceptors**: limits on the production of US-made systems able to take down high-speed missiles have been felt hard, not least in Ukraine, after much of the stockpile was depleted during the war with Iran.
- **Air defence remains underinvested**: Hensoldt's chief financial officer described the air defence market as underinvested despite growing by 20% a year, with militaries confronting threats ranging from small drones to ballistic missiles.
- **Investor concern shifted to execution**: analysts mostly describe the rearmament boom as structural, unlikely to be derailed even if the wars in Ukraine and Iran end, while Europe must catch up amid uncertainty over US security guarantees. Valuation concerns have nonetheless dented defence stocks this year amid project delays and contract cancellations.

## Event Details

The Hen­soldt disclosure is the one worth reading twice, because it dissolves the contradiction the market kept trying to maintain. Optical and sensor technologies grew 52%, and the CFO attributed much of it to tanks and armoured vehicles rather than to new platforms. In other words, the revenue generated by the drone era landed inside the accounts of the companies that make the things drones are meant to replace, because those things still need optics, and now need more of them.

Rheinmetall's result shows the same pattern from the other direction. Counter-drone systems sit inside an air defence business that grew 63%, which means the segment benefiting from the new form of warfare is the same segment that has been building traditional air defence systems for decades. It is also the segment where the earnings carry a warning, since the company cut its full-year outlook over project delays rather than over weak demand.

That distinction runs through the whole sector this quarter. Order books have swollen since the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in early 2022, and the expectation that this is structural rather than cyclical has become consensus. But consensus about demand has a cost, because once everyone agrees the order book is sufficient, the only remaining source of differentiated information is execution, and execution is exactly where several programmes have disappointed.

## Competing Views

The bullish argument rests on structural demand plus a European catch-up requirement. If the rearmament cycle is structural and Europe must close a decades-long investment gap under uncertainty about US guarantees, then order books should stay full for years. Hensoldt's own assessment of the air defence market, growing 20% annually while still underinvested, supports that reading from the supplier side.

The sceptical argument targets delivery rather than demand. Rheinmetall demonstrated in a single reporting period that a business can grow 63% in its strongest segment and still disappoint on the full-year outlook. That is the pattern investors have punished all year, alongside contract cancellations, and it suggests that the binding constraint is shifting from appropriations to engineering and production throughput.

A third view emphasises that the change is happening to the shape of platforms rather than to their numbers. Tanks and weapons are increasingly woven into connected, data-driven battlefield networks, meaning legacy manufacturers face a product-transition pressure that is not yet large enough to impair anything but is already large enough to redirect capital spending. On this reading, the strategic question for incumbents is not whether drones take their market, but whether they can capture the electronics value that the transition creates.

## Panoramic Analysis

The most valuable thing these results show is that they prove, in the same quarter, two propositions the market had been treating as contradictory: drones have not displaced conventional equipment, and conventional equipment makers have not been marginalised by them. The Hensoldt figure carries that argument. Optronics revenue grew 52%, and much of the growth came from tanks and armoured vehicles. Translated, the demand created by the drone era did not leave the books of the firms building what drones are meant to replace. It arrived as sensors, fire control and networked combat systems, and it stayed.

That dissolves a framework the market carried for two years. The substitution thesis assumed new technology would erode old revenue, so legacy contractors deserved a discount. The actual structure is expansion along two curves at once. Drone systems grow in the incremental market, air defence and interceptors grow in the incremental market, and legacy platforms themselves get a second sales opportunity as they are retrofitted with new electronics. When one supplier grows on both curves, the premise of the discount disappears.

The second layer concerns where the bottleneck actually sits. Attention has focused on drone production capacity, but the scarce node is high-end interceptor munitions, the least substitutable component in the chain with the longest replenishment cycle. Stocks of the systems able to take down high-speed missiles were heavily depleted during the war with Iran, and the same constraint is felt in Ukraine. Even if electronic production scales quickly, the defensive system cannot be completed while its most expensive ammunition stays in short supply.

The third layer is the European contradiction visible inside one set of results. A company can post 63% growth in air defence and still cut its full-year outlook over delays, which locates the real constraint of the rearmament cycle. It is no longer primarily the willingness of governments to spend. It is the ability to convert appropriations into delivered systems on schedule. Where that transition goes next decides whether Europe's catch-up window is measured in years or in decades.

The valuation lesson follows from all of this. The sector's underperformance relative to its fundamentals was not the market misreading demand. It was the market pricing demand first and then correcting for delivery progress. Once an industry's order book is treated as universally sufficient, the only remaining source of differentiated information is execution timing. Which means that in the next reporting round the order backlog itself carries less information than the delivery schedule, the ramp rates, and the contractual terms around scope changes.

Four things deserve tracking: whether NATO member commitments on counter-drone spending show up as actual procurement rather than as headline commitments, the production expansion and restocking rates for high-end interceptors, whether companies such as Rheinmetall can recover their reduced full-year outlooks, and whether sensors and networked systems continue to take a larger share of legacy platform orders. The last is the one most likely to be ignored, and it determines whether legacy contractors are ultimately valued as vehicle builders or as electronics suppliers.
