## Core summary

Ethiopian government forces entered the regional capital of Tigray in early October after months of military operations, ending years of armed control by the Tigray People's Liberation Front. The development marks a new phase in Ethiopia's internal war with deep implications for the wider Horn of Africa, where the long-running conflict has shaped humanitarian conditions, regional diplomacy and the political architecture of Africa's most populous landlocked country.

## What happened

Drawing on coverage by Reuters, the Associated Press, France 24 and Al Jazeera, the recapture of the Tigray regional capital by Ethiopian federal forces can be summarised as follows:

- **Military progress**: After months of sustained operations, Ethiopian government forces entered the Tigray regional capital of Mekelle in early October. Tigray People's Liberation Front fighters lost the direct battle for the city and a significant number withdrew towards surrounding highland areas.
- **Background to the conflict**: The Tigray war dates back to 2020, when the Tigray People's Liberation Front refused to accept reforms that would dilute its regional political and military power. It then held parts of the region through armed force for several years, displacing large numbers of civilians and triggering a deep humanitarian crisis.
- **Ceasefire cycles**: Over the past two years, the African Union and several partner governments brokered a series of fragile ceasefire arrangements. Repeated breakdowns, driven by a lack of political and security trust, allowed armed fighting to restart.
- **Regional spillover**: The Tigray conflict has spilled over into Eritrea and Sudan at various points, and has imposed sustained pressure on Red Sea shipping, cross-border trade and refugee flows.
- **Humanitarian situation**: Multiple United Nations agencies have repeatedly warned that Tigray faces severe food insecurity, critical shortages of medical supplies and the breakdown of basic services, a picture that further escalation risks worsening.
- **Political variable**: With Mekelle now in federal hands, the political arrangement between Ethiopia's federal government and Tigray, the broader ethnic balance, and the future architecture of regional governance will become the key variables in determining long-term stability.
- **International response**: The African Union, the United Nations and several Western governments have previously mediated on the conflict. After the latest developments, all are watching closely for the reopening of political dialogue and humanitarian access.

Reuters notes that, as one of Africa's most populous countries, Ethiopia's internal stability has significant spillover effects on the Horn of Africa, the wider continental security landscape and the shipping corridors of the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden.

## Wider perspective

The Ethiopian federal forces' recapture of Mekelle marks a new phase in the long-running Tigray conflict, and the way forward will reshape the security, humanitarian and governance architecture of the Horn of Africa.

**First, the long-tail pattern of African conflicts is again on display.** The Tigray war has cycled since 2020 through large-scale fighting, fragile ceasefires, repeated deal breakdowns and renewed armed clashes, until the recent decisive gains by federal forces. **The trajectory shows that the real resolution of a regional conflict cannot rely on military outcomes alone; it requires political settlement, ethnic reconciliation and international coordination to advance in step.**

**Second, the compound effect of internal wars spilling across borders is accumulating across the Horn of Africa.** Beyond the humanitarian cost inside Ethiopia, the Tigray conflict has at times spilled into Eritrea and Sudan and has imposed sustained pressure on Red Sea shipping, cross-border trade and refugee arrangements. **The Horn of Africa is a critical node for global shipping and geopolitics, and the linked effects of its internal conflicts travel well beyond the events themselves, a reality that deserves continued international attention.**

**Third, the risk of long-forgotten humanitarian crises warrants vigilance from the international community.** Multiple United Nations agencies have repeatedly warned of severe food insecurity, medical shortages and the breakdown of basic services in Tigray, yet global media attention and aid resources for this crisis have been visibly limited. **With decisive military progress now on the table, restoring humanitarian access and avoiding a new wave of human suffering must be a priority for the international community.**

**Fourth, federalism, ethnic relations and post-war political architecture will determine long-term stability.** Ethiopia relies on a federal framework to hold together its diverse ethnic communities, and the Tigray conflict exposes deep structural tensions in how federalism handles regional politics, the distribution of military power, and central-local relations. **With Mekelle now under federal control, the key question is whether political trust between the federal government and Tigray can be rebuilt, and whether ethnic reconciliation and sustainable governance can be designed in a way that breaks the conflict cycle.**

**Fifth, the boundary between African-led mediation and external support is being redefined.** The African Union and several partner governments have played a leading role in mediating this conflict, yet international political attention and resources devoted to African conflicts remain limited. **Effectively resolving African conflicts in the future will require a more stable and predictable international support mechanism that respects African agency, so that long-running crises do not become forgotten ones.**
