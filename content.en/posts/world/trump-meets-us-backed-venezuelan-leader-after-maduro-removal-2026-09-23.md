## Trump Meets US-Backed Venezuelan Leader for First Time Since Maduro Was Detained

**[Core Summary]** On September 23, 2026, US President Donald Trump welcomed Delcy Rodriguez, the US-backed transitional leader of Venezuela, to the White House. The meeting marks the first formal high-level engagement between Washington and Caracas since former president Nicolas Maduro was detained and removed from Venezuela. The encounter signals that the Trump administration's policy toward Venezuela is moving from confrontation toward active political shaping, with significant implications for regional diplomacy and global energy markets.

According to the BBC, the talks focused on the political transition process in Venezuela, cooperation on the country's vast oil resources, regional security dynamics, and the possible easing of US economic sanctions. The meeting was held in the Oval Office and acknowledged with a brief official statement, but specific details of the conversation were not publicly disclosed.

Rodriguez is a long-standing figure in Venezuelan politics who held senior government positions during the Maduro era. After Maduro's detention and removal, she emerged as the US-backed transitional leader. Trump has publicly expressed confidence in Rodriguez, arguing that she can maintain stability while gradually steering Venezuela toward outcomes aligned with US strategic interests.

A central topic of the meeting is widely believed to be Venezuela's oil industry. Venezuela holds some of the world's largest proven crude oil reserves, but production has collapsed over the past decade due to sanctions, underinvestment, and political turmoil. Any normalization of US policy could open the door to foreign investment and a partial restoration of Venezuelan crude exports.

**[Panorama]** The Trump-Rodriguez meeting represents a fundamental pivot in US policy toward Venezuela. For years, Washington's approach relied on a combination of economic sanctions, diplomatic isolation, and support for opposition figures. The decision to personally meet and elevate a US-backed transitional leader signals that the administration is now seeking to actively engineer the country's political trajectory rather than simply apply external pressure. This represents a more activist, and arguably more risky, model of regime engagement.

The regional implications are substantial. Latin American governments that maintain cooperative relationships with Washington will see the meeting as vindication of their approach, while governments in Cuba, Nicaragua and elsewhere that retain ties to the former Maduro administration will face renewed diplomatic pressure. Left-wing political movements across Latin America will also reassess their own relationships with the United States in light of what appears to be a successful, if controversial, transition playbook.

The global energy implications could prove equally significant. Venezuelan oil returning to international markets — even in modest volumes — would alter supply dynamics at a time when producers and consumers are recalibrating their strategies amid the energy transition. OPEC members, particularly those competing for market share in the Americas, will watch closely. For major importing nations, any normalization would be welcomed as an opportunity to diversify supply away from more volatile producers.

**[Multi-perspective Comparison]** Supporters of the meeting, including US energy industry voices, several Latin American governments friendly to Washington, and parts of the Venezuelan diaspora, argue that a managed political transition is the most realistic path back to stability and economic recovery. They also emphasize the humanitarian dimension, noting that a return to economic normalcy could alleviate the regional migration crisis.

Critics, including left-leaning Latin American governments, international legal scholars, and some Western European officials, question the legitimacy of one government selecting and installing the leadership of another sovereign state. They argue that the precedent set in Venezuela could encourage similar interventions elsewhere and warn that any transition that lacks broad domestic legitimacy risks producing new forms of political instability.

International bodies, including the International Organization for Migration and the Office of the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights, have called on all parties to ensure that the transition process respects international human rights standards, protects vulnerable populations, and avoids actions that could trigger new waves of displacement.
