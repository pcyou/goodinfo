**[Core Summary]**

US President Donald Trump and the Iranian government separately stated on October 11 that the upcoming US midterm elections will not factor into the Middle East peace talks. Against the backdrop of Houthi strikes on Riyadh's airport, both sides' synchronized messaging is widely interpreted as an effort to stabilize a diplomatic tempo shaken by recent regional escalation.

**[Event Details]**

- **Trump's position**: At a White House press briefing, Trump made clear that the midterm elections are "not relevant" to Middle East peace talks, emphasizing that US foreign policy operates independently of the election cycle.
- **Iranian response**: Iran's Foreign Ministry spokesperson stated that Tehran's approach to negotiations "remains unchanged," and accused the US side of lacking "good faith" by linking negotiations to domestic politics.
- **Background event**: Houthi forces recently struck Riyadh's airport, causing multiple casualties; the Saudi-led coalition responded with strikes against targets inside Yemen.
- **Trump administration recalibration**: After the Riyadh attack, Trump publicly stated he is "looking at" joining Saudi Arabia in strikes against the Houthis, though no final decision has been made.
- **Regional escalation**: Iran-aligned militias in Iraq, Lebanon, and Syria have shown increased activity, raising risks of broader regional spillover.
- **Market reaction**: International crude prices saw heightened volatility, with Brent crude swinging more than 3% intraday.

**[Panoramic Analysis]**

Trump and Iran's joint denial that midterm elections factor into the talks is fundamentally a synchronized "political firewall" statement. From the US domestic political perspective, Republican midterm priorities concentrate on the economy and immigration, leaving foreign policy unlikely to become a central campaign axis. Trump is eager to frame Middle East policy as a "bipartisan continuity achievement" to avoid diplomatic setbacks damaging the electoral outlook.

However, the Houthi strike on Riyadh's airport is striking in its timing. The attack came at a critical juncture of US-Iran nuclear negotiations and is widely viewed as a "stress test" of the negotiation process. As a traditional US ally in the Middle East, Saudi Arabia's core infrastructure coming under attack places the Trump administration in a two-sided bind: staying out would weaken the US commitment to Riyadh, while joining the strikes could collapse the negotiations entirely.

From a broader Middle East perspective, the four-way game between Iran, the Houthis, Saudi Arabia, and the United States is entering a new phase. Iran seeks to extract leverage through limited escalation, Saudi Arabia demands clear international recognition of its security concerns, and the United States must strike a balance between "strategic restraint" and "strategic commitment." Whether Middle East talks can continue depends on these actors' concrete moves in the coming weeks, not their public statements.

**[Comparative Perspectives]**

- **Trump administration**: Officials maintain that midterm elections do not affect the talks, emphasizing that foreign policy "is moving forward as planned," while signaling potential US participation in Saudi strikes against the Houthis.
- **Iranian government**: Officials deny using midterm elections as a bargaining chip, express willingness to continue talks, and demand that the US side show "good faith and constructiveness."
- **Saudi government**: Officials condemn the Houthi strikes and stress that the international community must act, otherwise Saudi Arabia will reserve "the right to self-defense."
- **Houthi forces**: The group claims the Riyadh airport strikes and warns that further escalation by Saudi Arabia or the United States will trigger attacks on additional Middle East targets.
- **Democratic Party**: Critics accuse the Trump administration of "vacillating" Middle East policy and argue that inconsistent messaging risks amplifying regional instability.
- **Market analysts**: Crude oil volatility has intensified, reflecting rising geopolitical risk premiums in the Middle East, though key technical levels have not yet been breached.
