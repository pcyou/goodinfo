## Key Facts

High-level delegations from Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey travelled to Tehran on Monday for meetings with Iranian authorities in an attempt to avert renewed fighting in the Iran war, Iran's Foreign Ministry said. It was the first joint visit to Tehran by the three countries since they formed a defence alliance in Mecca in August.

Saudi Defence Minister Prince Khalid bin Salman, Pakistan's army chief Field Marshal Asim Munir and Ibrahim Kalin, head of Turkish intelligence, met with Iran's top security officials and negotiators to push for a deal between Tehran and Washington. The meeting ended without concrete announcements, with the various parties saying they agreed to continue discussions, according to Iranian state media. Iran's security chief Mohsen Rezaei said the discussions were very good, adding that a new atmosphere is emerging in the region and that security should be achieved by the countries themselves, with an emphasis on preventing any foreign intervention.

Two regional officials told the Associated Press that the war in Yemen and strikes by Iran-backed Houthi rebels against Saudi Arabia were also among the topics discussed. The talks followed a Qatari mediators' trip to Tehran on Sunday, coordinated alongside the United States. Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian, preparing to travel to Moscow for the Gas Exporting Countries Forum, said the visit would include discussions aimed at securing energy transit routes, and noted that the group oversees more than 70% of the world's gas supply.

## Comparison of Viewpoints

- **Iranian side**: Emphasises regional security as the responsibility of regional states and opposes any form of foreign involvement, while attributing the continuing conflict to the United States. The president expressed support for any agreement that deepens regional cooperation and resolves differences.
- **Regional mediators**: Saudi Arabia said the delegations hoped to find ways to de-escalate tensions and improve regional security. No concrete outcome was announced, but the atmosphere was described as positive.
- **External assessment**: Public information shows only principled formulations such as agreement to continue dialogue, with no verifiable arrangement yet in place.

## Panoramic Analysis

The appearance of the top leadership of three countries in the same capital is itself a structural signal. Earlier multilateral attempts in the Middle East took the form of shuttle diplomacy or unilateral statements, whereas a joint delegation by Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey shows regional states trying to assemble scattered mediation efforts into something with the beginnings of a mechanism. The participants include a Sunni-led Saudi Arabia, a Shia-majority Pakistan and Turkey, whose internal interests do not align; the formation of a common position already carries a coordination cost.

The choice of location is equally telling. Tehran is the counterpart's home ground, approached by the three mediators rather than inviting Iran to a third country, which suggests that at this stage every side is preserving a minimum political posture for direct contact. At the same time, by linking the Tehran visit to the following trip to Moscow for the energy forum, the Iranian president tied the regional security agenda to energy transit routes, a formulation that answers European concerns while also folding the country's economic lifeline into the bargaining position.

What will decide the outcome of this round is whether "continuing dialogue" can become a verifiable arrangement. The public record currently shows no specific commitments. This is the familiar trap of regional mediation: every party is willing to voice de-escalation, yet none wants to move first and unilaterally lower military readiness. If tensions rise again, the real test will be whether the mechanism can actually be activated rather than merely continuing to exist.
