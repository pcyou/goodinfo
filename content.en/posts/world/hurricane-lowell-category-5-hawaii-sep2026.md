## Core Summary

Hurricane Lowell has rapidly intensified from a tropical storm to a Category 5 hurricane with sustained winds exceeding 250 km/h. Forecasters predict the storm will make its closest approach to Kauai and Niihau islands Monday night, still at Category 3-4 strength. Hawaii Governor Josh Green has declared a state of emergency, and residents on Kauai have been rushing to stockpile supplies. This marks Hawaii's third tropical cyclone threat in less than a month.

## Event Details

According to the National Hurricane Center (NHC), Lowell is currently about 1,000 km south of the Hawaiian Islands, moving west-northwest at approximately 15 km/h. The hurricane has undergone two eyewall replacement cycles, causing its intensity to fluctuate between Category 4 and 5.

Hawaii's Emergency Management Agency has activated comprehensive disaster preparedness plans. Multiple schools on Kauai have been converted into emergency shelters, and stores have seen water, canned food, and batteries sold out within hours. Hawaiian Electric has warned residents to prepare for possible power outages and has deployed repair crews on standby.

Notably, Kauai residents are no strangers to hurricanes — Hurricane Iniki in 1992 devastated the island and remains one of the most significant natural disaster memories for many residents. Officials say preparations this time are significantly more thorough than for the previous two hurricanes.

Additionally, three active hurricane systems — Lowell, Karina, and Marie — are currently churning in the Pacific, which is unusual for early September. Meteorologists attribute this to elevated sea surface temperatures this year.

## Panoramic Perspective

Hurricane Lowell's approach highlights the growing challenge that increasingly frequent Pacific hurricane activity poses to the Hawaiian Islands. As one of the most isolated archipelagos on Earth, Hawaii faces unique logistical challenges in disaster response — if infrastructure is damaged, external relief supplies can take days to arrive.

From a broader perspective, the heightened activity of this year's Pacific hurricane season has drawn significant attention from the climate science community. Rising ocean surface temperatures provide more energy for tropical cyclones, significantly increasing the probability of rapid intensification. Lowell's leap from Category 2 to Category 5 in under 48 hours is a textbook case of this trend.

For Hawaii, frequent storm threats affect not only resident safety but also pose potential impacts on tourism, the economic lifeline of the islands. Labor Day weekend is a critical tourism peak season in the United States, and flight cancellations and disrupted vacation plans have already caused considerable economic losses. Building more resilient island disaster preparedness systems in the context of climate change will be a long-term challenge for Hawaii and the entire Pacific region.

## Multiple Perspectives

**Meteorological experts** at the NHC emphasize that while Lowell will most likely pass west of Hawaii rather than make direct landfall, outer rainbands could still bring over 200mm of rainfall and dangerous surf. The risks of inland flooding and landslides should not be underestimated.

**Local government response**: Governor Green urged residents to "stay alert but not panic" at a press conference, emphasizing that preparations are adequate. The Mayor of Kauai urged residents to complete all disaster preparations before the weekend.

**Resident reactions**: Some residents expressed fatigue at the frequent hurricanes, especially with Hurricane Lala having just passed in August. However, others believe that the previous experience has made the community better prepared.
