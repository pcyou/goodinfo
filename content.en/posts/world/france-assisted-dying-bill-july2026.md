## Core Summary

After years of intense debate, France's National Assembly gave final approval to a landmark assisted-dying bill. The legislation allows terminally ill patients to request medical assistance in ending their lives under strict conditions, making France one of the latest European countries to legalize euthanasia. The bill's passage marks a historic step for French society on end-of-life ethics.

## Event Details

According to AP News, France's National Assembly passed the controversial assisted-dying bill by a narrow margin. The voting results reflect deep divisions in French society on this issue, but it ultimately passed with a slim majority.

Reuters reported that the bill establishes strict medical evaluation procedures and multiple safeguard mechanisms. Applicants must be adults suffering from incurable diseases, with their condition having progressed to terminal stages. Medical teams must conduct independent evaluations to confirm that the patient's request is voluntary and consistent. The bill also mandates waiting periods to ensure patients have adequate time to reconsider.

The French President issued a statement after the bill's passage, calling it "a respect for individual dignity and autonomy." He stated that French society has matured enough to rationally discuss this sensitive topic and reach consensus through democratic procedures.

The opposition expressed strong concerns. Some lawmakers and religious groups believe the bill may open a "slippery slope," creating implicit pressure on vulnerable populations. They worry that under economic pressure or family burden, some elderly or disabled individuals may be "chosen" for assisted dying rather than truly acting voluntarily.

## Panoramic Analysis

The passage of France's assisted-dying bill reflects a profound shift in European society's approach to end-of-life ethics. Traditionally influenced by Catholicism, France has now redefined the boundaries of "death rights" amid secularization trends. The core controversy of this legislation lies in: how to balance respect for individual autonomy against preventing abuse?

The bill establishes strict medical evaluation procedures and waiting periods to avoid "slippery slope" effects. However, experience from the Netherlands and Belgium shows that euthanasia's scope has gradually expanded. Initially limited to terminal patients, it has progressively extended to chronic disease patients, mental illness patients, and even minors. Whether France will follow the same path remains to be seen.

From a comparative law perspective, France follows the Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg, and other countries, but as a populous nation, its demonstration effect may influence more European countries to re-examine related laws. Spain and Portugal are already discussing similar legislation, and France's passage may accelerate this trend.

For the medical profession, euthanasia legalization presents profound professional ethics challenges. Doctors traditionally take "saving lives" as their duty, and are now asked to participate in ending lives. This role transition poses challenges to healthcare workers' mental health and professional identity.

## Multiple Perspectives

Supporters view this as a victory for "death with dignity," granting terminal patients ultimate autonomy. They emphasize that in the face of incurable suffering, individuals have the right to choose a dignified end to life rather than passively waiting for death.

Opponents (including religious groups and disability rights organizations) worry this may create implicit pressure on vulnerable populations. They fear that under economic pressure or family burden, some individuals may be "chosen" for assisted dying rather than truly acting voluntarily.

The medical community is divided on the transformation of doctors' roles. Some doctors believe this respects patient autonomy and should provide "humane assistance"; others insist on the "do no harm" principle, believing participation in assisted dying violates medical ethics.
