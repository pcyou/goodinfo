## Iran Offers Seven Day Plan to Reopen Strait of Hormuz US Not in a Rush to Accept

## Core Summary Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi presented a seven-day plan to the United States on Friday to reopen the Strait of Hormuz and proposed using that as a basis to restart broader ceasefire negotiations. The plan is seen by observers as Iran's latest move to gain negotiating leverage ahead of the US midterm elections, but US officials made clear they are not in a hurry to accept the proposed terms, emphasizing that Washington retains military and shipping advantages in the strait.

### Event Details

**Specific content of the seven-day plan**: According to CBS News and Reuters, Iranian Foreign Minister Araghchi presented the plan while in New York for the United Nations General Assembly. The plan is structured in two stages: a seven-day window for the gradual reopening of commercial shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, followed by a return to broader ceasefire talks. The proposal is reportedly similar to the US-Iran memorandum of understanding signed in June, though specific implementation details and timelines have not been made public.

**The US rejection stance**: US military officials said on Friday that in the past two days US forces have redirected seven commercial vessels in the strait, bringing the cumulative number of vessels redirected since Washington reinstated its blockade of Iranian ports on July 14 to 122, with 12 ships redirected in the current week. US Central Command also noted that nearly 40 million barrels of crude oil have transited the strait under US military escort in the past 48 hours, a level comparable to prewar figures. One US official made clear that Washington is currently not in a rush to agree to any deal because the United States holds a significant advantage in the strait.

**Regional ripple effects**: In remarks at the UN General Assembly, Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif warned that Houthi drone attacks on the holy city of Mecca in Saudi Arabia constitute a red line for Islamabad. Rashad al-Alimi, head of the Saudi-backed Yemeni Presidential Leadership Council, called for general mobilization to counter Houthi expansion along the Red Sea coast. The UN Security Council issued a statement condemning Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia. The European Commissioner for Energy warned EU member states in a letter that the Iran war is causing a price crisis for the bloc, urging them to consider measures to curb energy demand.

## Full Picture Viewed at a deeper level, Iran's seven-day plan is essentially a carefully calculated management of negotiating tempo rather than a genuine concession. The Strait of Hormuz carries roughly a fifth of globally seaborne crude trade; prolonged closure not only drives up global oil prices and disrupts supply chains, but directly squeezes Iran's own oil-export revenues. By proposing a "reopen first, then negotiate" path within a seven-day window, Tehran is converting short-term economic pressure into negotiating leverage and amplifying its bargaining power through the lens of the US domestic political calendar. The timing — only weeks before US midterm elections — also reveals Iran's precise read of Washington's political ecosystem.

The episode reflects three new features of Middle East geopolitics in 2026. First, traditional ceasefires are giving way to segmented, issue-by-issue "small-step, fast-iteration" bargaining; from shipping through the strait to energy prices, from Red Sea routes to Saudi security, every party is bargaining across multiple tracks simultaneously, and the overall picture is highly fragmented. Second, the US military footprint in the Middle East remains the hard currency at the negotiating table — the nearly 40 million barrels escorted through the strait in 48 hours is a vivid illustration — but military escort itself means Washington must keep consuming naval resources, and the war-powers debate in Congress (the Senate this week rejected a bill to constrain presidential authority to strike Iran) will further amplify the White House's political cost. Third, the role of third-party states is on the rise; whether Pakistan's red line over attacks on Mecca, the EU's warning of an energy crisis, or the Yemeni government's call for general mobilization, all point to a Middle East conflict whose spillover radius now extends well beyond the traditional Gulf Six, with global supply chains, energy prices, and great-power politics resonating at a markedly higher frequency.

For global markets, the pace at which the Strait of Hormuz reopens will be one of the most important indicators to track over the coming weeks. If Iran's plan makes substantive progress in the next few days, global oil prices, marine insurance rates, and safe-haven asset prices could all pull back in tandem, and capital-flow patterns in emerging markets may improve as a result. Conversely, if Washington continues to delay with a "not in a rush" posture, the long-term militarization of the strait will become more entrenched, and the fiscal burdens on regional states, the diffusion risk of regional conflicts, and the tempo of the global energy transition will all face fresh uncertainty. The true direction of the Middle East conflict may ultimately be settled not in trenches or at the negotiating table, but by how the energy market prices the patience and red lines of every party involved.

## Comparison of Viewpoints

**Iran's official stance**: Foreign Minister Araghchi, through the seven-day plan, sent a signal of "willingness to reopen first and negotiate later," but refused to commit to any specific security concessions before reopening. Iran also stressed that the safety and free flow of the Strait of Hormuz serves the common interest of the international community, and that unilateral militarization cannot be sustained in the long run.

**US government and military**: White House and Central Command officials made clear that they are not in a rush to accept any deal, emphasizing that Washington retains military and shipping advantages in the strait. The Senate's rejection this week of a bill to limit the President's authority to strike Iran reflects that the executive-legislative split over war powers will persist in the short term.

**Regional and third-party states**: Pakistan drew a red line over Houthi attacks on the holy city of Mecca, the EU warned that the Iran war is triggering an energy price crisis, and the Saudi-backed Yemeni government called for general mobilization. Taken together, these statements make clear that negotiations over the Strait of Hormuz are not an isolated event but are embedded in a broader Middle East security, energy-supply-chain, and great-power politics landscape.

**Independent analysis and market views**: Some analysts see Iran's seven-day plan as a tactical concession unlikely to genuinely change the structural disagreements between the two sides on key issues. Other observers argue that even if a comprehensive agreement is hard to reach in the short term, segmented and issue-by-issue negotiation itself helps reduce the risk of miscalculation and prevents the situation from sliding toward more dangerous full-blown confrontation. In the markets, most analysts read the US "not in a rush" posture as a signal that "the advantage is ours," and oil prices and marine insurance rates are likely to remain range-bound at elevated levels in the short term.
