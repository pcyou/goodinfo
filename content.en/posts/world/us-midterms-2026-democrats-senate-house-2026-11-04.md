## Key Facts

Counting in the United States midterms intensified on Tuesday evening, with all 435 House seats and 35 Senate seats up for grabs. According to Associated Press results, Democrats have won 33 House seats against 13 for Republicans, and in the Senate Democrats and allied independents have secured 43 seats against 31, including seats not up for election this cycle. The Blockchain Association's election dashboard showed Democratic odds of controlling the Senate at 94 percent on Kalshi and 93 percent on Polymarket, with House control priced at 98 and 99 percent.

- **Scale of the contest**: every House seat and 35 Senate seats were contested.
- **Confirmed Senate results**: Massachusetts Senator Ed Markey and Delaware Senator Chris Coons have both won reelection, as has Virginia Senator Mark Warner, who defeated Republican challenger Bert Mizusawa in a race widely expected to go his way.
- **Georgia**: Democratic Senator Jon Ossoff defeated Republican Representative Mike Collins to win reelection, becoming the only incumbent Democratic senator defending a seat in a state Donald Trump carried in 2024.
- **The candidates**: Collins is the owner of a family trucking company who drew scrutiny over ties to family members and former staff members who espoused white nationalist and antisemitic views, and who continued to endorse Trump's false claims about widespread election fraud in 2020. Ossoff, a former congressional aide and documentary filmmaker, gained national attention for his questioning of Trump appointees at hearings and has said he is not running for president in 2028.
- **Historical context**: Democrats flipped both Georgia seats in 2021, and Raphael Warnock barely held his in a runoff in 2022.
- **Industry focus**: the Blockchain Association's dashboard identifies 62 priority races, comprising 18 Senate contests and 44 House contests.
- **On regulation**: Maylea Ma, deputy general counsel at 1inch, expects the Securities and Exchange Commission, the Commodity Futures Trading Commission and the Treasury to drive real movement in digital asset policy rather than Congress, while noting that agency engagement does not provide the stability the industry sought through legislation. The stalled CLARITY Act remains unresolved.

## Competing Views

**Prediction markets have repriced to near certainty, which does not make the outcome more predictable.** Senate control at 94 and 93 percent and House control at 98 and 99 percent represent a move from roughly 75 percent earlier the same day. A swing of that size overnight measures the mood of an election night rather than the reliability of a forecast.

**Confirmed races carry a different kind of certainty.** Markey, Coons, Warner and Ossoff are Associated Press calls rather than market prices, and treating the two as equivalent overstates how settled the result actually is.

**The party line on digital assets is already fracturing.** Markey voted against the stablecoin bill in 2025. Coons has criticised the president's crypto ventures and called a memecoin gathering for holders a form of corruption. Warner opposed advancing the CLARITY Act in September on the grounds that digital asset legislation needs safeguards against the president's own financial interests. Ossoff supported impeachment in April 2025 over plans to host a private dinner for top holders of the president's memecoin. Four senators, four positions, and no single direction for the chamber.

**Industry practitioners are, by contrast, relatively relaxed.** The expectation that agencies rather than legislators will set the pace has the merit of being realistic about how the previous decade of stalled bills actually went.

## Panoramic Analysis

The most consequential fact about this election night is not which party won. It is that the crypto industry has quietly relocated its expectations about who writes the rules. Practitioners increasingly argue that meaningful progress on digital asset policy will come from the Securities and Exchange Commission, the Commodity Futures Trading Commission and the Treasury, even if Congress does nothing at all.

That relocation has a sound basis. American crypto policy has spent years in a structural deadlock: legislation requires agreement between two chambers, and electoral cycles, partisanship and conflicts of interest keep deferring it, while rulemaking through agencies needs no new statutory authority, only an interpretation of existing frameworks. This mismatch pushed the industry to place all of its hope in Congress. This election arrives at the same moment the industry begins to acknowledge that the agency route is the main route, which means two readings of one event are running simultaneously.

The distribution of individual senators' positions is more instructive than the chamber totals. Four newly confirmed senators hold four different stances on digital assets: one voted against a stablecoin bill in 2025, one has publicly criticised the president's crypto business, one demanded protections against the president's own financial interests before advancing market structure legislation, and one supported impeachment over a dinner for token holders. A Senate that may change hands does not thereby become a chamber where digital asset legislation moves easily, and additional dissenting votes could make passage harder rather than easier.

There is also a widening gap between the timing of elections and the timing of regulation. Congressional reorganisation takes weeks to confirm, billmaking takes months to years, and even comparatively fast agency rulemaking passes through notice periods and transition arrangements. Meanwhile agency engagement with the industry is increasing, which cuts both ways for firms: it offers a predictable channel for dialogue while placing regulatory discretion more directly in the hands of individual officials rather than in a statute.

What actually deserves attention is not the election itself but three things. Whether the final Senate count reaches a majority and whether the trailing margins in the states that flipped are ultimately confirmed. What the new Congress's first digital asset proposals look like. And whether the three agencies continue the pre-election pace of engagement. The third sets the near-term path. The first two set the long one.
