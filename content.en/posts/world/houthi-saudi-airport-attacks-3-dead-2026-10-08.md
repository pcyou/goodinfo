**[Core Summary]**
Al Jazeera and Reuters reported that Houthi forces struck two airports in Saudi Arabia, killing at least three people and wounding 36. The attacks highlight an escalation in spillover risk from the Yemen conflict and pose serious challenges to regional civil aviation and energy corridors.

**[Event Details]**
- **Casualties**: A Saudi-led coalition spokesman said the strikes on two Saudi airports killed three civilians and wounded 36, with damage to some airport facilities.
- **Targeted Airports**: One of the targets was a civilian airport in southwestern Saudi Arabia near the Yemeni border, suggesting the Houthis are extending their strike range.
- **Weapons Used**: Preliminary findings indicate a mix of drones and missiles; some were intercepted by Saudi air defenses, but others breached the defense perimeter and hit airport areas.
- **Context**: The Houthis have repeatedly targeted Saudi and Emirati sites since 2023; the latest round is among the larger ones in recent months.
- **Regional Response**: Saudi Arabia vowed to take all necessary measures to respond, while the GCC moved into emergency consultations on security coordination.
- **Global Impact**: Several international carriers adjusted Middle East routes, with some airlines suspending flights to certain Saudi airports.

**[Panoramic Analysis]**
**First**, the strikes again demonstrate that the Houthis have built a sustained capability to hit targets deep inside Saudi territory. With Yemen's civil war grinding on and Iranian regional influence expanding, the group's drone, missile and intelligence networks are receiving external support, and its accuracy and destructive power continue to improve. Saudi Arabia's lavishly funded air defense system is under increasing pressure; interception alone cannot fundamentally eliminate the threat.

**Second**, Middle East conflict spillover is expanding from Red Sea shipping and port infrastructure to civilian aviation. Since 2024, the Houthis have repeatedly attacked Red Sea commercial vessels, forcing many shipping companies to reroute via the Cape of Good Hope. Now that the target has shifted to airports, both major civilian transport and energy logistics arteries are under simultaneous strain, making Middle East nodes in global supply chains and aviation networks more fragile.

**Third**, the round of strikes cannot be ignored for its impact on international oil prices and shipping costs. Saudi Arabia is a major global oil exporter, and frequent attacks on its airports and energy infrastructure will directly affect market confidence in stable Middle East crude supply. Combined with rising Iran tensions and Hormuz Strait risk premia, international crude prices could climb again in the short term, exposing global inflation management to fresh imported pressure.

**Fourth**, the attacks will push Saudi Arabia and the GCC to further deepen security coordination with the United States and Israel. Growing Saudi reliance on external air defense, intelligence sharing and joint counterstrike capabilities may trigger a new round of arms sales and security cooperation agreements. At the same time, internal Saudi reflection on "self-defense capability" will accelerate the localization of its defense industry.

**[Comparative Perspectives]**
- **Saudi Arabia**: Strongly condemned the Houthi attacks as "terrorist acts," vowed retaliation and emphasized the protection of civilians and foreign residents.
- **Houthis**: Claimed the attacks were retaliation for Saudi Arabia's continued military involvement in Yemen and the "siege" of Yemeni civilians, warning of an expanded target set.
- **United States and Western Allies**: Reaffirmed commitments to Saudi security, with some countries adjusting embassy and citizen-safety advisories, but no specific military response was announced.
- **International Aviation Industry**: Several carriers reassessed Middle East route safety, with some suspending regular flights to certain Saudi airports.
- **Regional Observers**: View the escalation as resonant with rising Iran tensions and Gaza conflict spillover, arguing the Middle East has entered a new "multi-front turbulence" cycle.
