## Key Facts

A new wave of cyberattacks targeting supermarket refrigeration and freezer equipment spread on Monday across Belgium, Ireland, Luxembourg and Sweden, where temperature settings on cold cabinets at dozens of stores were pushed up or switched into a defrost cycle, spoiling large quantities of fresh and frozen food. Ahold Delhaize and Colruyt both confirmed the incidents overnight. Security researchers pointed to internet-connected refrigerators made by Danish company Danfoss, which no longer provides security updates.

## Event Details

- **Geography**: Stores in Belgium, Ireland, Luxembourg and Sweden confirmed affected. French reporting also named Finland and Estonia, and the BBC counted seven European countries with similar cases.
- **Method**: Beyond raising temperature directly, several stores reported settings switched to defrost mode, with the setpoints modified remotely by the attackers.
- **Confirmation**: Ahold Delhaize and Colruyt confirmed overnight. One Belgian company operating Delhaize systems said the hackers had changed the settings to defrost.
- **Damage**: Stores suffered heavy food losses, with defrosted or spoiled goods thrown out.
- **Relation to the previous wave**: This is the second attack on supermarket cold cabinets in a month. The first hit France, where hundreds of supermarkets suffered food loss and fires after temperatures were deliberately raised.
- **Technical cause**: Security researchers said the cause was internet-connected refrigerators made by Danish company Danfoss, which no longer provides security updates.
- **Extension to military sites**: US Department of Defense officials are reportedly investigating whether freezer units on military bases were hacked by Iran-backed hackers. Earlier this year, cyberattacks against US water providers were blamed on the same source.

## Competing Views

**The retail groups response has been about containing losses rather than assigning blame**. Public statements from Ahold Delhaize and Colruyt stopped at confirmation and damage assessment, with no forensic progress announced. That restraint is itself information, because companies facing cross-border law enforcement cooperation rarely name a culprit early.

**The technical explanation is far more specific than the phrase about hackers**. The researchers point at a particular category of device, connected refrigerators, and at a particular corporate decision, security updates no longer provided. That converts the story from an enigmatic adversary into a supply chain management problem: the equipment ships connected and then loses maintenance for the rest of its life. What matters to a consumer is not that a device was attacked but that an attacked device spoils their food without anyone noticing.

**The difference between the France incident and this one deserves recording**. The first showed that an attack can cause mass spoilage. The second shows an attack capable of reproducing across countries, with the targeted equipment pointing at a single vendor. Intelligence agencies have long warned that food distribution systems are a soft spot, and the cyberattack on food distributor UNFI last year emptied shelves across the United States. That timeline makes this round read as a continuation of the same class of action.

**The military base review is the variable worth watching most**. The Department of Defense is checking whether base freezers were hit by the same actors, and earlier attacks on US water systems were attributed to Iran-backed forces. If that conclusion holds, the character of the event shifts from an attack on commercial infrastructure to an assessment with intelligence preparation attached.

**Caution is still warranted**. Every reference to an Iranian connection remains in the investigating stage, and no independent technical evidence publicly ties this round to a specific actor. Until attribution is complete, the most useful question is not who did it but why equipment like this ends up connected to the internet and unmaintained for years.

## Panoramic Analysis

Treating temperature control systems as targets looks undignified by the standards of traditional security narratives, and yet it exposes a genuinely new risk structure that connectivity is producing.

Traditional information security talks about data: what leaked, who was breached, how privacy is violated. This round points instead at the control surface of the physical world, where what the attackers obtained was not data but write access to temperature, pressure and valves. The value of that capability does not lie in the data. It lies in being able to stop a supply chain. That is why intelligence agencies in several countries pay food distribution, water utilities and building climate systems an attention level they do not extend to most commercial systems.

It is worth noting how little technical sophistication these attacks require. Attackers are cashing in a debt accumulated over years, namely that vendors stopped providing security updates. A category of device that ships connected and then goes unmaintained becomes an exposure with no end date. And such equipment tends to sit in exactly the places least able to afford security, independent stores, suburban supermarkets, the last mile of cold chain. The threshold for these attacks is therefore very low and their replicability very high.

The deeper structural problem is that the internet of things handed over control without handing over responsibility. When a refrigerator maker stops shipping software, it is not clear where the duty falls. The supermarket owns the asset but does not control the firmware. In the end no party has sufficient incentive to carry the cost of the update. This is a classic tragedy of the commons replayed across hardware lifecycles.

The other side of this round is that it expands the definition of critical infrastructure. When people discuss infrastructure security they picture grids and ports. A national fresh food chain has far more actual nodes than a single grid substation, and those nodes are more scattered and less defended. Once cold chain has been validated as an attack surface, the direction of defensive spending changes with it, and the same logic applies to any industrial device that has been connected.
