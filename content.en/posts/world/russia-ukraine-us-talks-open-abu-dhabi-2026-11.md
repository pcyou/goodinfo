## Key Facts

- **Where and when**: Three-way talks between Russia, Ukraine and the United States opened on Tuesday in Abu Dhabi, in a two-day session that is the first of its kind since February.
- **The agenda**: The White House said before the discussions that the session would focus on security guarantees. Russia sees NATO as a threat, and Ukraine wants to prevent future Russian invasions.
- **Who is in the room**: Rustem Umerov, who leads Ukraine's delegation, said the day began with a meeting between the Ukrainian team, the US envoys, and representatives from the United Kingdom, France, Germany, Italy, NATO and the European Commission.
- **The US framing**: Jared Kushner, the president's son-in-law, said on X that he and fellow envoy Steve Witkoff were working on a "negotiated solution that can deliver lasting peace and create the conditions for a sovereign, secure, and prosperous Ukraine within a more stable European security environment."
- **The Russian delegation**: Vladimir Medinsky, who heads the Russian delegation, noted at the start of the talks that the delegation "has become bigger and more professional," and expressed hope the meeting "will allow us to advance on the path towards peace in the interests of the people of Russia and the people of Ukraine."
- **Fighting did not pause**: Russia launched 162 drones of various types and a ballistic missile at Ukraine overnight, Ukraine's air force said. Russia's Defence Ministry said its forces shot down 155 Ukrainian drones overnight above eight Russian regions as well as the Crimean Peninsula.
- **The distance that remains**: The two governments remain far apart on key elements of any prospective deal, and there are no signs the fighting is abating.
- **The record so far**: There were no significant breakthroughs in three previous rounds of talks earlier in the year. The war has already killed hundreds of thousands of soldiers and thousands of civilians, analysts and humanitarian organisations say.
- **The new variable**: European participants are now physically in the opening session, after Moscow had insisted that the three-party format was settled and that Europe had no seat.
- **The strain on the mediator**: This administration has devoted much of its attention to the war with Iran, which has narrowed the bandwidth available to produce a breakthrough here.

## Competing Views

**The presence of European and NATO representatives in the opening session is the structural change that is easiest to overlook.** Moscow had publicly insisted the three-party format was settled and Europe had no place. Ukrainian officials now say the day opened with the British, French, German, Italian, NATO and European Commission representatives in the room. The objection has become a posture, because participants already inside cannot be asked to leave. It also disposes of the old European question of who speaks for Europe: Europe is not there as a single voice but as several sovereign delegations sitting at once.

**Kushner's formulation is worth reading word for word, because it reveals where Washington is aiming.** The reference to a "more stable European security environment" treats Europe's security as a precondition rather than an outcome. That is a highly realistic construction, but it leaves open exactly the gap that matters most to Kyiv: a stable environment is not the same as a guarantee against renewed invasion, and that gap is what the talks will have to close.

**Medinsky's description of a delegation that has "become bigger and more professional" is a directional signal.** The Russian team already arrived with deputy foreign ministers, a deputy operations chief and an intelligence chief. A leader who volunteers the professionalisation of his own team is usually preparing to enter substance rather than process. The same seniority, however, is equally consistent with preparing to manage expectations, and to leave the room with a document that can be presented as a success.

**Simultaneous escalation on the front line is a pressure technique rather than a sign of collapse.** A hundred and sixty-two drones and one ballistic missile against a hundred and fifty-five drones claimed shot down is what leverage looks like when it is applied to a negotiating table. This matches the well-worn pattern in which fighting intensifies before talks begin precisely because the military situation has to change before the diplomatic one can. A quiet first night would have been the more worrying signal, since it usually means both sides expect the talks to be cover for continued operations.

**The most realistic risk for Europe is not exclusion but demotion.** European officials are present in several national capacities, without a common voice and without common authority. The likely result is participation in every discussion and exclusion from every decision, which is precisely the situation Zelensky invoked when he cited the 1945 Yalta conference: affected countries admitted to the room without anyone authorised to speak for them collectively.

## Panoramic Analysis

The dividing line in this round is not what was said on the first day but the fact that the variable of who is in the room has changed irreversibly. A structure designed as the United States relaying messages between two belligerents has become three parties plus four allies plus a multilateral periphery, with the United Kingdom, France, Germany, Italy, NATO and the European Commission all present but with neither a common voice nor common authority. That configuration was unthinkable a year ago, and it means no European security arrangement can now be settled by the three principals alone, even if Washington still hopes to sign it itself.

The simultaneous escalation should not be read as the precursor to failure but as the precondition for progress. Both sides used the first night to impose large-scale long-range strikes, which is the classic shape of talks that begin with fighting: the military situation has to change before the diplomatic one can move. If both sides had gone quiet the moment talks opened, that would have been the more alarming outcome, because it usually means each expects the talks to be cover for continued operations.

The real asymmetry sits in the core issue of security guarantees. Kushner framed the American proposal as creating conditions for a sovereign, secure and prosperous Ukraine "within a more stable European security environment", which treats Europe's security as a precondition rather than a deliverable. Kyiv is asking for the opposite, that any commitment name who acts and how if Russia attacks again. That distance cannot be closed in a single session, and closing it requires a constraint mechanism. Nothing so far suggests Washington is prepared to supply one.

The scarcest resource at the table is the mediator's credit. This administration has spent much of the year on the war with Iran, which has consumed the bandwidth needed to produce a breakthrough, and a mediator who cannot guarantee compliance is no longer a mediator. Adding participants to the room does not repair that, but it does mean Europe no longer has to wait for Washington to announce the result unilaterally.

Four things are worth watching. Whether European officials operate as participants or as observers in the remaining agenda. Whether the Russian team can produce anything beyond procedural language on security guarantees. Whether the United States proposes a mechanism neither side can veto alone. And whether the heavy long-range strikes on the Ukrainian side begin to taper after the second day. The first two determine whether the talks are here to sign or to manage expectations, the third determines whether any outcome can be executed, and the fourth determines what the whole exercise was worth.
