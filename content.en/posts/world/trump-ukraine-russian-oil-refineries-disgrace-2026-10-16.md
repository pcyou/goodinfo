**[Summary]**

U.S. President Donald Trump publicly criticised Ukraine on October 16 for resuming long-range drone strikes on Russian oil refineries, calling the campaign a "disgrace". The comments come as Washington continues to push ceasefire negotiations and as European allies step up support for Ukrainian energy infrastructure, exposing a fresh divergence between the United States and Ukraine over energy warfare, diplomatic tempo and strategic end-state.

**[Event Details]**

- **Strike pattern**: Ukraine has used domestically produced long-range heavy drones to hit refineries and depots along the Volga, in Krasnodar Krai and around Rostov, with several sites experiencing extended outages that have disrupted Russian domestic fuel supply and export cadence.
- **Trump's position**: Trump called the strikes a "disgrace" and suggested the campaign could derail U.S.-led negotiations, urging Kyiv to focus on the negotiating table rather than the battlefield.
- **Ukraine's position**: Ukrainian officials say strikes on Russian energy infrastructure are a necessary means of degrading Russia's war machine and will continue until Moscow demonstrates genuine willingness to negotiate.
- **European position**: Several European governments tacitly support or back the strikes while supplying additional air-defence and long-range strike capability to Kyiv, but warn of risks that Russia could retaliate against European energy assets.
- **Negotiation status**: U.S.-brokered ceasefire talks have again stalled over disputes on Ukrainian territorial status, European security guarantees and war-time reparations, with both Moscow and Kyiv refusing to compromise on core demands.

**[Panoramic Perspective]**

Trump's criticism reveals a mid-term divergence between Washington and Kyiv on the strategic objective of the war. The United States, as Ukraine's most important political, military and financial backer, has long balanced weapons supply and intelligence support with a desire to engineer a limited ceasefire that serves U.S. strategic resource allocation and domestic political agendas. U.S. backing for Ukrainian military operations carries a clear strategic boundary: when Kyiv's actions risk derailing U.S.-led negotiations or triggering direct confrontation with Russia, Washington tends to apply public pressure and even the threat of aid cuts to constrain Ukrainian operations.

The deeper tension is that the energy dimension of the conflict is becoming a key bargaining chip for all sides. Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries have materially reduced Russian export capacity, putting real pressure on Russian fiscal capacity and the war machine, but they have also lifted global and domestic fuel prices and disrupted energy flows through the Red Sea and Black Sea corridors. Energy infrastructure warfare has therefore extended beyond the purely military sphere into economic, financial and diplomatic domains, with both sides using the issue to pressure the other and third parties.

From the global energy market perspective, weakened Russian refining capacity and instability along the Black Sea corridor are reshaping global product trade flows. Asian buyers have increased procurement from Middle Eastern, U.S. and Asian refiners, while Europe is pushing ahead with full decoupling from Russian energy. Global product markets may therefore enter a phase of "regional breakpoints": logistical efficiency between Europe and Asia deteriorates, while Middle Eastern, U.S. and Asian refiners run higher utilisation rates, with regional price differentials widening.
