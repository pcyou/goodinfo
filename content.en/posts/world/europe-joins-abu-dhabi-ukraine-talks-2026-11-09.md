## Key Facts

European officials will attend the US-mediated Russia-Ukraine talks in Abu Dhabi this week, French foreign minister Jean-Noel Barrot said on Sunday, days after the Kremlin insisted there could be no European role in the meeting. The sessions are set for Tuesday and Wednesday and are the fourth round of negotiations between the two warring countries brokered by the Trump administration, and the first three-way round since February. Barrot said France will be there and will ensure Europe's voice is heard, without specifying who would represent Paris or whether European officials would negotiate directly or merely observe. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov had earlier ruled out a European role, saying the trilateral format had already been agreed.

## Event Details

- **The announcement**: France's foreign minister said European officials would attend, days after Moscow insisted no European powers would be involved.
- **Venue and dates**: the meetings take place in Abu Dhabi on Tuesday and Wednesday, previously presented as a three-way session between Washington, Moscow and Kyiv.
- **The Kremlin position**: Peskov said the trilateral format had been agreed and accused European governments of failing to decide who speaks for them, while acknowledging that security talks would at some point require Europe's involvement.
- **The US framing**: President Trump met Kremlin envoy Kirill Dmitriev on Thursday and said a coming agreement was going to involve more than Ukraine and might involve all of Europe.
- **The Ukrainian caution**: President Zelensky told CNN on Friday he had not been given details of the agreement Trump referenced, warning it was not simple and could be dangerous, and invoked the 1945 Yalta conference, where Allied leaders settled Europe's division without the involvement of several affected countries.
- **The precedent**: Moscow confirmed it would return to the table only after US envoy Steve Witkoff met President Putin in St Petersburg in October, where Putin announced the release of an American prisoner from a Russian jail, an apparent Kremlin gesture to Trump's earlier lifting of sanctions on Russian diesel exports; Ukraine had vigorously opposed the fuel sales.
- **The agenda**: Witkoff has said the latest talks will cover postwar security arrangements, de-escalation and humanitarian issues, shortly after Melania Trump helped broker a limited ceasefire that allowed humanitarian aid to enter a town inside Russian-occupied Ukraine.
- **The Russian delegation**: led again by Vladimir Medinsky, a hardline Putin aide present at most talks since the first failed discussions in March 2022, and for the first time joined by deputy foreign ministers Sergei Ryabkov and Mikhail Galuzin, along with Yury Zhigarlovsky, a deputy head of the General Staff's operations directorate, and military intelligence chief Igor Kostyukov.
- **The gap**: Russia continues to demand that Ukraine give up large parts of its territory and renounce NATO membership, while Zelensky insists any security commitments should be spelled out, including who would act and how if Russia attacked again.
- **The outlook**: earlier US-brokered meetings made little progress, and the chances of a breakthrough have narrowed as the administration focused on its war with Iran.

## Comparison of Viewpoints

**France's move is a statement about institutional representation rather than about the substance of any settlement.** Barrot announced that Europe would be present without naming a representative and without specifying whether European officials would negotiate or observe. That formulation is deliberate. Europe has been unable to speak for itself in this format for the entire war, which is precisely the charge Peskov levelled when he said European governments had failed to decide who speaks for them. Announcing attendance without resolving representation lets Paris register European participation while preserving the option of a separate European channel if the talks fail.

**The Kremlin's objection reveals that Moscow treats the format itself as part of the deal.** Peskov's position that the trilateral format was already agreed is not a legal objection but a status objection. If the process is the concession, then adding a fourth party changes what was bargained for rather than what is being discussed. The earlier episode with Russian diesel sanctions, and the prisoner release that followed, illustrates how Moscow has treated procedural linkage as currency: a visible concession purchased a visible gesture.

**The composition of the Russian delegation is the most concrete signal in the story, and it cuts in two directions.** The presence of military intelligence and operations officials suggests Moscow is prepared to discuss questions it has previously avoided. The same individuals, however, are ones the Institute for the Study of War has previously identified as seeking to slow down talks or push harder on Moscow's demands. A more senior roster can mean greater ambition for a deal or a more senior capacity to obstruct one; the roster alone does not distinguish between the two.

**Scepticism about the outcome is widespread and comes from a direction that is usually dismissed.** In an Atlantic Council op-ed, John Herbst, a former US ambassador to Ukraine, argued that Putin had rejected every US proposal Kyiv accepted and preferred participating in peace talks to pursuing actual peace, predicting that the only outcome would be agreement to another round. Zelensky's own framing, invoking Yalta, is a warning about process rather than about Russia's willingness: the danger he describes is that affected countries accept an arrangement made about them.

**The election result may matter more than the format.** Zelensky noted that the Democrats' midterm victories could improve Ukraine's chances of additional US aid. If congressional control shifts in January, the durability of American-brokered diplomacy depends on a Senate and House that may be less committed to the administration's framing, which makes the presence of European participants at the table a hedge rather than a courtesy.

**The humanitarian thread is a modest counterweight to the diplomatic arithmetic.** The limited ceasefire that allowed aid into a town under Russian occupation shows that operational arrangements can move even while the political track stalls. That matters because the credibility of any settlement will rest as much on whether people in occupied territory receive aid as on whether security guarantees are signed. A process that produces no such arrangements, whatever its format, leaves the most concrete measure of progress unchanged.

## Panoramic Analysis

What makes this round worth following closely is not the disagreement over attendance but the fact that a negotiation originally designed as a shuttle mediated by Washington has begun acquiring participants it was not designed for. A three-party format with a single mediator is a structure for delivering terms. A four-party format with an ambiguous fourth party is a structure for discovering whether the others can agree on anything at all before the terms are fixed. Europe entering the room changes the question being answered from whether Russia and Ukraine can be brought to a deal to whether there is anyone left to enforce one.

The representation problem that Peskov raised is genuinely the hardest part. Security arrangements in Europe cannot be built on the basis of one participant speaking for states with divergent interests, and the war has made that visible in the most expensive way. The reference to Yalta that Zelensky invoked is not a historical flourish but a direct charge: at Yalta the division of Europe was settled by the powers over the heads of the countries that lived there. If European capitals are now admitted to the room but do not yet know who will speak for them collectively, the risk is not that Europe is excluded but that Europe is present in a way that reproduces the original exclusion.

The escalation in the Russian delegation should be read against that backdrop. Bringing military intelligence and General Staff operations officials into a negotiation, at the same table as officials with a record of stalling, may reflect a Moscow that wants the talks to produce a document it can present as a success while keeping the underlying security questions unresolved. Alternatively it may reflect genuine readiness. The distinction will show up in the agenda in the first hours rather than in the communique at the end.

There is also a structural shift in the broader picture. The administration's capacity to act as an impartial broker has been consumed elsewhere this year, and the marginal value of diplomacy administered by one distracted party is correspondingly lower. That is the argument for Europe's presence. The counter-argument is that a broker who cannot be relied upon to hold the other side to a commitment is not a broker at all, and adding participants to a table does not repair that. What would repair it is a mechanism that binds, and no such mechanism has been described for this round.

For readers, the concrete indicators are narrow and checkable. Whether European officials negotiate or merely observe tells us whether Europe has been admitted as a party or decorated as a guest. Whether the Russian delegation produces anything beyond process language on security guarantees tells us whether Moscow came to sign or to manage expectations. Whether Zelensky receives the terms before the press does tells us whether the Yalta concern is being addressed. And whether European capital continues to flow at current levels through the winter tells us what any of it is worth. None of these require interpretation, which is convenient, because the temptation to over-read the first day of any talks is strong.
