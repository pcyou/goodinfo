## Trump Administration Plans to Gut Clean Car Rules

### Key Summary

The New York Times reported on Saturday evening that the Trump administration is preparing a sweeping package of measures to weaken U.S. clean car rules. The plan would withdraw the final phase of greenhouse gas emission standards for passenger cars and light trucks set under the Biden administration and would accelerate an effort to revoke California's long-standing waiver to set its own vehicle emissions rules. The rollback is widely viewed as a major retreat for U.S. climate regulation and will reshape the pace at which the domestic auto industry transitions to electrification.

### Event Details

According to three people briefed on the discussions, the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration and the Environmental Protection Agency are drafting new rule proposals expected to be released for public comment as early as next month. The core changes include: first, scrapping the final tightening steps of the Obama- and Biden-era greenhouse gas standards for passenger cars and light trucks; second, using federal legislation or administrative action to limit California's authority under the Clean Air Act to set stricter emissions limits of its own; and third, lowering the ceiling on penalties for non-compliance with emissions rules.

The California Air Resources Board has set a goal of phasing out new gasoline-powered car sales by 2035 and has built a coalition of more than a dozen states that follow its standards. If the federal waiver is revoked, California and the allied states are widely expected to challenge the move in court. Fourteen states, including New York, New Jersey, and Oregon, have committed to following California's zero-emission vehicle roadmap, which means the legal fight will carry significant practical weight.

Reactions within the auto industry are split. Detroit's three major automakers have long complained about rising compliance costs and squeezed margins as they shift toward electric platforms; Tesla and other pure-play EV manufacturers have publicly supported the existing rules. Environmental groups, including the Sierra Club and the Natural Resources Defense Council, have said they will file lawsuits as soon as the new rules are published, with cases that may ultimately reach the Supreme Court. European and Japanese automaker associations have also cautioned that the U.S. unilaterally weakening its standards would undermine global cooperation on climate change.

It is worth noting that the Trump administration is simultaneously pushing proposals to revoke the federal EV tax credit and to restrict state-level funding for charging infrastructure. Market observers believe that, taken together, these moves will noticeably slow the growth of U.S. new energy vehicle sales in the second half of 2026.

### Full Scope Analysis

This policy shift carries significant implications for both the U.S. and the global energy transition. Within the U.S. market, California's waiver has long served as the critical link between federal regulation and state-level climate ambition. Since the waiver was created under the Clean Air Act in 1967, California has used it to drive the nationwide rollout of catalytic converters, zero-emission vehicles, and stricter fuel economy standards. Ending the waiver would consolidate final authority over vehicle emissions at the federal level and substantially weaken state-level climate ambition.

From an industrial perspective, U.S. legacy automakers already felt the uncertainty of policy whiplash during Trump's first-term withdrawal from the Paris Agreement. Layering weakened emissions standards, the removal of EV tax credits, and reduced charging subsidies on top of each other could push U.S. automakers further toward hybrid platforms rather than pure battery-electric platforms, widening the gap with the accelerating electrification push in China and the European Union. In capital markets, electric vehicle supply chain stocks have already begun to price in the regulatory expectation, with several battery and charging-pile companies seeing pullbacks in recent weeks.

On the international stage, the rollback will deepen the tension between U.S. domestic emissions policy and the country's Nationally Determined Contribution commitments under the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change. The European Union's zero-emission vehicle rules and the United Kingdom's 2030 phase-out of new gasoline vehicles both depend on synchronized action by major economies. A continued U.S. retreat would likely slow the global pace of cutting emissions from new passenger cars in half by 2035 and create fresh friction in climate diplomacy.

### Multiple Perspectives

Supporters argue that the existing clean car rules are overly aggressive, impose heavy compliance costs on legacy automakers, and push up new car prices for consumers. The Trump administration has repeatedly said it wants to revive U.S. manufacturing and jobs by reducing regulatory burdens.

Opponents are sharply critical. Environmental groups and corporate climate alliances have pushed back hard. The Sierra Club's executive director has called revoking California's waiver the single largest threat to clean air in fifty years. The Alliance for Automotive Innovation has warned that regulatory whiplash will shake investor confidence and make it harder for automakers to choose between electric and hybrid strategies.

Academic and neutral institutions generally note that once a standard is set, a stable medium- and long-term framework is most important for automakers' R&D cadence, and that frequent adjustments to emissions targets tend to raise the long-term cost of transition. Research from the Pew Research Center and several investment banks shows that, regardless of how the state lawsuits resolve, U.S. new energy vehicle market growth will diverge sharply between 2026 and 2028: states that follow California's standards will keep growing, while states aligned with the new federal rules may enter a plateau.
