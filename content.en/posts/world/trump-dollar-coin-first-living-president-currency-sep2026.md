## Trump $1 Coin Makes Him First Living President on US Currency in a Century

**[Core Summary]** On September 3, 2026, the US Mint officially released a gold-colored $1 coin bearing President Trump's portrait, making him the first living US president to appear on circulating currency since 1926. The move breaks a century-long tradition and has sparked constitutional debate and historical discussion.

### Event Details

According to BBC and The Washington Post, the US Mint on September 3 officially issued a gold-colored one-dollar coin featuring President Trump's profile portrait. This marks the first time since 1926 that a sitting US president's image has appeared on circulating currency.

The US Treasury Secretary stated at the release ceremony that the coin aims to "commemorate the economic achievements accomplished under the current administration." However, the decision has triggered intense political debate in Washington.

Democratic lawmakers quickly criticized the move, calling it a violation of "American democratic traditions." The House Minority Leader posted on social media: "Currency should represent the nation, not an individual. Placing a president's portrait on a coin is a dangerous step toward monarchy."

Constitutional scholars have also entered the debate. A Harvard Law School professor noted that while the law does not explicitly prohibit a sitting president from appearing on currency, the practice contradicts the founding spirit of "avoiding personality cults." "President Washington himself firmly opposed any symbols that could be seen as monarchical."

However, Trump's supporters view this as a symbol of "breaking Washington establishment conventions." A senior White House advisor told Fox News: "The president received a mandate through democratic election. His image appearing on currency is precisely a reflection of democracy."

### Panoramic Analysis

The deeper significance of this coin release lies in its touching a sensitive nerve in American political culture: how to handle leader worship within a democratic framework. The American founding fathers deliberately avoided using sitting leaders' portraits on national symbols like currency and stamps, aiming to prevent the personification of power and uphold the republican principle that "institutions are above individuals."

From a historical perspective, the 1926 precedent — the sesquicentennial commemorative coin bearing Washington's portrait — was historical in nature, not a personal tribute to then-sitting President Coolidge. Therefore, this Trump coin indeed establishes an entirely new precedent.

From a political communication standpoint, this coin has become another symbol of American political polarization. Supporters see it as "the courage to break conventions," while opponents view it as "a sign of democratic backsliding." This starkly opposed interpretation precisely reflects the deep divisions in current American society regarding fundamental political values.

### Multiple Perspectives

**White House Position**: The White House characterizes this as "a celebration of American achievements." The spokesperson stated the coin design went through proper legal approval procedures: "The president has made extraordinary contributions to the nation, and having his image remembered is a reflection of national honor."

**Opposition Party Voice**: Democrats view this as another example of "abuse of power." The Senate Minority Leader stated: "We are witnessing an attempt by a president to place himself above the nation. This coin is not commemoration but self-coronation."

**Constitutional Scholars' Views**: Legal opinions are divided. Some scholars believe this "while not illegal, violates the constitutional spirit"; another camp argues "Congress has the authority to determine currency design, and courts should not intervene in political questions."

**Public Reaction**: Polls show American public opinion is highly divided. About 45% of respondents expressed support, believing "the president deserves to be commemorated"; 48% expressed opposition, believing "this doesn't fit American tradition"; the remainder were uncertain.
