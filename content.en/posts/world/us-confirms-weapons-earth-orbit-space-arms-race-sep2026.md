## Core Summary

BBC reported on September 15 that the US government publicly acknowledged for the first time the deployment of weapons in Earth orbit, with defense analysts warning this move may trigger a new round of global space arms race. This acknowledgment represents the most breakthrough shift in US space policy in recent years and will have profound effects on global satellite communications security, commercial space activities, and the reform of international space legal frameworks.

## Event Details

The US Space Force vice commander confirmed at a routine Pentagon press conference on September 15 that the US has deployed specific types of space combat equipment in Earth orbit, emphasizing that these weapons serve "defensive" purposes to protect critical US and allied space assets. This is the first time the US government has officially acknowledged the deployment of weapons in orbit in public, breaking the decades-long "ambiguity strategy" practice.

According to BBC defense correspondent analysis, the orbital weapons now publicly acknowledged by the US may include directed-energy lasers, microwave weapons, and anti-satellite missiles. Some of these weapons are already capable of destroying low-Earth orbit satellite targets within minutes, while others possess "soft kill" capabilities for jamming or blinding enemy satellite sensors. US Space Force emphasized that all relevant weapons are deployed outside the framework of the "non-weaponized space treaty," aiming to address "space threats" from countries such as China and Russia.

This US move operates in a gray area of the 1967 Outer Space Treaty. Although that treaty prohibits the deployment of weapons of mass destruction on the Moon and other celestial bodies, it does not explicitly prohibit the deployment of conventional weapons in Earth orbit. The international community has long tacitly accepted that "Earth orbit belongs to the weaponizable area," but in practice countries have adopted an "unacknowledged" restraint policy. The US breaking of this practice may trigger explicit and visible space militarization processes in other countries.

In response, the Chinese government expressed "serious concern" through its Foreign Ministry spokesperson, stating that this US move "severely undermines the international consensus on the peaceful use of space." The Russian Ministry of Defense warned that the US "will bear full responsibility for space militarization." The NATO Secretary General stated at a Brussels press conference that he "understands US security concerns" but also called on all parties to "exercise restraint." The EU High Representative for Foreign Affairs and Security Policy called for the early launch of a new round of UN space arms control negotiations.

## Panoramic Analysis

The US first public acknowledgment of weapons deployment in Earth orbit marks a fundamental rupture in the post-Cold War "non-militarization of space" tacit understanding.

From an international law perspective, this US move exposes the limitations of the 1967 Outer Space Treaty. As a product of the Cold War, that treaty only prohibits the deployment of weapons of mass destruction and military bases on the Moon and other celestial bodies, with no explicit restrictions on conventional weapons deployment in Earth orbit. This legal gap provides broad "gray operational space" for space powers such as the US, Russia, and China. After the US publicly breaks the "ambiguity strategy" practice, the international community needs to launch a new round of space arms control negotiations as soon as possible to clarify the red lines and exclusion zones for weaponization of Earth orbit.

From a military strategic perspective, the US public acknowledgment of orbital weapons deployment reflects forward-looking positioning for future high-end warfare. Modern warfare increasingly depends on satellite communications, satellite navigation, and satellite reconnaissance — from cruise missiles to precision-guided bombs, from drone swarms to aircraft carrier battle groups, almost all modern military operations rely on space assets. In the context of intensifying great power competition, ensuring the security of space assets has become the core issue of major military strategies. This US move is both a response to the enhanced anti-satellite capabilities of China and Russia and a strengthening of its own space superiority.

From the commercial space perspective, the parallel acceleration of space militarization and commercialization will have complex effects on the global commercial space industry. On one hand, the space arms race will drive rapid development of space military technology, and partial technology spillovers will benefit civil space; on the other hand, the militarization of Earth orbit will significantly increase operational risks for commercial satellites, the space debris problem will further worsen, and low-Earth orbit may become "unsafe for use" within decades. This pattern will force commercial space companies to invest more resources in satellite protection and orbital maneuvering capabilities.

From the geopolitical perspective, this US move will have significant catalyzing effects on China-Russia space cooperation. China and Russia have conducted deep cooperation in the space field in recent years, including sharing satellite navigation data and joint space situational awareness. Against the backdrop of the US publicly deploying orbital weapons, China-Russia space cooperation may deepen further and may give birth to new space military alliances. This pattern will exacerbate the camp-based trend in global geopolitics and pose new challenges to the global governance system.

From a long-term perspective, the ultimate boundary of the space arms race will be the "mutually assured destruction" balance of terror. When Earth orbit is filled with various weapons and anti-satellite systems, any space strike initiated by one side may cause retaliation against its own space assets. Although this "space balance of terror" may to some extent constrain great power behavior, it will also expose humanity's exploration and use of space to unprecedented systemic risks.

## Multiple Perspectives Comparison

**US government position**: The US Space Force vice commander emphasized at the press conference that the relevant weapons deployment serves "defensive" purposes, designed to protect critical US and allied space assets and address "competitor" space threats from China and Russia. A State Department spokesperson stated in a subsequent statement that the US "remains committed to the peaceful use of space" but "will not stand idle when its space assets face threats."

**Chinese government position**: A Chinese Foreign Ministry spokesperson expressed "serious concern" at the regular press conference on September 15, stating that this US move "seriously violates the international consensus on the peaceful use of space and harms the common interests of all humanity." The spokesperson emphasized in answering questions that China "has always advocated the peaceful use of space, opposed space militarization and the space arms race," and called on the international community to "take concrete action to stop US adventurism."

**Russian government position**: The Russian Ministry of Defense issued a statement through TASS warning that the US "will bear full responsibility for space militarization." The statement emphasized that Russia "reserves the right to take countermeasures" and hinted at potentially accelerating the development of anti-satellite weapons. The director of the Russian Academy of Sciences Space Research Institute publicly commented that this US move "completely opened Pandora's box."

**NATO and EU position**: The NATO Secretary General stated at the Brussels press conference that he "understands US security concerns" but also emphasized that "space militarization should be kept within manageable limits." A NATO spokesperson confirmed that NATO has established a dedicated working group to assess the impact of space militarization on collective security. The EU High Representative for Foreign Affairs and Security Policy called for the early launch of a new round of UN space arms control negotiations and emphasized that "multilateralism is the only viable path to address space security challenges."

**International academia**: The director of the Space Security Project at Harvard Kennedy School warned in a recent article in Foreign Affairs that once the space arms race is launched, "it will be very difficult to reverse," calling on the US government to "conduct open international consultations before deploying new weapons." A senior fellow at the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace believes that space militarization is "already inevitable" and all parties should establish "space norms of behavior" as soon as possible to avoid misjudgments and accidental conflicts.

**Commercial space industry**: Spokespersons for major commercial space companies including SpaceX and Blue Origin remained cautious in their responses, emphasizing that they "will continue to comply with international law and cooperate with governments." Some small satellite operators openly expressed concerns and called for the early establishment of a "space traffic management" mechanism to cope with the increasingly crowded Earth orbit.
