## Core Summary

Germany's Alternative for Germany (AfD) party is leading in polls ahead of upcoming state elections, poised to potentially win governing power at the state level for the first time. This outcome marks a significant shift in Germany's post-war political landscape, as far-right forces achieve a breakthrough at the regional level. Analysts note this trend reflects deeper social drivers behind Europe's political rightward shift and poses a serious challenge to mainstream parties' coalition strategies.

## Event Details

According to NPR reporting, recent polls show AfD support in Thuringia has reached historic highs, leading traditional mainstream parties. The party, built on anti-immigration, anti-EU, and Eurosceptic platforms, has continuously expanded its voter base in recent years. If AfD successfully forms a government in this election, it would become the first far-right-led state government in German history.

Germany's current chancellor has stated that mainstream parties will not form a coalition with AfD, a "firewall" position supported by both the CDU and SPD. However, if AfD wins a plurality of seats, the resulting impasse could trigger a constitutional crisis. Political observers note that German parties have yet to form a unified strategy for handling AfD's rise, with internal divisions persisting.

## Panoramic Analysis

AfD's rise is not an isolated phenomenon but a microcosm of Europe's broader political rightward shift. In recent years, parties like France's National Rally, Italy's Brothers of Italy, and the Netherlands' Freedom Party have performed strongly in elections, reflecting voter dissatisfaction with traditional establishment parties. This dissatisfaction stems primarily from three factors: cultural identity anxieties triggered by immigration, class divisions brought by economic globalization, and sovereignty disputes in EU integration.

For Germany, AfD governance would bring multiple impacts. First, the state's immigration policies could tighten significantly, affecting local labor markets and multicultural ecosystems. Second, AfD's Eurosceptic stance may conflict with the federal government's EU policies, exacerbating Germany's internal divisions within the EU. More profoundly, this could break Germany's post-war political consensus that "far-right parties should not govern," providing legitimacy demonstration for similar parties across Europe.

## Multiple Perspectives

**AfD Supporter Position**: Party supporters argue AfD represents the voice of "forgotten Germans," particularly in eastern regions where voters feel uneasy about immigration and dissatisfied with the economy. They advocate restoring national sovereignty, strict border control, and opposing excessive EU intervention.

**Mainstream Party Position**: CDU, SPD, Greens, and FDP all refuse cooperation with AfD, calling its platform "contrary to German fundamental values." However, this firewall strategy faces criticism for avoiding voter concerns and potentially causing mainstream parties to lose further public support.

**International Observer Position**: European political analysts note AfD's rise reflects democratic systems' dilemma when facing populist challenges. On one hand, excluding far-right parties may be seen as "anti-democratic"; on the other, cooperating with them may legitimize extreme positions. Balancing democratic inclusion with value baselines has become Europe's core political challenge.

**German Public Reaction**: Polls show German public attitudes toward AfD's rise are highly divided. Western residents generally express concern, while some eastern voters identify with AfD's "anti-establishment" stance. This regional divide reflects long-standing economic and social gaps remaining after German reunification.
