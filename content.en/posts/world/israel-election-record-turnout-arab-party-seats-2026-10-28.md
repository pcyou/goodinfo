## Key Facts

- Voting in Israel's parliamentary election reached a turnout of 77.7 percent, the highest in more than two decades.
- Arab citizens are around 20 percent of Israel's population, and some Arab voters told CBS News that life under a hard-right government had been difficult, prompting a record turnout for that minority community.
- Exit polls showed the opposition bloc led by Gadi Eisenkot ahead of the prime minister's camp by 52 seats to 50. Neither side had a clear path to forming a government.
- The Likud party was still projected to emerge as the largest single party in the 120-seat parliament, even though the bloc it leads was behind.
- Israel's two Arab parties won more seats in parliament than in any previous election.
- Both Netanyahu and the parties opposing him vowed not to form a government reliant on Arab parties, arguing it would endanger the country if they gained enough seats to influence its conduct in multiple conflicts.
- Breaking that pre-election vow is a common occurrence in Israeli politics, and if the opposition breaks it, it could remove Netanyahu from office.
- This was Israel's first general election since the October 7, 2023 attacks, and came after the multifront war in Gaza, Lebanon and Syria and two direct rounds of conflict with Iran.
- The cost of the eight-month Iran war has risen to an estimated 47 billion dollars, according to an estimate from the US military's Central Command obtained by CBS News. The bulk of that, 29 billion, is for replacing expended munitions.
- A September report from the Congressional Budget Office estimated the war could cost roughly 2 to 3 billion dollars per month for as long as it continues.

## Competing Views

The logic on one side rests entirely on the security issue. The argument is that the positions of Arab parties on war and defence would constrain Israel's room to act, and that this is why they cannot be admitted to coalition talks. That position is consistent with the central promise made during the campaign.

The opposition bloc argues that the main opposing force has already been given a mandate by most voters, and that the deadlock does not come from a split vote but from the sitting prime minister's refusal to compromise with the opposition before the election. On that reading the deadlock is procedural, and procedural deadlocks can be resolved by procedural means.

A third-party view is colder. Each side holds a card that would prevent the other from forming a government, and neither has chosen to play anything beyond that card. What may actually decide the outcome is the precise distribution of Arab party seats, because it determines how many partners each bloc needs and how high the political price becomes if the pre-election promise is broken.

As for the cost figures, their effect is to turn the phrase that this election is about a war from rhetoric into an account. Forty-seven billion dollars over eight months, with twenty-nine billion of that spent replacing expended munitions, and a running cost of two to three billion a month, will all land in the next government's budget. Any coalition platform that promises reconstruction of housing and infrastructure meets this arithmetic first.

## Panoramic Analysis

What is worth recording about this election is not how deadlocked the outcome was, but that three records appeared at once and pointed at the same dead end.

The first is the turnout of 77.7 percent. Eight months of war with Iran, active fronts and domestic insecurity pushed voters to the polls at a level not seen in more than twenty years. High turnout is usually read as a healthy sign, but it also raises the cost of bargaining. When votes concentrate in two opposed blocs rather than spreading across a spectrum, the room for trade disappears. In a three-way contest, a decisive result produces rigidity rather than flexibility, and a government assembled from that result will have to govern for the full four years rather than review itself midway.

The second is the combination of record Arab party seats and a pre-commitment to exclude them. A community making up roughly one-fifth of the population turned out at historic levels, delivered the most seats in its history, and was told by both leading camps that those seats would not count toward forming a government. For a minority that had just lived through a hard-right administration, growing the vote while being locked out of power creates a political debt. Whoever forms the next government will have to settle it at a higher price than the previous one paid, and the settlement cannot be deferred indefinitely because the demographic arithmetic does not improve with time.

The third calculation happens on the battlefield rather than on the ballot. Forty-seven billion dollars over eight months, with 29 billion of that spent replacing expended munitions, plus a running cost of 2 to 3 billion a month, means the incoming government inherits a budget already reshaped by a war. Israel's fiscal space was already compressed by post-war reconstruction, and a discretionary war turned a choice into a fait accompli. That is the real subject of this election. Not who governs, but who pays. The arithmetic of the coalition arithmetic is solvable, because deadlocks in three-way systems always are; the arithmetic of a war bill already paid for is not.
