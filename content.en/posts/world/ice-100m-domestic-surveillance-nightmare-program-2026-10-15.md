**[Core Summary]**
A leaked report reveals that US Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) is planning a roughly USD 100 million domestic surveillance system internally described as a "nightmare" program. The plan includes facial recognition, social media monitoring and cross-database linkage, and has drawn sharp criticism from privacy advocates, immigrant communities and members of both parties in Congress, becoming the latest flashpoint in America's ongoing debate over domestic surveillance and civil liberties.

**[Event Details]**
- **Project Overview**: According to internal documents disclosed by The American Prospect, ICE is planning a domestic surveillance initiative codenamed in connection with ERO-ONE, with an estimated cost of about USD 100 million, integrating facial recognition, social media monitoring, vehicle and license plate recognition, and cross-departmental database linkage.
- **Stated Capability**: The documents describe the system as "nightmare"-grade, suggesting monitoring capabilities well beyond existing immigration enforcement tools and potentially extending to a much broader slice of the US population's daily movements and online activities.
- **Driving Context**: The plan comes amid sustained pressure and deep politicization of US border and immigration enforcement, and aligns with a broader trend of federal agencies purchasing commercial facial recognition and social media monitoring tools at scale.
- **Congressional and Judicial Response**: Some members of Congress have demanded that ICE provide details on the project's scope, budget sources and oversight mechanisms, while the Department of Justice and privacy oversight bodies are evaluating whether it complies with the Fourth Amendment and federal privacy statutes.
- **Public Reaction**: Immigrant communities, minority groups and civil liberties organizations fear the system could be used for mass social control and have called for legislative and judicial constraints.

**[Panoramic Analysis]**
**First**, if deployed, this project would push US domestic surveillance capabilities to a new level. Integrating facial recognition, social media monitoring and vehicle plus license plate recognition means that federal law enforcement will simultaneously obtain individual trajectories, social relationships and behavioral profiles in physical and digital spaces. Such a "panoramic" surveillance capability would significantly compress ordinary citizens' anonymity in public space and pose a serious challenge to the privacy expectations protected by the Fourth Amendment.

**Second**, while ICE's enforcement targets are immigrant populations, once the technology is deployed it has no natural boundary. Facial recognition, social media analytics and cross-database linkage have strong spillover effects and could be repurposed for other law enforcement scenarios, or even accessed by local agencies, commercial platforms and private contractors without adequate judicial oversight. History shows many surveillance technologies have followed a path of "first used on specific groups, then spreading to society at large," and this project carries similar risk.

**Third**, the project will further intensify the federal-state tug-of-war over surveillance authority. In recent years, California, New York and other states have placed restrictions on facial recognition and social media monitoring tools, and many cities have banned government agencies from using such technologies. If ICE drives a federal-level domestic surveillance system, it is likely to trigger a new round of federal preemption lawsuits and force Congress to legislate on authorization, oversight and data retention.

**Fourth**, from a global perspective, US domestic surveillance expansion will affect cross-border tech companies' compliance and product strategies. Data protection authorities in the European Union, the United Kingdom and Canada have long paid attention to the legal framework for US government data access. If ICE's domestic surveillance project is implemented, cross-border data transfer compliance friction will intensify further and may push some countries to reassess cooperation mechanisms with US intelligence and law enforcement agencies.

**Multiple Perspectives**
- **ICE and Supporters**: Emphasize that the project targets illegal immigration and transnational criminal organizations, helps fill existing enforcement blind spots, and will come with judicial oversight and privacy protections.
- **Civil Liberties Organizations**: Argue that the project lacks transparency and judicial oversight, carries risks of mass social surveillance, and may violate the Fourth Amendment and federal privacy statutes.
- **Bipartisan Members of Congress**: Some lawmakers have demanded disclosure of project details and budget sources, while others support stronger immigration enforcement tools; the dispute centers on oversight rather than the project's existence.
- **State and Local Governments**: California, New York and other states have already restricted facial recognition and similar monitoring tools and may trigger a new round of authority disputes and litigation at the federal level.
- **International Data Protection Authorities**: Closely monitor the impact of US domestic surveillance expansion on cross-border data transfer agreements, with EU and UK data protection authorities likely to require stronger compliance reviews.
