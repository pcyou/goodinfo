## Core Summary

The US and Iran have separately engaged multiple mediators in recent days to revive indirect talks aimed at ending the prolonged military confrontation. The new mediation effort comes amid escalating regional tensions, though neither side has publicly acknowledged direct dialogue.

## Event Details

According to Reuters, Washington and Tehran have communicated via intermediary countries through separate channels over the past several days. No specific list of mediators has been released, but regional diplomatic circles generally view Oman, Qatar, and Switzerland as likely key players.

A US State Department spokesperson said at a regular press briefing that the US still seeks a diplomatic resolution to differences with Iran, but stressed that any agreement must address core issues including Iran's nuclear program, ballistic missile development, and regional proxy networks. Iran's foreign ministry reiterated that Tehran will not negotiate under pressure, but did not entirely close the door to diplomatic engagement.

The mediation effort comes against a backdrop of recent maritime incidents linked to Iran in the Middle East, growing international concerns about navigation safety in the Strait of Hormuz, and continued domestic economic pressures in Iran including currency depreciation and inflation.

## Full Picture

This US-Iran indirect contact illuminates multiple tensions in Middle East power dynamics.

First, **indirect diplomacy returns to center stage**. With direct dialogue facing political obstacles, third-party mediation has become the main vehicle for US-Iran communication. If this pattern continues, it may give rise to a more mature multi-tiered intermediary system — but it also adds complexity and unpredictability due to mediators' own interests.

Second, **Strait of Hormuz navigation safety is the key issue**. About one-fifth of global oil shipments transit the Strait of Hormuz, and the waterway's security directly affects global energy markets and the world economy. Any US-Iran progress will be anchored on this issue, since neither side can bear the economic and military consequences of a complete shutdown.

Third, **Iran's domestic economic pressure provides negotiation leverage**. Iran's currency has depreciated sharply in recent years, inflation is high, and living standards are strained. This internal pressure both tests the regime's resilience against external sanctions and serves as a real driver for diplomatic breakthroughs. Historically, Iran has tended to seek negotiations for sanctions relief under severe economic pressure.

Fourth, **the nuclear issue remains the core obstacle**. The US insists any agreement must include durable constraints on Iran's nuclear program, while Iran seeks sanctions relief while preserving enrichment capability. This structural divide has persisted across multiple negotiation rounds and is the key observation point for assessing whether the current mediation can achieve breakthrough.

Fifth, **regional proxy network issues are increasingly bundled in**. The US in recent years has folded Iran's regional proxy networks — including Hezbollah, Houthis, Iraqi Shia militias — into the negotiation framework. This expands the agenda dramatically but reduces the prospects for a comprehensive agreement.

## Comparison of Viewpoints

**US position**: The US government publicly emphasized that diplomacy is the preferred path to resolve Iran issues, while saying it "will not repeat past failed agreements." This suggests the US may seek a broader, more restrictive new framework rather than simply reviving the 2015 JCPOA.

**Iran position**: Iranian officials on one hand reiterate that Tehran will not yield to external pressure; on the other hand, actual engagement keeps a negotiation window open. Recent statements from Supreme Leader Khamenei reflect a complex attitude toward negotiation prospects.

**Gulf states position**: Mediators such as Oman and Qatar tend to push for low-profile diplomacy to advance US-Iran contact, hoping to prevent further regional escalation that would damage their own interests.

**Israeli position**: Israel is highly wary of US-Iran negotiations, concerned that any agreement may loosen constraints on Iran's nuclear program and regional activities. Israeli officials have repeatedly stated recently that Iran's nuclear capability constitutes an "existential threat."

**International energy markets**: Analysts note that even if negotiations achieve initial progress, the navigation risk premium for the Strait of Hormuz will persist — sensitivity of oil prices to regional dynamics cannot be fundamentally changed in the short term.
