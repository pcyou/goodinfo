## Core Summary

The US State Department has formally announced the removal of Syria from the "State Sponsors of Terrorism" list. This decision means the US formally recognizes the government led by Syria's new president Ahmed al-Sharaa. Al-Sharaa's previous links to al-Qaeda make this diplomatic pivot highly controversial. The move marks a major adjustment in Middle East geopolitics that could reshape the post-civil war regional order.

## Key Details

### A Dramatic Transformation from Enemy to Ally

Ahmed al-Sharaa, formerly known as Abu Mohammed al-Julani, was once a core member of al-Qaeda in Iraq. During the Syrian civil war, his Hayat Tahrir al-Sham (HTS) group toppled the Assad regime. After months of engagement, the Trump administration has decided to embrace this former militant as a key figure for Syrian stability.

The State Department stated that Syria's new government has shown "genuine commitment to combating terrorism" and pledged not to allow Syrian territory to be used for attacks against the US or its allies.

### Regional Impact

The decision has far-reaching implications for the Middle East. Israel has expressed "serious concern," worried the new Syrian government may maintain anti-Israel positions. Saudi Arabia and Turkey have cautiously welcomed the move, seeing it as beneficial for Syrian reconstruction and regional stability.

Iran and Russia strongly oppose the decision. Iran's foreign ministry called it "condoning terrorism," while Russia said the US "sacrificed principles for geopolitical interests."

### Domestic Syrian Reaction

Reactions within Syria are mixed. Some citizens welcome US recognition, hoping it will bring economic aid and reconstruction investment. However, human rights organizations express concern, noting the al-Sharaa government's record of rights violations against minority groups during the transition.

## Analysis

The removal of Syria from the terrorism sponsors list is a classic case of "realist diplomacy." It reflects deeper changes in Middle East geopolitics:

Post-Assad Syria needs international acceptance and economic reconstruction support. US recognition will open channels for international financial institution loans and foreign investment, crucial for war-devastated Syria.

The decision also reflects the Trump administration's consistent "transactional diplomacy" style — prioritizing practical interests over ideology. However, this approach carries moral risks: embracing a leader once linked to al-Qaeda may signal to other armed groups that "violence can buy legitimacy."

## Perspectives

**US Government**: The State Department says this is "a decision based on the Syrian government's concrete actions," emphasizing specific counter-terrorism measures.

**Israel**: The foreign ministry expressed "serious concern," demanding the US ensure Syria's new government won't threaten Israeli security.

**Human Rights Groups**: Amnesty International notes "serious moral and legal issues in dealing with a former al-Qaeda associate."

**Middle East Analysts**: Multiple experts call the decision "pragmatic but not without risk," saying Syria's stability ultimately depends on whether the new government achieves inclusive governance.
