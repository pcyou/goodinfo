[Core Summary]

According to CBS News and NBC News on Oct. 17, attacks on commercial vessels in and near the Strait of Hormuz have hit a "record high" in recent weeks, with at least two cargo ships sunk. The reports note that the rising attack frequency contrasts with limited US responses, putting significant pressure on shipping insurance rates and global oil prices. The Strait of Hormuz carries roughly one-fifth of global oil shipments, and the security of this corridor is directly tied to the energy supply stability of several major economies.

[Event Details]

CBS News rolling coverage, citing multiple shipping data platforms, reported that the number of attacks on commercial vessels in and near the Strait of Hormuz had reached a "record high" in recent weeks. The attacks have taken multiple forms, including small boats approaching to conduct on-board inspections and seizures, projectiles causing damage to hulls from afar, and certain vessels being fined as they approached the Strait.

NBC News, in its follow-up coverage, pointed out that some of these attacks have resulted in at least two cargo ships sinking near the Strait. The report, citing shipping insurance and intelligence data, noted that the frequency of interdictions or attacks on commercial vessels transiting the Strait has risen sharply in recent weeks, while the US military response to any single incident has been relatively limited, taking mainly the form of escort operations and intelligence sharing rather than direct strikes against attackers. NBC News analysis argued that this "low-intensity, persistent" mode of confrontation is eroding confidence in shipping operations and pushing up the implicit cost of global energy transport.

The reports also noted that several major global maritime insurance lines have raised premiums for transits through the Strait of Hormuz over the past period and added extra war-risk clauses for certain vessel types. Multiple shipping analysts told Reuters that the change will feed directly into crude-oil transport costs and global refined-product prices, and that some refineries dependent on the Strait for crude deliveries have already begun considering alternative routes or stockpiling strategies.

[Multi-Party Viewpoints]

Western governments and multiple militaries stressed that attacks on commercial vessels violate international law and must be stopped. The US military said it would continue to maintain escort operations and intelligence sharing with international partners in the waters, and called for clearer diplomatic and economic pressure on attackers. Some European and Asian countries called for the swift launch of a new round of multilateral maritime security dialogue to prevent unilateral action from intensifying regional tensions.

The shipping and insurance industries have taken a more pragmatic stance. Multiple industry associations and insurers said they would charge higher premiums for transits through the Strait based on real-time risk assessments, and issue "not recommended" guidance for some high-risk time windows and segments. Some shipowners have already rerouted some voyages that originally planned to go through the Strait to alternative routes, but those alternatives are at a disadvantage in distance, capacity and cost and cannot fully replace the Strait's central role.

Iran has repeatedly reiterated the "legitimacy" of its operations in the relevant waters, accusing foreign warships deployed in the area of escalating tensions, and stressed that its actions are directed at "forces that threaten national security." This posture has made it difficult for the international community to rapidly reduce the attack frequency through diplomatic means in the short term.

[Panoramic Perspective]

From a broader perspective, the "record-high" attacks in the Strait of Hormuz are not isolated security incidents but a tangible manifestation of the Middle East geopolitical landscape layered onto the vulnerabilities of global energy supply chains. Over the past decade, contests over energy corridors, market share and regional influence have repeatedly projected themselves into this critical waterway in different forms; and the combination of rising attack frequency and the relatively restrained US response is pushing the global energy market into a new "low-intensity, high-premium" state.

The deeper impact lies in the readjustment of global energy supply chains. If the attack frequency continues to rise in the short term, some crude buyers may be forced to expand stockpiles and shoulder higher costs for shipping insurance and alternative routes, costs that will eventually pass through to consumer fuel prices. Conversely, if the situation eases, the accumulated risk premium will gradually release. In the short term, what global energy markets must respond to is not just the direct impact of any single attack, but a new normal of "corridor risk becoming routine" — meaning that each country's choices on energy security strategy, reserve policy and diplomatic path will directly shape the band of global energy price swings over the coming years.
