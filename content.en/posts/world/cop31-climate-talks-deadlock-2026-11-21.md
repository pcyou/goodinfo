## Core Summary

Negotiations at the COP31 UN climate summit in Turkey are in deadlock after running through the night, as countries remain unable to agree on a new global climate deal. A group of nations led by France and Panama pushed back against the draft agreement which included no reference to reducing the world's demand for fossil fuels.

## Event Details

Ministers from island states, Panama, and a strong contingent of EU nations including Spain, France, Belgium, Sweden and Finland have said they are pushing to get a commitment to move away from oil, gas and coal back into the final agreement after fossil fuel producers led by Saudi Arabia pushed for its removal.

Panama's minister for the environment Juan Carlos Navarro stated firmly: "It is not acceptable to have a COP or a cover decision that does not even mention fossil fuels and we simply will not put up with it." France's Minister of Ecological Transition Monique Barbut echoed this position, criticizing multiple references in the text to carbon capture, use and storage technology as a license for other countries to keep burning oil and gas.

## Panoramic Perspective

The stalemate at COP31 reflects deep contradictions in global climate governance. On one hand, developing countries and small island states most severely affected by climate change urgently need clear emission reduction commitments and financial support. On the other hand, fossil fuel exporting countries are attempting to protect their economic interests and resist radical energy transition policies.

Notably, the agreement officially known as the "Antalya Accords" acknowledges for the first time that the goal of keeping global temperature rise below 1.5C has been missed. The world is entering what scientists warn is an "overshoot" period, meaning millions more people will be exposed to sea level rise and extreme heat.

Australia's climate change and energy minister Chris Bowen, who is leading negotiations, argued that this COP was about implementation and should focus on the move towards clean energy sources. It is understood that one proposal is to keep fossil fuels off the table in exchange for Saudi Arabia and other petro-states signing up to a pledge for 35% of their power to come from electricity by 2035.

However, this approach does not solve another key issue raised by developing nations - the lack of money. Earlier versions of the text included an agreement to ramp up money for adaptation to $120bn by 2035, but that has now been removed. Ghana's Environment Minister and chair of the African Group of negotiators Zanetor Agyeman Rawlings stated bluntly: "We did not come here to beg we came here to claim what is rightfully ours for our people who are suffering the brunt of anthropogenic activities of developed countries."

## Multiple Perspectives

**Countries supporting retention of fossil fuel clauses** argue that a climate agreement without mentioning the cause is ineffective. One lead negotiator expressed sympathy: "How can you have a climate agreement with no mention of the cause?"

**Opponents**, represented by Australia, advocate focusing on implementation pathways for clean energy transition, arguing that excessive entanglement in fossil fuel wording would hinder substantive progress.

**The developing country bloc** emphasizes the importance of funding commitments, pointing out that financial support for climate adaptation should not be weakened. They demand that developed countries fulfill their historical responsibilities and provide sufficient financial assistance.

**Divisions also exist within the EU**. While France, Spain and others actively push for a hardline stance, some EU countries declined to join the "rebellion," believing it would undermine the negotiation atmosphere.
