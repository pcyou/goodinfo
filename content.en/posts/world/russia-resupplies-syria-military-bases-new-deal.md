## Core Summary

Al Jazeera reported on September 15 that Russia has, for the first time under the new military-cooperation framework agreed with Syria's transitional government in late 2025, shipped equipment and supplies to several long-used military bases inside Syria. The move is viewed as a key signal that the new agreement has moved into substantive execution, and as a concrete step by Russia to recalibrate its military footprint in the Middle East and re-confirm the boundaries of its cooperation with the transitional government.

## Event Details

**First resupply under the new framework**: According to the report, in mid-September Russia used a combination of sea and land transport to deliver maintenance supplies, communications equipment, barracks materials and certain non-lethal military items to several long-used Russian posts along Syria's coast and in its central regions. This is the first planned resupply carried out under the terms of the new agreement since it was signed at the end of 2025.

**Core content of the new framework**: Under the framework concluded at the end of 2025, Russia and the transitional government agreed on the use of military bases, equipment maintenance, the status of Russian technical personnel, access to ports and airfields, and the jurisdictional arrangements that govern them. The agreement clearly defines the facilities Russia may continue to use inside Syria, the duration of that use, and the legal and logistical guarantees provided by the transitional government for Russian personnel and assets. It replaces the more opaque arrangements of the Assad era.

**Calculations inside the transitional government**: Multiple political currents within the transitional government have been cautious in their public comments on the resupply, stressing that the operation is fully within the new framework's legal scope and that "external balancing" remains the government's strategic principle in regional affairs. In a brief statement on September 15, the transitional government's press office reiterated that the framework is the legal basis for military cooperation with all external partners.

**Russia's strategic intent**: Russian and Middle East analysts argue that the resupply is designed to consolidate Russia's long-term military presence on the eastern Mediterranean coast and prevent the loss of access to key facilities such as Tartus and Hmeimim following the Syrian political transition. Russia is also seen as aiming to preserve its political influence in the Middle East and to keep a stable foothold in its relationships with Turkey, the Gulf states and Iran.

**Regional and international reactions**: Turkey, Iran and several Gulf states have kept a low profile while watching the move closely. Turkey reiterated the importance of preserving Syria's sovereignty and territorial integrity. Iran stressed that the transitional government should take the legitimate concerns of regional parties into account. A US State Department spokesperson said on September 15 that Washington is monitoring the developments closely and hopes that any foreign military presence will be consistent with international law and Syrian sovereignty.

## Full Perspective

The first substantive resupply under the new framework marks a landmark moment in the transition of Russia-Syria military relations from the Assad era to the post-transition period, with multiple deep implications for the Middle East geopolitical landscape.

**The Russia-Syria military relationship enters a "legalised" phase.** During the Assad era, Russia's military presence in Syria rested largely on bilateral political understandings and high-level communication, with no clear legal or institutional framework. After the political transition, the relationship has faced pressure to evolve from a "special partnership" into a "rule-based cooperation". The signing of the new framework and its first substantive execution means that Russia's presence in Syria now rests on an explicit legal basis, with defined facilities and jurisdictional arrangements. This will increase predictability for both sides and reduce the risk of a sudden disruption in military cooperation triggered by political turbulence.

**A key pillar of Russia's Middle East strategy is preserved.** The Tartus naval facility and the Hmeimim air base are Russia's two most important military installations on the eastern Mediterranean coast, and serve as strategic springboards for power projection across the Middle East and North Africa. If Russia were to lose access to these facilities, it would not only lose a naval logistics and operational node in the Mediterranean but also weaken its leverage in talks with Turkey, Iran and the Gulf states. The resupply sends a clear signal to regional players: Russia is not going to substantively withdraw from Syria, but will continue to maintain a critical military presence under the new framework.

**The transitional government's "external balancing" strategy gains concrete form.** Since coming to power, the transitional government has faced competing demands from Turkey, the Gulf states, the United States and Russia. "External balancing" — pursuing deeper cooperation with one external partner without becoming overly dependent on any single one — has been the guiding principle of its diplomacy. The resupply takes place within the Russian framework, but the transitional government simultaneously emphasises that this is a routine arrangement under a legal agreement and that it applies the same standards to all external partners. This stance meets the political need to cooperate with Russia while avoiding the impression that the government is tilting back toward Moscow.

**Potential implications for regional counter-terrorism and maritime corridor security.** Residual extremist groups inside Syria, cross-border smuggling networks and broader maritime security threats are challenges shared by Russia, Syria, Turkey and the Gulf states. A stable Russian military presence inside Syria has significant value for Russian counter-terrorism intelligence, monitoring of maritime corridors and coordination with regional security mechanisms. After this resupply, cooperation between Russia and the transitional government on intelligence sharing, maritime security and cross-border coordination is expected to become more institutionalised, with substantive effects on the regional security architecture.

**Indirect effects on the Russia-US and Russia-EU contest in the Middle East.** Russia's stable presence in Syria is one of its most important assets in its Middle East contest with the United States and the European Union. Washington and Brussels have long wanted to see that presence weakened or subjected to tighter constraints. The continued implementation of the new framework means that the Russia-US and Russia-EU contest in the Middle East will persist, extending from this Syrian node into the eastern Mediterranean, the Levant and North Africa.

## Multi-perspective Comparison

The **Russian Ministry of Defence and foreign-policy apparatus** describes the resupply as a "routine arrangement" under the new framework, emphasising that the cooperation is fully consistent with international law and Syrian sovereignty, and signalling that Russia's strategic presence in the Middle East will not be shaken by external pressure. The **Syrian transitional government** stresses the legal-framework nature of the operation, reiterates that "external balancing" is the core of its diplomatic strategy, and reaffirms that cooperation with all external partners must serve Syria's national interests. The **Turkish government** reiterates the importance of preserving Syria's territorial integrity and sovereignty, implying that it expects any foreign military presence and activity to fit within the unified framework for Syria that Turkey supports. **Iran** emphasises that the transitional government should consider the legitimate concerns of regional players and avoid any unilateral military cooperation that would undermine regional stability. The **US State Department** says it is monitoring developments closely and hopes any foreign military presence will be consistent with international law and Syrian sovereignty. The **EU High Representative for Foreign and Security Policy** stresses that any military activity inside Syria should aim at a political settlement, Syrian sovereignty and regional stability.
