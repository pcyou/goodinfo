**[Key Facts]** Senior Iranian military officials recently publicly stated that they will accelerate plans to expand missile range, even as multi-round indirect talks with the United States have reached an impasse. The statement is widely seen as Tehran's tough response to sustained US pressure and further complicates the Middle East security picture.

**[What We Know]**

- **Military statement**: Senior commanders from the Islamic Revolution Guard Corps and the regular army both said that missile range expansion is a "defensive national interest" and has "entered the implementation phase"
- **Talks progress**: According to multiple media reports, this round of indirect talks focused on limiting uranium enrichment levels and the missile program, but the two sides have not reached agreement on key issues
- **Regional reaction**: Both Israel and Saudi Arabia expressed "grave concern" over Iran's statement; Gulf states called for diplomatic resolution of differences
- **International context**: UN Security Council discussions on the Iran missile issue have again picked up activity, and several European countries are mulling a new round of sanctions

**[Wider Perspective]**

**First**, Iran's announcement at this moment is both a genuine reflection of its technical capability and a political response to Washington's maximum-pressure tactics. With no substantive breakthrough in talks, the missile issue is being front-loaded as leverage to raise the threshold for entering future negotiations.

**Second**, from a strategic security angle, any substantive expansion of Iran's missile range would directly affect threat assessments for Israel, US Gulf bases, and parts of eastern Europe, forcing relevant countries to revisit their defense deployments and early-warning mechanisms.

**Third**, for global energy markets, every escalation of Middle East tensions is transmitted through oil price volatility and geopolitical risk premium channels. Layered on OPEC+'s recent decision to maintain output, the near-term downside for oil prices is further constrained.

**Fourth**, the negotiating window is not closed. Iranian officials still preserve diplomatic channels, and Washington has not publicly refused engagement, but mutual trust levels have fallen significantly. Any breakthrough will require third-party mediation or substantial interest-swap arrangements rather than narrow topic-by-topic negotiation.

**Fifth**, in the medium-to-long term, the missile issue is linked with the Strait of Hormuz issue. Iran's parliament speaker has already stated that the strait will not reopen until conditions are met, which means any negotiation must package the missile, enrichment, and strait tracks together.

**[Viewpoints Compared]**

- **Senior IRGC commanders**: The missile program is a "non-negotiable sovereign right," and range expansion is a necessary defensive measure
- **Iranian foreign minister**: Still emphasizes that negotiation is the "preferred option" for resolving differences; military options are not the first choice
- **US State Department**: Expresses "strong concern" over Iran's latest statement, hinting at the possibility of a new round of sanctions
- **Israeli Ministry of Defense**: Will closely track Iran's missile progress and adjust defense deployments if necessary
- **Gulf states**: Call on all parties to exercise restraint and resolve tensions through diplomatic channels
