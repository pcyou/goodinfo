Axios reports that Saudi Arabia is planning a major military offensive against Yemen's Houthis, aimed at retaking key western Yemeni territory held by the group and decisively breaking its stranglehold on Red Sea shipping lanes. Multiple outlets indicate Saudi Arabia plans to commit roughly 100,000 troops to the operation, suggesting a significantly larger scope than previous rounds.

Notably, according to Axios, the United States has for now opted not to join the operation directly. This posture contrasts with the long-standing pattern of US intelligence, aerial refueling, and weapons support for Saudi operations in Yemen, and reflects Washington's complex balancing between Middle East proxy conflicts and global maritime security: on one hand, the Houthis' continued disruption of Red Sea commercial traffic is pushing up global shipping costs and energy prices, harming multiple economies; on the other hand, Washington appears to want to avoid being drawn back into large-scale ground warfare and is shifting more of the burden onto regional allies.

Red Sea shipping is critical to global energy and commodity flows, and any sustained intensification of threats to the waterway will further raise international marine insurance premiums and energy prices, eventually feeding through supply chains to consumers worldwide. If Saudi Arabia's operation proceeds smoothly, it could become an important turning point for Red Sea security in recent years; if it stalls or drags on, it may further entrench the current disruption of the shipping corridor.
