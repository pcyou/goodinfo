## Core Summary

CBS News reported that Cuba's national power grid suffered another large-scale collapse on 18 September, leaving millions of residents across the country without electricity. It is the sixth nationwide large-scale grid failure of 2026. The Cuban government activated emergency response procedures, prioritising hospitals and water supply systems, but progress on restoring residential power has been slow. The incident has once again placed Cuba's long-standing problems of ageing energy infrastructure, external sanctions pressure and foreign exchange shortages under international scrutiny, and has prompted broad regional discussion about how small economies can survive in an era of energy transition.

## Event Details

According to CBS News, the grid collapse occurred on the afternoon of 18 September local time. The trigger was equipment failure at a major power plant in the east of the country, which set off a chain reaction that destabilised the national grid within minutes. The power regulator activated a load-shedding scheme within hours, but in several provinces restoration took longer than 24 hours, and some rural areas remained without power for several consecutive days.

Cuban officials acknowledged at a subsequent press conference that this is the sixth nationwide large-scale grid collapse of 2026, with similar-scale incidents occurring on average roughly every two months, a noticeably higher frequency than in 2024. Energy ministry officials said the immediate cause was the simultaneous unplanned maintenance of several flagship generating units, compounded by a recent peak in demand. They also admitted that the underlying cause lies in years of underinvestment in the country's power infrastructure, severe ageing of critical equipment and the difficulty of replacing it in a timely manner.

On the economic side, Cuba has been suffering from severe foreign exchange shortages in recent years, which have made it difficult for the country to import sufficient generator parts and maintenance consumables from the international market. Long-standing external sanctions have further constrained Cuba's access to international energy technology markets. Although the country has attempted to ease part of the pressure over the past few years through energy cooperation with Russia and Venezuela, the scale and stability of that cooperation still fall short of national demand.

From a social impact perspective, frequent large-scale blackouts have severely disrupted daily life for Cuban residents. Food refrigeration, medical equipment operation, water supply systems and communications networks all depend heavily on stable electricity, and every prolonged outage triggers a chain of issues including food spoilage, rising medical risk and stalled commercial activity. Several Cuban residents told international media they had all but lost confidence in the reliability of current power supply. Some middle-class families have begun to buy small solar and storage systems at their own expense, but such equipment remains a luxury for the vast majority of ordinary households.

## Panoramic Perspective

Cuba's sixth nationwide grid collapse of 2026 looks on the surface like an energy accident in a single country, but in substance it reflects a complex picture woven from multiple deep-seated tensions. From a historical perspective, much of Cuba's power infrastructure still dates from the Soviet-era aid projects of previous decades, and after decades of operation has reached the end of its service life. From a geopolitical perspective, long-standing external economic sanctions have severely limited Cuba's access through international markets to modern energy technologies and equipment parts. From a macroeconomic perspective, the country's foreign exchange shortage and industrial hollowing-out have left any large-scale infrastructure renewal without funding. The combination of these three pressures has produced today's fragile pattern in which a small fault tips the entire country into blackout.

Viewed from the wider Latin American region, Cuba's energy difficulties are not entirely isolated. Across the Caribbean and Central America, several small economies face similar problems of ageing energy infrastructure, high import dependence on fuel and insufficient funding for renewable energy transitions. Cuba's case provides an important warning to these countries: in a global context of accelerating energy transition, neglecting the renewal of traditional power infrastructure carries catastrophic consequences.

From a strategic energy-transition perspective, Cuba's predicament exposes another side of the global energy justice agenda. Developed countries, when promoting their green energy transitions, often focus on emissions targets and new technology R&D, paying insufficient attention to the sustainable operation of existing infrastructure in developing countries. The reality of Cuba's power system collapsing repeatedly demonstrates that during the energy transition, "transitional period resilience" of infrastructure is equally important. Any green upgrade must be built on the stable operation of the existing system.

From a geopolitical standpoint, Cuba's energy crisis also creates new variables in the gamesmanship of external powers. Long-standing allies such as Venezuela and Russia face their own energy industry difficulties, and the support they can offer Cuba is limited. Meanwhile, the United States' policy of swinging repeatedly between sanctions and dialogue has added further uncertainty to Cuba's energy diplomacy. As long as the energy problem cannot be fundamentally improved, Cuban social and economic pressure will continue to accumulate, potentially triggering larger migration flows and creating sustained pressure on border policy in neighbouring countries and the United States.
