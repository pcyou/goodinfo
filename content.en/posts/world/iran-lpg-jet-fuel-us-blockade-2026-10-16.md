**[Core Summary]**

A Reuters analysis of ship-tracking data shows that Iran has continued to export LPG and jet fuel to Asian buyers over the past months despite the US maritime blockade, using third-party transshipment, flag changes, and document forgery. The episode highlights the execution difficulty of US energy sanctions and Iran's adaptive capacity under long-running sanctions pressure.

**[Event Details]**

- **Blockade backdrop**: The US recently imposed a maritime blockade on Iran aimed at cutting off its main energy export channels and pressuring Tehran to restart nuclear talks and rein in regional proxy forces.
- **Smuggling routes**: According to Reuters ship-tracking data, some Iranian LPG carriers switched off transponders after leaving Iranian ports, conducted ship-to-ship transfers on the high seas, and then proceeded to destination ports under Southeast Asian flags with refreshed documentation.
- **Destination markets**: The cargoes flowed primarily to Asian markets including South Asia, Northeast Asia, and parts of Southeast Asia; some cargoes were re-routed to other restricted markets after transshipment.
- **Product mix**: Exports were concentrated in LPG and jet fuel, both of which are highly sensitive categories under international sanctions; LPG is used for civilian and chemical industry needs while jet fuel covers both military and civilian aviation demand.
- **Data sources**: Reuters cross-referenced commercial ship-tracking platforms, maritime insurance records, and destination port call logs to reach its findings, with several independent data providers offering supporting verification.

**[Panorama]**

The US maritime blockade on Iran faces a classic "counter-blockade adaptation" challenge. Historical US blockade experiences involving Cuba, North Korea, and Venezuela have shown that naval presence and port blockades alone cannot fully cut off exports of small-lot, highly distributed energy products. **To truly shut down Iranian energy exports, the US would need to build a multi-layered enforcement network combining satellite identification, maritime law enforcement, third-country port cooperation, and financial sanctions, a network that is far harder to build than a traditional military operation.**

The deeper tension is that "gray corridors" in global energy markets are becoming a new frontier of geopolitical contest. Compared with crude oil, LPG and jet fuel have more diffuse buyers and more flexible shipping methods, making them easier to route through transshipment and blending to evade sanctions. **The effectiveness of global energy sanctions will therefore increasingly depend on dual pressure on midstream logistics networks and end buyers, rather than solely on upstream production-side blockades.**

From a global energy market perspective, Iran's adaptive capacity highlights the "decentralization" of modern energy supply chains. Historically, a small number of large tankers and a small number of major buyers dominated global energy shipping; today, fragmented small-scale fleets, regional buyers, and diversified settlement tools are redrawing the map of global energy trade. **The core battlefield of energy sanctions contests is therefore shifting from the production end to the logistics end, with financial institutions, insurance companies, port operators, and customs regulators all entering a new arena of sanctions enforcement.**

**[Multiple Perspectives]**

- **US government**: Insists that the Iran energy blockade is making "significant progress" and that it will continue to strengthen maritime cooperation with allies to cut off Iranian sanctions evasion, while acknowledging that full enforcement faces complex challenges.
- **Iranian government**: Denies that the reported export activity violates international law, stresses Iran's right to conduct foreign trade within a sovereign framework, and says it will continue to defend energy export interests through legitimate and multilateral channels.
- **Asian buyers**: Some Asian countries and buyers publicly deny violating Iran-related sanctions, stressing that their purchases comply with domestic law and international rules; however, some private buyers have been drawn in by price incentives to participate in gray-market transactions.
- **International maritime agencies**: Hold differing views on the reliability and interpretation of ship-tracking data; some call for more unified global standards on ship tracking and sanctions enforcement.
- **Energy market analysts**: Argue that Iran's energy exports remain price-competitive in Asian markets, that the blockade is unlikely to fully cut off flows in the short term, and that such exports will continue to disrupt global energy prices and geopolitical dynamics.
