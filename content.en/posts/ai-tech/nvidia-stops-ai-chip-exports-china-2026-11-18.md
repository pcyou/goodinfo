## Key Facts

Nvidia's interim CEO announced that the company will no longer export artificial intelligence chips to China. This decision marks a further escalation of US technology restrictions against China and reflects the profound restructuring of the global semiconductor supply chain under geopolitical pressure. Analysts believe this move will accelerate China's domestic substitution process while potentially having a significant impact on Nvidia's global revenue.

## Competing Views

**US Government**: A White House National Security Council spokesperson stated that export control measures are designed to protect US national security and key technological advantages. Officials emphasized that these restrictions target military and surveillance applications and do not affect consumer-grade products.

**Nvidia Management**: During an earnings call, the company stated it is actively adjusting its product strategy to develop "special edition" chips that comply with export regulations. However, it acknowledged that increased compliance costs and market segmentation will put pressure on profit margins.

**Chinese Companies**: Tech giants like Alibaba, Baidu, and Tencent said they will continue advancing domestic chip adaptation efforts. Huawei's rotating chairman stated that external blockades have instead strengthened cohesion within the domestic industry chain and accelerated the maturation of autonomous and controllable technologies.

**International Observers**: The European Commission's Digital Affairs Commissioner warned that technological nationalism could damage the global innovation ecosystem. Semiconductor industry associations in some Asian countries called on all parties to exercise restraint and resolve differences through dialogue rather than confrontation.

**Academia**: Scholars from MIT and Stanford jointly published a research report pointing out that historical experience shows technology blockades often backfire, with blocked parties ultimately achieving breakthroughs through independent innovation. They recommended policymakers reassess the long-term effectiveness of current strategies.

## Panoramic Analysis

Nvidia's decision to stop exporting AI chips to China is another landmark event in the long-term institutionalization of US-China technological competition. From a broader perspective, this reflects the global technology system splitting into two relatively independent ecosystems—the Western camp led by the US and the Eastern camp represented by China.

For China, this ban is both a challenge and an opportunity. In the short term, Chinese AI enterprises and research institutions will face computing power shortages, potentially delaying large model training and frontier AI application development. However, in the long run, external pressure will force China to accelerate independent R&D. Domestic chip companies like Huawei Ascend, Cambricon, and Biren have received more policy and financial support and are expected to narrow the gap with international leaders in the coming years.

For Nvidia, losing the Chinese market means substantial commercial losses. China is one of the world's largest semiconductor consumer markets and one of the most active regions for AI application deployment. Although emerging markets in Southeast Asia and the Middle East are rising, they cannot fully replace the scale effect of the Chinese market in the short term. Additionally, the ban may damage Nvidia's reputation and ecosystem influence within China's developer community.

From a global supply chain perspective, this "decoupling" trend may lead to efficiency losses and slowed innovation. The semiconductor industry has long relied on global division of labor and open cooperation; artificially segmenting markets may raise costs, extend R&D cycles, and ultimately harm the interests of all participants.
