## Key Facts

- Judge Geoffrey Vos is retiring in a few days as the most senior figure in England's civil justice system, and is using the occasion to push a debate about whether and how judges should use artificial intelligence.
- Current guidance advises judges in England to confine their use of AI to administrative tasks, while Vos says he uses a commercial assistant tool constantly to locate far-flung documents and summarise large bodies of text.
- Vos recently visited a civil court in Shenzhen, China, where civil disputes are already being decided with heavy assistance from AI, and said he was impressed by what he saw.
- He believes personal injury claims, low-value professional negligence cases and similar matters could easily be adjudicated by AI, while complex commercial disputes and sensitive family cases might require a human judge.
- He framed the question as where the line should be drawn, noting that what most people want in a civil dispute is resolution.
- Multiple lawyers have been caught citing case law hallucinated by AI.
- In a first for the United Kingdom, somebody recently lost their licence to practise in part because they misused the technology.
- Litigants using chatbots as legal counsel are flooding the courts with an unprecedented number of claims, some of them bogus.

## Competing Views

The supporting argument is one of fit rather than replacement. Low-value claims with a strong professional element and relatively structured facts are exactly the kind of case where the applicable rules are most reproducible, so handling them with a system could shorten waiting times, lower costs and free limited judicial capacity for the matters that genuinely require discretion. The visit to Shenzhen is what converted this from a thought experiment into a personal conviction for him.

The sceptical argument is about the chain of responsibility. The clearest gap is who pays for a wrong decision. A human judge who gets it wrong has an appeal route and a professional standing behind the ruling; an algorithmic error carries neither a signature nor a licensed professional who can be answerable for it. The licence loss already seen in the United Kingdom shows the failure mode is not abstract: real people are losing their livelihoods over it.

A third reading from people who build legal technology treats this as a procedural design problem. Before generative systems are permitted to assist in adjudication, courts need reliable technical assessment, citation verification and human review. If verification cannot keep pace with generation, efficiency gains arrive paid for with a higher error rate, and the cost of that error eventually lands on litigants and on public funds.

## Panoramic Analysis

What is worth noticing is not that a retiring judge said it, but that he chose to say it now. His term is ending, so the statement carries no career consequences, which makes it an unusually disinterested intervention. The timing is also telling: the advocate of acceleration is speaking in the same period in which hallucinated case law is costing lawyers their licences and bogus claims are arriving in volume. The race between efficiency and reliability has moved out of the seminar room and into practice.

Within the judicial system there is a boundary that is often blurred. Processing standardised small claims, retrieving files and drafting documents are closer to expanding administrative capacity. Final determination of disputed fact, however, involves the allocation of burdens of proof and the weighing of competing values, and consistency in applying legal text does not automatically translate into legitimacy in an individual case. The line Vos draws is broadly reasonable, but he draws it on the side where the benefits are most visible to the public and where the regulatory costs are least.

Deeper still is the remaking of accountability. When decisions are generated in bulk, consequences fall on parties, and no identifiable person stands behind them, the traditional architecture of judicial accountability is hollowed out. Public confidence in courts does not rest on decisions never being wrong; it rests on there being a stage at which being wrong can be pinned on someone. A mechanism that expands adjudication while diluting the concentration of responsibility may ease backlogs in the short term while eroding the willingness to use those tools in the long term, and once that willingness is gone, no amount of efficiency is worth anything.
