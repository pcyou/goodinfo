## Key Facts

Magnus Grimeland, founder and chief executive of Antler, told the Fortune Southeast Asia 500 Summit in Vietnam that the artificial intelligence boom is the first platform shift in which Asia holds real advantages for building the leading companies, citing the region's rapidly growing electricity grid, the abundance of advanced chips and the scale of AI engineering talent.

- **The claim**: Grimeland said on 3 November that going to the United States to build companies remains very popular and is happening at scale, but that for the first time in one of these platform shifts there are real advantages in Asia that will lead to a lot of the leading companies being built out of here.
- **Power**: he said that in global power production there is four times more power being generated and built in Asia per year than in Europe and the United States combined, most of it in China but also elsewhere in the region, and that the cost of power has gone down drastically compared with what is accessible in the United States.
- **Capital**: according to Crunchbase data, investors poured 42.8 billion dollars into startup funding rounds across all of Asia in the second quarter of 2026, the highest quarterly total in over three years.
- **Physical AI**: Grimeland said the leading companies in that space are in Asia, noting that there are very few autonomous vehicles on the streets in the United States while one Chinese company has more on the streets than the rest of the world combined.
- **Uneven allocation**: according to Dealroom data, 68.1 per cent of the 141 billion dollars of venture capital that entered Asia in 2026 so far, or 96.1 billion dollars, went to China, mainly to AI startups such as DeepSeek, Moonshot AI and StepFun, while Southeast Asia received 10.7 billion dollars in the first nine months.
- **Infrastructure gap**: Franco Varona, managing partner at the Philippines-based Foxmont Capital Partners, said the country does not have the infrastructure for a large language model developer and not even the infrastructure for AI applications to be developed in the Philippines.
- **New capital forms**: Singapore-based Granite Asia's Libra Hybrid fund combines the characteristics of debt and equity in a single instrument. Managing partner Ming Eng said many Asian businesses are family owned or part of conglomerates and may not want dilution or an IPO at any point, but still need strategic capital to grow.

## Event Details

Varona argued that this environment creates different investment opportunities: in developed markets investors can benefit from infrastructure that already exists and can back innovators who have everything around them, whereas in an emerging market such as the Philippines the approach has to be different. Foxmont has launched several themed funds since, including one on digitisation in the Philippines and another focused on accessibility and affordability for Filipino consumers.

The three panellists all remained bullish on the region's venture scene despite recent geopolitical headwinds. Grimeland's conclusion was that the balance of innovation is clearly shifting, and that there is a real chance more than the majority of the most exciting and innovative companies in AI will have been built out of Asia, which has never happened before.

## Competing Views

The supporting evidence comes down to two quantifiable series: the fourfold gap in annual power additions relative to Europe and the United States combined, and the 42.8 billion dollars of second-quarter funding, the largest in over three years. The first maps directly onto the physical constraints of training and inference; the second determines whether ideas become companies.

The challenge comes from the distribution of that capital. Of the 141 billion dollars entering Asia this year, 96.1 billion went to a single market, while Southeast Asia received 10.7 billion over nine months. Varona attributes the gap to infrastructure: without local infrastructure there is no path to a language model developer, which pushes local opportunity towards applications and affordability rather than foundation models. In other words, the advantage on offer today is closer to an advantage held by one market than a regional one.

A third reading shifts attention from geography to the form of capital. Ming Eng treats family ownership and conglomerate structures as the reason hybrid debt-equity instruments appear at all, since many businesses will not accept dilution or rush to market but still need strategic capital to expand. The value of such instruments lies in their fit with local ownership, not in a regional label.

## Panoramic Analysis

This view is worth recording because it moves the artificial intelligence conversation off the model leaderboard and onto power and capital, two things that cannot be flattened quickly.

Model capability can be copied and chip performance gaps can be closed with engineering effort, but generation capacity and grid expansion run on the clock of heavy assets and cannot be compressed in the short run. That is why, in every discussion of compute bottlenecks, the unit that keeps appearing in the mouths of decision makers is ultimately the megawatt rather than the parameter count. Comparing Asia's annual additions in generation against Europe and the United States combined at four to one points to a structural advantage that cannot be relieved quickly through trade or procurement.

But the same body of data carries a second tension. The concentration of funding shows that a power advantage does not automatically produce a regionally balanced startup ecosystem. The gap between 10.7 billion dollars for Southeast Asia and 96.1 billion dollars for China is close to ninefold, and the condition described by the Philippines-based fund manager, where the absence of infrastructure means even applications cannot be built locally, is precisely the mechanism that reproduces that gap: infrastructure, developer density and capital reinforce one another.

That is why the phrase Asian advantage needs more precision. On infrastructure density, Asia as a whole leads. On the concentration of capital and talent, that advantage currently sits overwhelmingly in mainland China. And for markets such as the Philippines, Vietnam and Indonesia, the right investment proposition is not to replicate Silicon Valley or Shenzhen but to look for application wedges around local payment capacity, offline commercial formats and existing industrial chains.

Three indicators are worth following: the actual grid-connection progress of data centre and power projects across Asian countries, whether AI startup funding into Southeast Asia changes by an order of magnitude next year, and whether a second, or a tenth, team in those markets genuinely develops models. Those three determine whether the platform shift stays on a conference stage or turns into a new distribution of companies.
