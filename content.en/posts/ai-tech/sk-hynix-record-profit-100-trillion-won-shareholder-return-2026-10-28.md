## Key Facts

- Revenue rose 327.9 percent from a year earlier while operating profit climbed 632.1 percent. Compared with the previous quarter, revenue grew 31.9 percent and operating profit surged 37.6 percent.
- SK Hynix posted record quarterly revenue and operating profit, with sales exceeding 100 trillion won for the first time in the company's history.
- The company announced plans to return about 100 trillion won to shareholders, including at least 30 trillion won in cash dividends and 60 trillion won in share buybacks and cancellations.
- The chief executive, Kwak Noh-Jung, said the company intends to return gains realised from its investment in the Japanese chipmaker Kioxia to shareholders through a special dividend this year.
- A third-quarter dividend of 14 trillion won was approved, including that special dividend funded by the Kioxia gains.
- The company will buy back 20 trillion won of stock, half of which will be cancelled this year, after completing a previously announced 40 trillion won buyback.
- Kwak cited financial market volatility and a correction in semiconductor stocks as the main reasons for halting the review of potential pre-IPO fundraising and listing options for the US subsidiary Solidigm.
- The company plans to raise its shareholder return target to more than 50 percent of cumulative free cash flow generated between 2025 and 2027, citing continued momentum in artificial intelligence investment from wider adoption of AI services and a more diverse investor base.
- SK Hynix expects the structural supply-demand imbalance in the memory market to continue.
- Shares rose more than 8 percent, outperforming the Kospi's 6.25 percent gain.

## Competing Views

The bullish reading is that the imbalance in the memory market is structural rather than cyclical, with wider adoption of artificial intelligence services as a demand variable that can be tracked over time. That related shares rallied after the results suggests the market re-read the print as evidence that compute spending will persist, rather than as evidence about one quarter alone.

The cautious reading points at a different disclosure on the same day. The company paused its review of a listing for the Solidigm subsidiary while semiconductor stocks were correcting. That is itself a signal: when the valuation window narrowed, it chose to contract capital actions and concentrate resources on returns already in hand. Raising the return target to above half of cumulative free cash flow also means management believes cash generation can carry that level, which by definition compresses the share retained.

Structural commentary draws a distinction from earlier memory cycles. In past cycles a company stopped expanding at the peak and waited for prices to fall. This one can expand capacity and still return capital of a hundred trillion won to shareholders after profits rose more than sixfold, which means demand is being treated as structural. That is exactly why excess profit is historically the signal most likely to trigger over-expansion, and because memory capacity takes eighteen to twenty-four months to move from decision to production, the lag is long enough for the cycle to turn before the supply arrives.

## Panoramic Analysis

The number that deserves attention in this report is not the 632 percent. It is the hundred trillion won.

Converted into more familiar units, roughly 100 trillion won is about 73 billion dollars, the same order of magnitude as a full quarter of revenue. A company returning the equivalent of one quarter's sales to shareholders in the same report in which it posted record profit is unusual by the standards of Korean corporate practice, and it signals that management believes cash generation is no longer constrained by the cycle. For a manufacturer that was barely profitable two years ago when the industry was in its worst downturn, that is a structural change rather than a cyclical one.

The decision announced the same day is the necessary counterweight. The company halted its review of a listing for its US subsidiary while semiconductor stocks were correcting. One corporate is simultaneously returning capital at a historic scale and closing the door on a new source of it. That is not contradictory; it is a precise statement about where the company believes it sits in the cycle. A buyback is reversible. A listing is not. Choosing the reversible instrument while the valuation window is narrow is what disciplined capital allocation looks like at the top of a memory upcycle.

The deeper change is in the shape of demand. Earlier memory cycles were driven by replacement demand from phones and personal computers, which was forecastable, comparable, and would saturate quickly once capacity arrived. This round is driven by the continuing deployment of inference compute, whose demand rises with the spread of services actually in use rather than pulsing with a promotional quarter. That shape inverts the old rule of capacity discipline. The historical instruction was not to expand into a price decline. The new instruction is that when demand appears structurally rising, capacity must be added, and returns must be paid out alongside the build, because capital expenditure funded entirely from internal cash is read by the market as waste.

The third layer concerns where profit sits in the supply chain. In the compute story the loudest names have always been the designers of the chips. Memory makers carried a label closer to that of a cyclical commodity. But when inference demand turns memory from a replaceable component into the capacity bottleneck, the memory layer becomes the thickest cash flow in the chain, and its ability to return capital can exceed that of the designers themselves. That helps explain why memory equities held up better than compute equities through this phase, and it points at where the next argument in the industry will be fought: not over whose model is strongest, but over who gets to price the expansion.
