## Key Facts

Tesla will stop making the Cybertruck in 2027, confirming the end of a product line that was once described by the company as the future of its Texas factory. This is the third vehicle Tesla has discontinued to redirect capacity toward artificial intelligence and robotics. Earlier this year the company ended production of the Model S sedan and Model X SUV, dedicating that factory space to making Optimus humanoid robots.

- **The original ambition**: when Musk handed over the first Cybertrucks in late 2023, he predicted sales of roughly 250,000 a year, and before construction began the company referred to its Texas plant as the Cybertruck factory.
- **A troubled launch**: first announced in 2019, the product was over-promised on the basics at its reveal event, where the head designer cracked the prototype windows with steel balls. It was then delayed by two years, partly because of the pandemic, and finally reached customers in late 2023.
- **Weak market performance**: the polarising design never sat well with buyers, particularly at a higher-than-expected price, and the truck struggled throughout its sales life.
- **A political variable**: during its first full year of sales in 2024, Musk became a major financial backer of Donald Trump, and the instantly recognisable truck became a target of street protests, a dynamic that continued into 2025.
- **The shift in focus**: Tesla is trying to become an artificial intelligence and robotics company, and its robotaxi ambitions are central to that. Cybercabs have been running on the streets of Austin for two months without major incidents, and the company says it plans to expand to Dallas later this month.
- **The wider robotaxi footprint**: robotaxi versions of the Model Y SUV are already operating in Austin, Houston and Dallas, and in three cities in Florida.

## Event Details

The Cybertruck occupied a peculiar place in Tesla's history. It was announced in 2019, with Musk describing it on an earnings call as probably his personal favourite for the next product, and he promised something radical inspired by cyberpunk science fiction. That ambition was realised in the design, but it was the fundamentals that were over-promised. The production version quietly diluted the stainless steel promise, and the reveal itself, with windows cracked by steel balls, became a lasting shorthand for a company that could sell the demonstration better than it could ship the product.

Market performance never recovered from that mismatch. Two years of weak sales followed, and the vehicle that was meant to define the next decade of the company became its clearest example of a product whose appeal depended on a design that also repelled the utilitarian buyers who form the largest pickup market.

The timing of the decision is the part that carries the most information. Tesla has been running Cybercabs in Austin for two months, without major incidents to date, and the expansion to Dallas is imminent. Ending the Cybertruck line in 2027 frees the manufacturing capacity, capital and engineering attention that the robotaxi programme will need at precisely the point when that programme has to scale beyond a single city.

## Competing Views

The case for ending production rests on resource allocation. A company repositioning itself as an artificial intelligence and robotics business cannot rationally reserve dedicated capacity for a vehicle with persistent sales shortfalls, and the same logic that retired the Model S and Model X applies here with even greater force, because the Cybertruck is the more expensive of the two to build.

The counterargument concerns brand and credibility. The Cybertruck was one of the very few production cars with genuine cultural distinctiveness, and retiring it weakens Tesla's standing as a manufacturer willing to take risks that nobody else will take. That argument is reinforced by the pattern of the reveal, where expectations were raised well beyond what the production vehicle delivered, and by the resulting erosion of trust.

A third reading places the decision inside a longer sequence rather than treating it as an isolated commercial judgement. This is the third product line retired in favour of a higher-priority programme, which suggests a systematic conversion of automotive capacity into artificial intelligence capacity rather than a one-off response to weak sales.

## Panoramic Analysis

Read the three discontinued product lines together and the strategy is considerably more deliberate than any single car failing to sell. The Model S and Model X were retired to free factory space for the Optimus humanoid robot, and the Cybertruck is being retired to make room for the Cybercab. Both moves point the same way: the automotive lines are being converted from profit centres into reserves of capital and production capacity for the artificial intelligence business.

The timing is what makes this more than a cost-cutting exercise. The Cybertruck was the most expensive of the three to build, with a dedicated plant, specialised stainless steel forming and a high unit cost, none of which can be easily transferred to a competitor. Ending it does more than forgo a marginal vehicle; it removes a fixed burden from the income statement. For a company whose valuation increasingly rests on an autonomy option, that matters, because an option only pays if the capacity to exercise it is cheap to acquire, and the automotive business has been sitting on exactly the best plants and engineering teams the autonomy programme needs.

There is a harder lesson underneath, about product definition. The Cybertruck carried an internal contradiction from the start: it was designed to attract technophiles through radical styling while needing to persuade mainstream pickup buyers through basic competence, and the over-promising at launch exposed how far the production version had to compromise on protection and practicality. Both core audiences were lost at once. For a company that advertises its willingness to redesign products from first principles, that is an uncomfortable counter-example.

The third layer is a mismatch between valuation structure and operating structure. When a company's market value approaches a call option on autonomy and robotics, its operating centre of gravity shifts instinctively toward lowering the cost of exercising that option, and the cheapest way to do that is to shed drag rather than add assets. This is why product discontinuations appear in this company's news more often than product launches.

The risks are equally clear. Cybercab remains in limited pilot operation across a small number of cities, and scaling it requires regulatory approval, a fleet maintenance organisation and real incident-rate evidence, none of which is yet demonstrated. Ending a genuine revenue line at this moment trades certain revenue for uncertain future revenue, and the line being ended happens to be the product that most sustains public attention and brand premium.

Four indicators deserve tracking: the scale and operating data of the Cybercab deployment once it leaves Austin for Dallas, Optimus progress on the production line freed by the Model S and Model X, whether Tesla introduces a more pragmatically priced electric pickup, and the pace at which Cybertruck inventory is cleared. The first two determine whether the narrative can be delivered, the third determines whether the company concedes that its earlier pricing strategy was wrong, and the fourth is the most immediate financial readout.
