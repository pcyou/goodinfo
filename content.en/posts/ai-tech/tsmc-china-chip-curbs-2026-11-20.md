[Brief] TSMC Tightens China AI Chip Manufacturing Restrictions

## Key Facts

- **New policy**: TSMC has strengthened its review procedures for mainland Chinese customers to prevent chips from being resold through third parties to circumvent US export controls.
- **Disclosure requirement**: Customers must disclose end-user identity and intended use for all orders at seven-nanometre processes and below.
- **Scope**: The restrictions primarily affect advanced chips used for training large language models and other AI applications.
- **Rationale**: The move aligns with recent updates to US export control rules, closing previously existing manufacturing loopholes.
- **Impact**: Chinese tech companies and their overseas subsidiaries may face delays of three to six months in AI projects.
- **Company statement**: A TSMC spokesperson said the company strictly complies with all applicable laws and regulations.

## Event Details

Sources familiar with the matter revealed that TSMC has implemented stricter customer vetting processes. Under the new policy, all chip orders at seven-nanometre nodes and below must disclose the final user's identity and intended application. If the chips are likely to be used for training large language models or other AI applications, TSMC will require additional compliance documentation. This initiative aims to cooperate with the US government's recently updated export control rules, filling previous manufacturing loopholes. Those primarily affected are Chinese technology companies and their overseas subsidiaries, which may have been obtaining advanced chips through third-country transshipment. An industry analyst estimated that this could delay some Chinese AI projects by three to six months.

## Competing Views

**Supporters see this as necessary compliance.** US Department of Commerce officials stated that the effectiveness of export controls depends on cooperation across the entire supply chain, including foundries. As the world's largest advanced-process supplier, TSMC's compliance attitude directly affects policy outcomes. The Semiconductor Industry Association also supports this approach, arguing that clear rules help long-term industry stability.

**Critics worry about innovation impact.** China's tech sector criticises the move as an extension of technological blockade, potentially hindering global AI research collaboration. Some international scholars point out that excessive restrictions may prompt China to accelerate independent R&D, ultimately weakening Western firms' market position. Additionally, increased review processes may raise costs for all customers, including those in non-sensitive fields.

**Neutral observers focus on implementation details.** Analysts note that the key lies in whether the definition of "end user" is clear and whether review standards are transparent. Vague standards could create compliance uncertainty, affecting business decisions. Balancing national security with commercial freedom remains an unresolved challenge.

## Panoramic Analysis

TSMC's decision reflects the semiconductor industry's difficult position under geopolitical pressure. As the world's most advanced chip manufacturer, TSMC sits at the centre of US-China tech competition. On one hand, it must comply with US law to maintain relationships with American customers like Apple and Nvidia; on the other, mainland China is an important market, accounting for about ten percent of revenue.

From an industrial perspective, this restriction marks chip manufacturing as a key battleground in great-power rivalry. Previously, export controls mainly targeted design software and equipment sales; now they extend directly to manufacturing. This means even if Chinese firms can design advanced chips, they may not find foundries to produce them. This "manufacturing bottleneck" may be harder to overcome than a "design bottleneck", as building advanced fabs requires tens of billions in investment and years of time.

Key developments to watch include whether other foundries (Samsung, Intel) will adopt similar policies, whether China will introduce countermeasures, and whether domestic Chinese fabs (such as SMIC) can fill the capacity gap. If TSMC's restrictions significantly slow China's AI development, this could reshape the global tech competition landscape; conversely, if China can rapidly achieve autonomous manufacturing, it may accelerate technological decoupling.
