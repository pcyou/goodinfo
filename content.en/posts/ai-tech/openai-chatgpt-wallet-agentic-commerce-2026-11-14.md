## Key Facts

- **Partner networks**: Built with American Express, Mastercard and Visa; cardholders keep existing rewards and the dispute protections that come with the card.
- **Token mechanism**: The agents never see the user's card details. The payment network issues a token containing a scoped card number, security code and expiry date, valid for that single transaction.
- **User control**: Buyers review the items, the total and the card to be charged before checkout, then approve each purchase with a security check such as Face ID or a fingerprint, which leaves a transaction record.
- **Onboarding**: Eligible subscribers add their card once; details are not re-entered for later purchases.
- **Launch scope**: A limited rollout to United States Pro subscribers aged 18 and over, deliberately contained so the feature can be tested before wider release.
- **Revenue posture**: OpenAI is not taking a cut of transactions at launch. The stated focus is building relationships with merchants and card networks, and a commerce partner playbook was published alongside the announcement.
- **Merchant resistance**: Amazon already blocks agents from purchasing on its platform in order to steer traffic to its own assistant, and United Airlines said this week it would block agents from accessing its booking systems.
- **Scale**: ChatGPT reports more than 1.2 billion weekly active users and close to 100 million paying users. Meta's personal agent product reports over ten million users with assisted shopping among its top activities, while food delivery and ride-hailing platforms report low agent order volumes.

## Event Details

OpenAI announced ChatGPT Wallet on Friday, a payment feature that lets its agents complete purchases on a user's behalf. The company built the feature with American Express, Mastercard and Visa, so cardholders keep their existing rewards and their ability to dispute a transaction. A subscriber adds an eligible card once, asks the agent to find something to buy, and approves each purchase with a security check.

The arrangement is deliberately narrow on credentials. OpenAI says the agents never access the user's card details; the payment networks instead hand the agent a token containing a scoped card number, security code and expiration date. OpenAI's head of product for monetization and payments framed the release as the natural next step after product discovery: once a user has found something worth buying, the aim is to carry them through to the endpoint.

Commercial logic runs ahead of monetization. Purchases made through the wallet could eventually open a new revenue line, but the company said it is not taking a cut of transactions to begin with, and is working instead on merchant relationships and on positioning itself as a serious platform for shoppers. Merchants are making their own calculations: Amazon prevents agents from purchasing products on its platform, and United Airlines said this week it will block agents from accessing its systems to book tickets. OpenAI published tools and guidance aimed at merchants alongside the announcement, including how to avoid inadvertently blocking its agents.

## Competing Views

**The bullish reading is that the real change is in settlement rather than in recommendation.** Agents have become quite good at product recommendation over the past year, and the genuine bottleneck has always been the last step. Payment requires a card, an approval and a traceable party of record. OpenAI's head of product for monetization and payments was direct about the positioning: if a user has found something they are excited about, the next step is to get them through to the endpoint. That moves the competitive centre of gravity from who recommends better to who actually controls the transaction.

**The cautious reading locates the obstacle on the merchant side rather than the technical side.** Amazon blocking agents and United Airlines closing its booking interface are not signs of an immature payment rail. They are signs of a fight over traffic and basket size: when an agent places the order, the platform loses the recommendation slot and the advertising slot that came with it. OpenAI publishing a merchant handbook and stressing that it is talking first to networks and merchants is itself an acknowledgement that the ecosystem still needs time.

**A third view argues that card networks are not being disintermediated but redefined.** In this structure the agent replaces the user's hand, not the bank. Tokens are issued by the networks, rewards and dispute rights stay in place, and authentication still runs on issuer infrastructure. Agentic commerce pushes the contest from the acquiring side to the agent side rather than pushing networks out of the chain.

## Panoramic Analysis

On the surface this release adds a payment button. What it actually does is turn the word agent from a descriptive term into a business role with execution power. Until now the capability boundary of these systems stopped at recommendation: an agent could tell a user which shop was cheaper but could not complete a purchase, and therefore could not really decide the outcome. That boundary has now been crossed, and once it is crossed, the metrics that judge an agent platform change with it. The question is no longer how often the answers are right, but how often the transaction succeeds, how often it is returned, and how often it turns into a dispute.

The second shift is in the trust structure. The load-bearing element of the design is the single-transaction token: the network issues a stand-in credential carrying a scoped card number, security code and expiry date, usable only for that one purchase. This effectively breaks authorisation down from a one-time blanket consent into a per-transaction grant that can be withdrawn at will. The technology is the same road card organisations travelled years ago with tokenisation, but the driving force is different. Back then the goal was to reduce data exposure; this time the goal is to let an automated agent that cannot be fully trusted safely spend money on a user's behalf. For the payments industry, the second motive carries far more commercial imagination and far more awkward questions.

The third layer is the ordering of commercial priorities. Not taking a cut at launch, and putting merchant and network relationships ahead of revenue, reveals a basic judgement: the moat in agentic commerce does not sit in the take rate, it sits in being the default entry point when a user decides to buy. Amazon's posture shows that no platform intends to hand that entry point over lightly. The next contest will run over agent identification, authorisation standards and anti-abuse rules, and whoever writes those standards decides whether agentic commerce produces a new unified entry point or settles into a permanent state of platforms blocking each other.

There is also a reverse effect worth naming. When agents make the choice, the weight that determines how products are ordered shifts further from platform ranking toward model-interpreted preference, which is the same movement already underway in search. Four indicators are worth tracking: the share of agent-placed orders in overall e-commerce transaction volume; whether platforms extend their blocks to more sites; how token issuance volumes and dispute rates move at the networks; and who ends up liable when an early user is charged for something they did not want or did not order.
