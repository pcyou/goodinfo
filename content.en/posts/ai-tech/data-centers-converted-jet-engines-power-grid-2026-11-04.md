## Key Facts

Facing years-long queues for grid connections and slow delivery of new turbines, some artificial intelligence data centres are generating their own electricity with converted jet engines, The New York Times reported. Rystad Energy analyst Audun Martinsen told the paper that the attraction is speed rather than efficiency, because with jet-powered units a data centre can be running in twenty-four months. Connecting one hundred megawatts to the grid in Northern Virginia takes about seven years according to the International Energy Agency.

- **The gap that matters**: seven years for a hundred megawatts in Northern Virginia against twenty-four months for converted jet engines.
- **Industry capacity timing**: an executive at Mitsubishi Power, one of the world's largest makers of generation equipment, told Bloomberg last week that most new power capacity is expected to come on by 2031.
- **Who is already doing it**: Elon Musk's xAI has installed eight converted jet-engine turbines in Mississippi to help power its Colossus 2 data centre in Memphis. The units come from ProEnergy, which rebuilds a GE engine commonly fitted to Boeing airliners.
- **The precedent**: in 2025 OpenAI followed the same approach with gas turbines at its first Stargate site in Abilene, Texas. Crusoe, the Denver startup building that campus, ordered thirteen converted ProEnergy units in April.
- **Business already lost**: Microsoft pulled out as the anchor customer of Nscale's data centre in Loughton, England after the local utility said it could not deliver enough power until the early to mid-2030s.
- **The trade-off**: converted engines are less efficient and pollute more than turbines designed for conventional gas plants, and it takes more of them to make the same power. Some experts expect efficiency and environmental concerns to matter more within a year or two.
- **Policy backdrop**: the administration is currently encouraging data centres to generate their own electricity. In September Crusoe walked away from a $1.25 billion deal to buy twenty-nine natural gas turbines from Boom Supersonic, though a company spokesperson said it still plans to use turbines.

## Competing Views

**The energy analyst puts speed ahead of long-run cost.** For Martinsen the point is getting power as quickly as possible, which is why converted engines are being considered at all. That ordering says the scarce commodity right now is time rather than the unit cost of electricity.

**The equipment maker supplies a different horizon.** Mitsubishi Power's executive put most new capacity at around 2031. A queue that long is not a procurement delay to be managed but a generational gap in the equipment cycle.

**The procurement side has already shown its own instability.** Crusoe walked away from a $1.25 billion turbine order in September, while still saying it intends to use turbines. A specialist builder of data centres cannot offer a delivery date with confidence, which says more about the constraint than any policy announcement would.

**A concrete commercial loss has already occurred.** Microsoft's withdrawal from the Loughton project because the local utility could not commit to power before the 2030s converts an energy cost question into a question of whether deals close at all.

## Panoramic Analysis

Converting retired airliner engines into power plants is easy to read as an absurdity, and it is one. But read as information rather than as colour, it is the clearest available statement that electricity has become the binding constraint on compute, and a binding constraint strong enough that operators will accept lower efficiency and higher emissions to escape it.

The ordering of constraints in the compute narrative used to be unambiguous. Chips came first, then advanced packaging, then memory, and electricity came last. That order is now being inverted. Chip capacity can be added in two to three years, and packaging lines can be built and ramped, but grid interconnection is decided by a process running through planning, permitting, transmission corridors and transformer delivery, none of which any single company controls. One hundred megawatts taking seven years in Northern Virginia is not a price problem. It is a schedule problem, and data centre leases, chip purchase orders and customer contracts are all counted in months.

That reversal has a consequence which is easy to miss: it changes what a site selection decision is about. Latency, land and tax incentives used to be the variables. Whether local power will physically exist, and when, now has to sit at the front of the list. Two follow-on effects follow directly. Operators with genuine self-generation capability acquire a real location advantage over those without, which is an entry barrier that did not previously exist in this industry. And scarcity in power gets capitalised into land, rents and equipment prices, carrying the constraint all the way into the cost of a unit of compute.

Converted jet engines will probably not be where this ends. They are less efficient, they pollute more, they need more units for the same output, and their economics depend on gas prices and the residual value of used airframes. Once the industry's attention shifts from speed to cost and emissions, they get replaced. What the episode reveals is a timing gap: if the electricity constraint persists for years, then the incremental compute supply arriving over the next two to three years will fall well short of what the market has been forecasting from chip capacity alone.

Three things are worth watching. Whether the alternative equipment orders can actually be delivered. Whether the backlog on conventional turbines keeps lengthening. And whether the list of projects abandoned for lack of power keeps growing. The third matters most, because each additional case along the lines of Microsoft's withdrawal from Loughton is a data point suggesting current demand forecasts are systematically overstated.
