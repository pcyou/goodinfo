## Key Facts

- **The range**: Anthropic set the price range for its initial public offering at $70 to $78 per share, according to an updated filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission on Tuesday, November 10.
- **Size of the offering**: The company plans to sell 825 million shares of Class A common stock, of which 801.4 million are newly issued and 23.6 million come from existing shareholders.
- **What it raises**: At the top of the range the offering would raise about $64.4 billion, including the stake held by selling shareholders.
- **Greenshoe**: Underwriters have an option to buy a further 123.75 million shares.
- **Two valuation figures**: Based on the roughly 13.09 billion shares the filing says will be outstanding after the offering, the range implies a market value of about $916 billion to $1.02 trillion. On a fully diluted basis the company could be worth up to $1.405 trillion, according to CNBC, up from the $965 billion valuation it announced in a funding round in May.
- **Below the earlier expectation**: The IPO was previously expected to be valued at as much as $2 trillion. As of last week Anthropic's rival OpenAI was valued at $1.25 trillion.
- **The chipmaker private placement**: Seven companies that supply Anthropic's computing power — Nvidia, Google, AMD, Broadcom, Micron, Microsoft and TSMC — have agreed to buy a combined $26 billion of Class N common stock, which carries no voting rights, at the IPO price.
- **Timing**: Anthropic expects to price the offering on November 18 and begin trading on November 19. The Wall Street Journal reported, citing sources, that the company is aiming for shares to start trading Thursday or Friday the following week.
- **Retail allocation**: The company is targeting a retail allocation of about 20%, well above the norm for an IPO, CNBC reported, citing people familiar with the details.
- **Ticker and venue**: Anthropic will trade on the Nasdaq under the ticker "ANT," which it revealed when it made its IPO filing public last month.
- **Revenue and loss**: The company behind the Claude chatbot earned $16.3 billion in revenue over the first six months of this year, growing nearly 14-fold from the same period a year earlier, according to a government filing late last month. It still reported a net loss of $80.7 billion over the same six months.
- **Risk disclosure**: Anthropic's filing devotes 90 pages to risk as it prepares for the IPO.
- **Control**: The co-founders retain majority voting control, and the common stock is separated by voting rights from the non-voting Class N shares sold in the private placement.

## Competing Views

**The bullish case rests on revenue velocity.** A near fourteen-fold increase in first-half revenue has few comparables on any public market. Even measured against a post-money market value of roughly $1.02 trillion, the static revenue multiple looks undemanding for a company of that growth profile. What makes the private placement more significant is who is buying: seven chipmakers and cloud providers that supply Anthropic's compute are committing $26 billion at the IPO price, which is institutional demand rather than retail enthusiasm, and it is rare to see that many names writing cheques at the same price on the same day.

**The bearish case focuses on the gap between two valuations.** The range implies about $916 billion to $1.02 trillion in market value after the offering, while the fully diluted figure reaches $1.405 trillion. The roughly $400 billion difference is not an accounting curiosity; it is the share count that future equity compensation and convertible instruments will dilute. After a drawdown in AI-linked equities, investor tolerance for dilution has thinned considerably, and how that gap gets resolved on pricing day may determine where the shares trade.

**The size of the loss reads very differently depending on the lens.** An $80.7 billion half-year net loss annualizes past $160 billion. Critics see that as evidence the company burns cash faster than it generates revenue, which argues for a discount to make room for the next funding window. Supporters note that spending of this kind is the cost of training frontier models rather than operational disorder, and point to the 90 pages of risk factors as evidence that governance is maturing rather than lagging.

**The retreat from $2 trillion is the reference point everyone keeps returning to.** The slide from a $2 trillion aspiration to a $1.4 trillion ceiling looks like a change in the tape rather than in the business, since the revenue data shows the underlying trajectory did not slow. The disagreement is therefore not really about operations. It is about what a public market will pay for a frontier lab whose business model is not yet proven sustainable.

## Panoramic Analysis

The most revealing number in this pricing is not the $78 ceiling but the $26 billion private placement. Nvidia, Google, Microsoft and TSMC buying non-voting stock in an unlisted company, at the same price and on the same day, does three things at once. It writes long-term compute supply into the capital structure, so the founding team does not have to surrender voting control to raise capital. It puts a layer of institutional demand under the offering at a moment when the equity market has shown it will punish richly valued AI companies. And it converts what was a variable cost line — buying compute at whatever the market charged — into a predictable fixed commitment for years.

That is a genuinely different structure from the AI funding rounds of the past two years, most of which were equity raises from venture investors taking large positions and large boards. If Anthropic's private placement is the template, then the frontier lab financing model is quietly shifting from venture capital toward strategic industry capital, with different covenants, different control arrangements and very different exit expectations.

The roughly $400 billion spread between post-offering market value and the fully diluted number deserves its own scrutiny. Such gaps are common and rarely contested in isolation. What makes this one consequential is the environment: a sector that has already corrected, a price range that has been cut from $2 trillion, and a $26 billion class of new holders whose own interests are tied to the stock performing well.

Whether the pricing holds will be settled within about three months of the November 19 listing. A retail allocation lifted to about 20% is a deliberate confidence test aimed at the general public; if that tranche is met with selling on day one, it will signal that the market is more worried about dilution than it is impressed by revenue velocity. If institutional demand oversubscribes at pricing, the question stops being whether $1.4 trillion is defensible and becomes how long a company with $16.3 billion of first-half revenue and an $80.7 billion loss needs to look before that number stops looking expensive.
