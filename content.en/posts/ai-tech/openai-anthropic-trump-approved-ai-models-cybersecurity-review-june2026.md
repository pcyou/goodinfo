## Core Summary

OpenAI and Anthropic have announced that during a government cybersecurity review, their latest AI models will be restricted to customers approved by the Trump administration. Simultaneously, the administration has opened Anthropic's Mythos model to over 100 US companies and government agencies, marking an unprecedented level of government control over frontier AI technology distribution.

## Event Details

According to AP News, OpenAI and Anthropic have formally implemented a new customer access mechanism: during the cybersecurity review period, their most advanced AI models will only be available to customers who pass the Trump administration's approval process. This means businesses, research institutions, and individuals without government approval will be unable to access these frontier AI tools.

Simultaneously, TechCrunch reported that the Trump administration has opened Anthropic's Mythos model to more than 100 US companies and government agencies. Mythos is one of Anthropic's most powerful AI models, which previously sparked controversy when CISA (Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency) was denied access.

The simultaneous rollout of these two policies reveals the Trump administration's dual strategy in AI governance: strictly controlling AI technology diffusion through an approval mechanism while selectively ensuring "trusted" enterprises can access frontier AI capabilities.

## Panorama Analysis

This event marks a fundamental shift in global AI governance. Previously, access to frontier AI models was primarily determined by market demand and technical capability. However, the US government's intervention signals that AI technology is transitioning from a purely commercial product to a strategic national resource, with distribution increasingly shaped by national security and political considerations rather than market forces alone.

From an industry perspective, this "approval-based" model could profoundly impact AI innovation ecosystems. It creates a "two-tier market" where approved enterprises gain significant competitive advantages while unapproved ones may fall behind technologically. This model could also trigger emulation by other nations, pushing global AI governance toward greater fragmentation and politicization.

From an international competition standpoint, the US government's move reflects both national security concerns and industrial competition intentions. By controlling frontier AI model distribution, the administration is effectively reshaping the domestic AI industry landscape. However, excessive government intervention may also stifle innovation and raise concerns among allies and competitors alike.

In the long term, this event could become a turning point in AI governance history, raising a fundamental question: in an era of increasingly powerful AI, who has the right to decide who can use these technologies?

## Multiple Perspectives

**US Government Position**: The White House states that cybersecurity review is essential to prevent AI technology misuse. A spokesperson emphasized the approval process would be "fast and transparent," aiming to balance innovation with security.

**OpenAI and Anthropic**: Both companies expressed support for the government's cybersecurity review, calling it an important part of "responsible AI deployment." However, industry analysts note this cooperation may also bring competitive advantages through government endorsement.

**Critics**: Civil liberties groups and technology policy researchers have raised concerns, arguing that linking AI model access to government approval could lead to "technology censorship" and "innovation barriers," harming the openness and competitiveness of the AI industry.

**Business Community**: The 100+ companies granted Mythos access welcomed the move, saying it would enhance their competitiveness. Unapproved companies expressed dissatisfaction, concerned that "selective openness" would create unfair competition.

**International Observation**: The EU and multiple Asian nations are closely monitoring the US approach. Some analysts believe this could trigger a global "AI approval race," with countries establishing their own AI access mechanisms.
