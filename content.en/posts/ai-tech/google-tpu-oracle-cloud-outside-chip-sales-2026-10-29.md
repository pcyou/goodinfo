## Key Facts

- Alphabet said on Tuesday that Oracle will offer Google tensor processing units, the custom chips the internet giant has been developing since 2013. It is the first time customers can access the chips outside Google infrastructure.
- The announcement came a day ahead of Alphabet third-quarter earnings report, and follows Google opening the chips to outside data centers in the second quarter.
- Thomas Kurian, who runs Google Cloud, spent more than 20 years at Oracle before joining Google in late 2018, and is now looking beyond his own cloud for growth.
- Analysts expect Google Cloud revenue to jump about 90 percent from a year earlier, after growing 82 percent in the second quarter, with the custom chips contributing to that momentum.
- Two banks model large and sharply divergent trajectories for the chip business: one sees about $9.4 billion of sales in the second half of this year and $62.5 billion in 2027, while another expects roughly $9 billion in the third quarter and about $99 billion next year.
- An analyst at a capital management firm said the chips are a very strong competitor and it is quite possible they eventually take market share from the incumbent leader of the market.
- A portfolio manager said Google was great at doing its own thing but was never able to serve customers, and that this mindset has changed.
- The move breaks with years of selling chips, models and cloud services as one bundled stack, a model Amazon and Microsoft have spent years trying to copy.

## Competing Views

**The optimistic case** is about channel economics. Margins in the chip business do not depend on how many data centers a company owns, they depend on how many outside customers are willing to pay for the compute. The constraint on the custom chip business was never capacity, it was that external customers had no way to obtain the hardware at all. Wiring in Oracle's cloud opens the demand side rather than the supply side.

**The cautious case** points at the estimates themselves. Two houses modelling the same product line are nearly $40 billion apart on next year revenue, which means nobody has a settled model for how this business behaves. Under that much dispersion, writing the custom chip contribution into a long-range growth narrative carries more risk than treating it as one product line among several.

**A third reading** focuses on pricing power. For years the pitch was to buy the chip, the model and the cloud in one place, and that bundle was a genuine differentiator. The moment the chip can be ordered on its own from another provider, the buyer gains the right to compare, and the bundle stops being a convenience and starts being a constraint. That is a voluntary surrender of part of the pricing advantage in exchange for a much larger market, and Google can afford it because the model and cloud layers carry the cash flow.

## Panoramic Analysis

The weight of this announcement is heavier than a single line about expanding sales channels.

The artificial intelligence compute market spent the past three years arranging itself into an almost closed structure. Nearly every accelerator was designed by one American vendor, nearly every leading chip was fabricated in Taiwan, and the advanced packaging that links the two sat in the same geography. Google building its own silicon since 2013 was, in that arrangement, the exception, and it kept that silicon to itself: own the design, own the fleet, sell the cloud.

Choosing now to place that silicon inside someone else's data centers carries two distinct meanings. Externally, it concedes a point that is easy to miss: selling chips, models and cloud as a sealed bundle was a differentiator while demand outran supply, and becomes a ceiling on revenue once it does not. A customer who can only buy the accelerator from the vendor of the cloud has no second option when the price looks high. Once the accelerator can be rented on a rival cloud, the accelerator has to defend itself on measured performance, price and toolchain, alone. Google is choosing a wider market over a protected margin, and it can make that trade because the model and cloud layers pay the bills.

The second meaning is structural, and it concerns how this industry actually scales. Cloud economics reward size, because the unit cost of a served request falls as volume rises. Chip economics behave more like manufacturing: capacity is a fixed cost once built, throughput is what amortizes it, and throughput requires buyers. For three years the allocation rule of the industry has been simple, namely that whoever controls the chip controls the terms. What comes next is a contest over who can reliably deliver that chip to a third party. By routing capacity through another provider's cloud, Google is borrowing someone else's delivery chain and someone else's floor space to absorb its own output, and it is doing so at the moment when the alternative would have meant building all of that itself.

The timing deserves attention too. A disclosure one day before a quarterly report is rarely accidental. Google Cloud is now growing at close to double digits on a percentage basis, the custom chip is repeatedly cited as a driver, and the chip has stopped being an experiment that needs defending. Resolving the channel question before the results land is in itself part of the answer to whatever investors ask about that contribution.

Look further out and one question becomes hard to avoid. If custom silicon can genuinely be obtained through third-party clouds, the assumption that compute comes from a single supplier stops holding. Buyers begin comparing the same generation of accelerator across different providers rather than comparing providers wholesale. For the industry that is a healthy development, because it means the custom chip has to win on open-market merit instead of on the flattering evidence of internal usage. Efficiency comparisons that the industry has long avoided are exactly the kind that, once they actually happen, tend to squeeze the most expensive participant first.
