## Core Summary

California Governor Gavin Newsom publicly stated on September 17 that he is seriously considering convening a state legislature special session or issuing an executive order to accelerate the construction of a regulatory framework for artificial intelligence technology. This statement demonstrates the urgency felt by the California state government in addressing the employment impacts, information authenticity issues, and ethical risks brought by the rapid proliferation of generative AI. California may become one of the first US states to establish a systematic AI regulatory system.

## Event Details

According to Politico's reporting, Governor Newsom clearly stated in recent public remarks that California cannot continue waiting for unified federal-level legislation on AI regulation and must take proactive action. He noted that California has previously signed multiple AI-related laws, including requirements to disclose AI-generated content and strengthen worker rights protections, but these scattered legal documents remain insufficient to address the comprehensive challenges posed by current AI technology.

The options Newsom proposed include two: first, convening a state legislature special session to focus on reviewing and passing comprehensive AI regulatory bills; second, issuing executive orders to take a series of regulatory measures within existing legal frameworks. Each option has its pros and cons: a special session can quickly advance the legislative process but requires sufficient support in the legislature; executive orders can take effect immediately but have relatively limited legal force and long-term stability.

This decision is driven by the unique pressures California faces as the center of the US technology industry. The state, home to Silicon Valley, hosts a large concentration of globally leading AI enterprises whose products and services are profoundly transforming the job market, information dissemination, and social ethics foundations. At the same time, California also serves as the most extensive social testing ground for AI technology applications, with various AI-related social issues first emerging here.

Notably, California has already been at the forefront among US states in AI regulation. Earlier this year, Newsom signed a new law requiring political parties and political advertisements to disclose AI-generated content. Additionally, California is advancing multiple protective legislative efforts regarding worker interactions with AI. These scattered legislative efforts are laying the foundation for establishing a more systematic regulatory framework.

Newsom's latest statement comes at a time when federal-level AI legislation progress remains slow in the US. While Congress has held discussions on AI regulation across multiple committee levels, no binding federal law has been formed to date. Against this backdrop, the necessity for unilateral action by states like California has risen significantly.

## Panoramic Perspective

California's potential regulatory action carries important national demonstration significance. California has long ranked at the top of US states in economic output, and its regulatory policies often become templates for other states to follow. Once California takes the lead in establishing a systematic AI regulatory framework, other states are likely to follow with similar rules, creating a de facto "California Standard" that dominates US AI regulation.

The deeper impact lies in the global competitiveness of the US AI industry. Overly strict state-level regulation may increase compliance costs for enterprises and affect the innovative vitality of startups; but if effective regulation is lacking, AI technology abuse could trigger serious social problems that ultimately damage the industry's sustainable development. The balance challenge Governor Newsom faces is actually the core dilemma that governments worldwide are jointly confronting.

From the perspective of federal-state power allocation, California's move may further exacerbate the "fragmentation" risk in US AI regulation. If states adopt different regulatory standards, AI enterprises will face complex compliance challenges, with product launch costs and legal risks both rising significantly. This situation may prompt the federal government to accelerate unified legislation, but in the short term it is difficult to change the pattern of states acting on their own.

Of greater strategic significance is that California's regulatory action may have spillover effects on global AI governance. The EU has already established a relatively comprehensive regulatory framework through the AI Act, and if California introduces similar comprehensive legislation, it will create parallel European and American regulatory systems. Global AI enterprises operating in both markets will need to meet both sets of standards simultaneously, further raising industry compliance thresholds.

## Multiple Perspectives

**Governor Newsom's Position:** Newsom emphasized that California must become a leader in addressing AI challenges and cannot wait for federal-level consensus. He noted that AI technology development speed far outpaces traditional legislative processes, requiring governments to adopt more flexible and forward-looking regulatory approaches. At the same time, he also acknowledged the need to find a balance between protecting public interests and maintaining innovative vitality.

**California Tech Enterprise Reaction:** Some large tech companies expressed support for a special session, believing that a systematic regulatory framework helps clarify enterprise compliance boundaries and reduce legal uncertainty. However, some startups and venture capital institutions worry that overly strict regulation may suppress innovation and affect California's global leadership position in AI.

**Civil Society Group Views:** Multiple civil rights organizations welcomed Newsom's statement, believing that AI technology has already had profound impacts on employment, privacy, elections, and other fields, urgently requiring proactive government regulation. However, some technical experts also warned that over-regulation may unnecessarily restrict AI technology applications and affect overall societal welfare.

**Federal Government Stance:** The White House has not yet formally responded to California's potential action. However, federal officials have previously repeatedly stated their support for taking a "flexible and risk-based" approach to AI regulation, opposing overly rigid unified rules. This stance has some tension with the more proactive regulatory posture California may adopt.

**International Comparison Perspective:** The EU has already taken the lead in AI regulation, with the AI Act performing risk-graded management on various AI applications. California's potential regulatory framework may draw on EU experience, but given differences in US political systems and legal traditions, the final regulatory path may exhibit distinct local characteristics.

**Academic Analysis:** Multiple scholars in law and AI noted that California's potential regulatory action carries important experimental significance, with its actual effects providing important reference for other states and even federal-level legislation. However, scholars also warned that the complexity and rapid evolution of AI technology means any regulatory framework needs to maintain the capability for continuous iteration.
