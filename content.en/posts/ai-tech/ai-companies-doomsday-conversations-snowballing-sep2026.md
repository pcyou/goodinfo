**[Core Summary]** A New York Times investigation published September 12 found that internal conversations about artificial general intelligence loss-of-control and human extinction scenarios have significantly intensified inside leading US AI companies, expanding from small safety research groups into product teams and board-level discussions. Citing more than ten current and former executives, researchers, and government officials, the report indicates that concerns about the current pace of capability advancement and product release cycles have deepened at OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind, with several senior researchers recently expressing dissent through resignations or public statements. The investigation concludes that this internal panic is spreading to the public at a pace approaching a mainstream topic and could materially shape global AI regulatory discussions and capital market expectations in the coming months.

### Event Details

**Reporting foundation.** The New York Times investigation is based on interviews with more than ten current and former AI executives, researchers, and government officials, spanning institutions including OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta's AI research division, and xAI. The report notes that while these companies maintain publicly optimistic stances on AI safety governance and controllable development, the tone of internal conversations has shifted materially, with multiple executives describing the relevant discussions as having "upgraded from a quarterly agenda item to a weekly agenda item."

**Key triggering events.** The investigation maps several triggering factors over the past three months. A senior Anthropic researcher recently resigned citing concerns about "out-of-control AI development," with the resignation letter explicitly flagging concerns over the scale of training data and the speed of compute expansion in cutting-edge models. After media coverage of the resignation spread, it triggered wide discussion in Silicon Valley technical circles and the AI ethics community, viewed as a public warning from the AI safety community about the industry's current race-style development pattern.

**Diffusion pathway.** The investigation notes that "doomsday conversations" inside AI companies are no longer confined to dedicated AI safety research teams and are now appearing in product planning meetings, model release evaluation meetings, and even board-level strategic discussions. One person close to a leading company's board disclosed that directors have recently been frequently asking executives for specific details on "worst-case response plans," including contingency plans for model loss-of-control, critical infrastructure attacks by AI systems, and mass unemployment scenarios.

**Industry reaction is divided.** Reactions across industry and academia to the rapid spread of this "doomsday mood" are mixed. One camp views this as a sign of AI industry maturation, meaning practitioners are beginning to take long-term risks seriously; another worries that over-focus on extreme risks may obscure more immediate issues such as algorithmic bias, privacy violations, and labor displacement, and may even be used as a discourse tactic to divert regulatory attention in commercial competition.

### Broader Perspective

The intensification of internal panic inside AI companies disclosed by The New York Times is not an isolated event but the cumulative result of multiple structural factors, with impact extending well beyond the discourse level of Silicon Valley technical circles and reshaping global AI governance policy discussions and public perception.

**Structural imbalance between technical progress speed and safety research cadence.** Current leading AI companies operate on quarterly or even monthly product release cycles, while the matching safety assessment, red-teaming, and interpretability research typically runs on annual cycles. This structural imbalance keeps tension between product and safety teams continuously elevated, particularly as compute and data scale for training the next generation of large models expand exponentially. Internal skepticism about whether companies truly understand the systems they are deploying has visibly deepened, indirectly confirmed by multiple insiders in this investigation.

**Double impact of AI safety talent loss and commercialization pressure.** The resignation mentioned in the report from an Anthropic researcher is not isolated. Over the past year, multiple senior researchers focused on AI safety, alignment, and interpretability have left leading firms; some moved into academia or non-profits, others founded independent AI safety consultancies. This talent outflow resonates with continuously rising commercialization pressure on AI companies, forcing them into difficult trade-offs between "safety first" and "first-mover advantage," further intensifying anxiety inside executive decision-making layers.

**Regulatory pressure from theoretical frameworks to concrete rules.** The internal discussions disclosed by The New York Times form a mutually reinforcing relationship with the accelerated AI legislative processes in the United States, European Union, and United Kingdom. While federal-level AI legislation in the US has stalled, multiple states have pushed forward on AI transparency and accountability laws. The EU's AI Act implementing rules have entered final consultations. The UK has launched a national-level institution dedicated to frontier model safety assessment. These regulatory developments in turn push AI companies to take internal risk assessment more seriously, catalyzing the intensification of "doomsday conversations."

**Long-term social impact from shifting public perception.** The most noteworthy signal in the report is that this anxiety, originally confined to corporate interiors, has for the first time entered public view at a pace approaching that of mainstream topics. Once AI loss-of-control risk becomes part of daily public discussion, it will have profound impact on the rollout of AI products in high-sensitivity sectors such as education, healthcare, law, and finance, and will accelerate legal and social pressure on companies to disclose risk boundaries in product documentation. From a broader perspective, the AI industry may be going through a "scientific self-warning" phase similar to early nuclear energy and genetic engineering, where the gap between technical capability and social acceptance will shape the AI industry's expansion pace over the next decade.

### Comparison of Perspectives

**Official statements from leading AI companies** continue to project a cautiously optimistic tone, emphasizing "responsible deployment" and "continuous safety research" as core corporate values, with recent safety policy updates and newly established safety advisory committees framed as positive signals. But the internal information disclosed by The New York Times shows a marked temperature gap between official statements and internal anxiety.

**The AI safety community and departed researchers** generally believe that current regulation and corporate self-discipline are far from sufficient. Some scholars have called for the establishment of a global AI governance body modeled on the International Atomic Energy Agency, with mandatory international notification and assessment mechanisms for frontier model training. This camp has spoken out densely in top-tier journals such as Nature and Science and in mainstream media.

**AI researchers critical of the "doomsday narrative"** point out that over-focusing on extinction scenarios may be exploited by commercial interest groups as a smokescreen to avoid addressing more pressing issues such as algorithmic discrimination, privacy violations, and labor displacement. This camp emphasizes that the immediate priority for AI governance is solving observable and quantifiable real-world harms, rather than highly speculative extinction scenarios.

**National regulatory bodies** have reacted differently. The EU tends to view this trend as an opportunity to accelerate the implementation of the AI Act. The UK has folded it into the core agenda of its frontier model assessment agency. The US federal level remains stuck amid political divisions, while state-level activity is fragmented but active. Overall, the global regulatory system has not yet formed a systematic response framework for "doomsday scenarios," but related discussions have entered the substantive phase of policy making.
