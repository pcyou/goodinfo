## Key Facts

Ahead of the midterm elections, OpenAI hired Jessica Schumer, daughter of Democratic Senator Chuck Schumer, and announced it would start calling its data centers "compute campuses." For the past two years, many of Silicon Valley's largest names built closer ties with the Trump administration; some scaled back diversity and inclusion programs, and some executives donated millions to super PACs supporting the administration and allied candidates.

Now that the midterms have shifted the balance of power in Washington, those companies are working to rebuild ties with Democrats, who have pledged to tighten AI oversight. J.B. Branch, director of federal AI governance and technology policy at Public Citizen, told TechCrunch the industry "realizes they have an optics problem" and characterised moves like the Schumer hire and the rebranding as "PR washing."

## Wider Perspective

The election result pushes this two-way courtship from posture into substance. Democrats have pledged tighter AI oversight, and the real cost of new regulation ultimately lands on corporate compliance teams, legal budgets and product schedules. For technology companies, opening channels to people and policy early costs far less than reacting passively once legislation reaches the stage of amendment. Personnel moves and rebranding are low-cost signals, and their practical function is to reserve a place in the subsequent lobbying and policy advocacy. The industry has been distributing resources across both parties for years, but the two parties do not agree on specifics, which means corporate policy spending now carries a structural double burden.
