## Key Facts

- The company's IPO filing identified the reliability of natural gas supply as a key near-term risk, warning that the data center gas buildout could drive prices higher in some regions and affect its ambitions.
- The filing says competition from other gas-fired data centers could leave the company exposed to fuel price volatility and supply disruptions outside its control, potentially driving up regional gas prices and impairing its ability to contract for firm fuel supply.
- An energy analyst firm estimates behind-the-meter gas projects in development reached 110 gigawatts as of October 2026, up from just 20 gigawatts in July 2025.
- One financial data research house estimates grid-connected data centers alone could add 15 billion cubic feet per day of natural gas demand by 2035, a 15 percent increase in overall demand, with onsite capacity in the pipeline adding roughly 3 billion cubic feet per day by the end of the next decade.
- Export demand is expected to double over the next decade, on top of an already tight market, as terminals ship more gas abroad for geopolitical and policy reasons.
- The company has traditionally rented compute from major cloud providers, with Amazon, Google, Microsoft and SpaceX specifically named in the filing, but has moved to directly lease and develop data centers in order to control its own capacity.
- The filing singles out a $50 billion partnership with a data center development and operations provider, and the company has announced sites in Texas and New York as well as ties to large onsite gas projects in Louisiana, Texas and West Virginia.
- The prospectus mentions newer generation sources such as solar, wind, battery storage, nuclear and geothermal power only to dismiss them, arguing they may not be available in the required quantities or on the required timelines, or only at higher cost.

## Panoramic Analysis

The significant detail in this filing is not that the risk list grew an item. It is who wrote it and where. For years, model companies disclosed risks clustered around compute supply, regulation and competition, while energy prices sat in the filings of cloud providers and data center operators. A frontier lab now writing a fuel price into its own risk factors means that layer of cost has been absorbed into the cost of compute.

The scale helps. Behind-the-meter gas projects in development went from roughly 20 gigawatts in July 2025 to 110 gigawatts by October 2026, more than a fivefold rise in about fifteen months. Set that against the research estimate that grid-connected data centers alone could add 15 billion cubic feet per day of demand by 2035, roughly fifteen percent of total consumption, and the structure of the problem becomes clear: demand is arriving in a burst while supply takes years to form.

What makes this difficult rather than merely tight is geographic overlap. The regions where data centers cluster are also where export terminals and populated areas sit, and a regional gas price is not a forward contract that can simply be locked away, because pipeline capacity is itself finite. When dozens of developers compete for the same corridor, the cost of securing firm supply rises to the point where projects stop clearing. For a model company, that is not an abstract cost curve; it is a delay to compute delivery.

The second-order path deserves attention because it reaches beyond the industry. A researcher noted that if hub prices rise without legislation to intervene, there is a chance the cost reaches consumers. That makes the energy bill for artificial intelligence infrastructure a household heating cost, which is to say a ballot-box variable. Once that feedback loop starts, the pace of expansion stops being a purely commercial choice, and the sector's policy exposure increases in a way that has not yet been priced into the buildout plans.

The strategic logic behind the shift is nonetheless coherent. Renting compute from cloud providers means accepting someone else's pace, someone else's supply chain and someone else's fuel exposure. Direct development buys determinism, and a company facing genuine demand will pay for determinism when the alternative is waiting. The move is a rational response to a scarcity of delivery capacity, not a misreading of risk.

What the filing exposes, however, is a shared exposure that no single company has fully priced. When frontier labs cluster their facilities in states with cheap gas, they have also concentrated themselves on the same fuel pipeline. Facilities in different states look diversified, but the commodity underneath them is one. That common risk line is not yet visible in most model company risk factors, and given how quickly the behind-the-meter buildout is scaling, it will not stay invisible for long.

## Competing Views

**The company's own disclosure** concentrates on fuel and pipeline capacity. The filing states plainly that competition among users for the same natural gas supply and pipeline capacity in those areas is expected to intensify, that this could drive up regional prices, and that it may impair the ability to contract for firm supply. It does not hide the cost of its own transition: renting compute from providers avoided energy exposure, while developing facilities directly bought control and took on the price.

**The analyst view** is more granular. The head of gas research for the Americas expects it would not be surprising to see gas become more expensive in the states where export projects concentrate, and argues that while markets are unlikely to reach the extremes of a prior energy crisis, a rise in hub prices could reach consumers absent legislation, since producers learned from that episode to increase output and push prices back down.

**The feasibility of alternatives** explains why the filing dismisses other generation sources rather than ignoring them. The prospectus concedes that newer generation technologies may not be available in the quantities or on the timelines the company requires, or may be available only at higher cost. That is a candid constraint rather than a rhetorical flourish, and it is the reason fuel risk appears in the risk factors at all.
