## Core Summary

OpenAI CEO Sam Altman is scheduled to attend the United Nations General Assembly next week and deliver a thematic briefing on artificial intelligence to the UN Security Council. This will be the first time a head of a leading AI company directly addresses the Security Council on AI matters, signaling that AI governance has formally entered the agenda of the world top security decision-making body.

## Event Details

According to provisional UN General Assembly agenda items reported by multiple media outlets, Altman briefing will focus on safety risks of frontier AI systems and pathways for global coordinated governance, covering capability spillover of large language models, AI deployment risks in critical infrastructure scenarios, and feasible paths for cross-border AI regulatory coordination. The briefing is expected to last 20-25 minutes, followed by live questions from Security Council member-state representatives.

Notably, the venue is not the UN General Assembly plenary but the Security Council — a more politically sensitive decision-making body. Observers note that the Council decision to place AI on its formal agenda reflects heightened concerns expressed by multiple member states over the past year about AI misuse risks in cybersecurity, biosecurity, and disinformation domains. OpenAI participation, as a representative of leading AI companies, is being viewed as a key dialogue node between industry and multilateral institutions.

## Full Picture

Altman briefing to the UN Security Council carries significance well beyond an ordinary industry-diplomacy interaction and can be analyzed at three levels:

First, on multilateral governance, the UN Security Council has historically dealt with armed conflict, nuclear proliferation, and international sanctions. AI entry into the Council formal agenda means global security decision-makers now view the potential misuse of AI as a risk source comparable in scale to traditional security threats. This framework shift is irreversible — once AI is incorporated into the security discourse, its subsequent regulatory path will inevitably intersect with export controls, technology restrictions, and even sanctions tools.

Second, on industry-diplomacy interaction, the direct address by heads of leading AI companies to Security Council member states is unprecedented in global tech governance history. OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind have all intensively visited national capitals over the past two years, building dialogue channels with the UK, US, and EU. The Security Council formal address marks an upgrade from bilateral lobbying to open debate within multilateral mechanisms, posing new tests for industry policy influence — companies will face more stringent transparency scrutiny in multilateral settings.

Third, on US-China tech competition, the introduction of AI into the Security Council inevitably carries geopolitical overtones as a core arena for great power competition. The differences between the US (industry self-regulation emphasis) and China (security assessment plus filing emphasis) in AI technology leadership and governance paradigm will be discussed simultaneously at the Council level for the first time, potentially serving as a critical outpost for future US-China AI dialogue or competition mechanisms.

## Comparison of Viewpoints

UN official position: UN Secretary-General Guterres has repeatedly called for a global AI regulatory framework and supports the UN playing a central coordinating role in AI governance. The UN Tech Envoy office believes AI global diffusion speed has significantly outpaced any single country or region regulatory capacity, leaving multilateral mechanisms as the only viable foundation for minimum coordination.

Industry position: OpenAI official statement said it looks forward to sharing the latest thinking on frontier AI safety research and global coordinated governance at the Council. But some AI safety researchers criticize this as overly PR-oriented, arguing that industry should commit to specific verifiable safety standards in multilateral settings rather than generic coordinated governance rhetoric.

Critical view: Some civil society organizations and scholars worry that placing AI on the Security Council agenda may securitize AI governance, providing legitimacy for states to implement technology restrictions, data localization, and even export controls in the name of security. This trend could negatively impact the global AI open-source ecosystem and cross-border research cooperation.
