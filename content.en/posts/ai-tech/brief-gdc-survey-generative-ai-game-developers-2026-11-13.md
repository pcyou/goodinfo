## Key Facts

- **Adoption rate**: Forty percent of surveyed developers say they use generative artificial intelligence as part of their job, up from thirty-six percent in 2025.
- **Scale effect**: Usage was higher among game developers operating at smaller scale.
- **Industry context**: The findings come from the Game Developers Conference annual industry survey and reflect adoption in the development workflow.

## Competing Views

**One reading points to the reality of cost structure.** Larger teams generally already employ dedicated engineering, art and testing staff, so the marginal efficiency gain from artificial intelligence tools is limited; smaller teams must cover the same output with fewer people and therefore have stronger motivation to adopt. That is why penetration runs higher among the smaller teams.

**Another reading focuses on the relationship between job changes and tool adoption.** The survey also notes that wider use of artificial intelligence tools and contraction of related roles are happening at the same time. Even if the causal direction is not yet clear, it suggests the industry is currently more inclined to convert the benefits of the tools into workforce restructuring than into simply expanded output.

## Panoramic Analysis

The most explanatory feature of this data is that the direction runs against intuition. Adoption of generative artificial intelligence among developers is rising, but the adoption intensity is strongest among teams with fewer resources. That says the tools are currently valued mainly for replacing repetitive capacity rather than for compressing judgement-intensive creative work.

For industry structure, this means the diffusion path will run around the incumbent advantages of the largest studios. Big teams can build their own model-assisted pipelines and can absorb added capacity through process and tooling, so the returns from scale expansion are limited for them; for small teams every efficiency gain is a net gain. This makes the pace of diffusion potentially faster than the market expects, because the first transformation is of long-tail supply rather than of the leading tier.

What matters next is the form in which that efficiency gain is ultimately realised: whether it becomes lower development quotes, a greater number of smaller projects, or contraction of headcount. The shape of the industry change depends on the relative weight of those three paths. Four indicators are worth watching. The change in adoption rate in next year's survey. Actual team sizes at small studios. The trend in average pricing for mid-sized projects. And how much artificially generated content appears in shipped titles rather than in prototypes.
