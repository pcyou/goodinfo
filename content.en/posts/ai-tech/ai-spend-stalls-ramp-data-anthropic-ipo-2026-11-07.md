## Key Facts

Ramp data released on Friday shows that enterprise use of artificial intelligence keeps climbing while spending on it has stopped climbing with it. Token spend was essentially flat across August and September, growing 1.5 percent in total, against a 42 percent month-over-month gain in July. October showed a rebound, with token spend up 8.9 percent between September 23 and October 23, but Ramp's chief economist Ara Kharazian argues the headwinds on growth remain strong.

## Event Details

- **The spending curve**: Ramp's data shows token spend flatlined across August and September, rising 1.5 percent in total, compared with a 42 percent monthly increase in July.
- **The top cohort retreats**: The top 1 percent of firms, the heaviest spenders, came in slightly below their summer peak of roughly $10,000 per employee during August and September.
- **The long tail is tiny**: Median firms spend $12 to $15 per employee per month, the top 10 percent spend $600 to $1,000, and the top 1 percent approach $10,000.
- **October reaccelerates**: Token spend rose 8.9 percent from September 23 to October 23, which does not change the trend call.
- **Price cuts are the proximate cause**: OpenAI and Anthropic have both cut prices. One filing indicates that an Anthropic pricing decision slashed revenue growth by roughly $600 million.
- **Competition intensifies**: Meta and Nvidia are introducing enterprise offerings with cheap or open-source models, joined by a range of open-model companies from China and the United States and by lightweight tools for choosing among a few predetermined outputs.
- **Buyer behaviour shifts**: Companies are imposing budget defaults, restricting long-running tasks from expensive models, and routing spend from standard to even cheaper lightweight models.
- **The listing window**: Reporting in the same period indicated that Anthropic's target valuation has been cut from an estimated $1.5 trillion to roughly $1.2 to $1.3 trillion, and that its latest fundraising goal fell by about $10 billion.

## Viewpoints Compared

**The bearish argument rests on customer concentration**. Kharazian noted that almost the entire spend on AI comes from venture-backed, high-growth tech companies. That is fine as long as spend keeps rising, but if it starts to slow because of something in the market or some other factor, those firms will all slow down simultaneously, which could lead to cascading effects. This is a structural fragility: demand is not broad industrial penetration but heavy concentration in a few company types and a handful of use cases.

**The bullish case is that lower prices bought wider adoption**. Price cuts are not inherently bad because they make previously uneconomic use cases viable. Kharazian conceded that a slowdown among the top 1 percent is sufficient to slow revenue entirely despite rising volumes, but he also pointed to the October reacceleration. Where companies constrain spend to cheaper models, the efficiency gain should eventually widen the set of viable use cases.

**A third explanation sits on the buyer side of the ledger**. Beyond price cuts, usage itself has become more disciplined: companies set task budget defaults and shift load from standard models to lightweight ones through routers. Notion co-founder Akshay Kothari captured the condition succinctly: tokens go brrr, costs go ahhh. This explains why spend stalled and simultaneously raises questions about the quality of this growth cycle.

**The sharpest disagreement concerns the next engine**. Investors widely expect personal agents to replace coding agents as the source of new spending. Kharazian is sceptical, noting that personal agents are not as economically productive as coding and will not deliver the nonstop growth the industry needs. Meta and Nvidia pushing cheap open models into the enterprise, he adds, is a persistent squeeze on the head labs.

## Panoramic Analysis

Why this dataset matters is not that it forecasts an AI slowdown, but that it exposes a structural shift in the business model with consequences well beyond a quarter or two of revenue. The industry has long sold usage and spending as the same thing, because the revenue formula appeared to be calls multiplied by unit price. But once price cuts drive unit price down, routers steer calls toward cheaper models, and model capability itself converges rapidly toward the low-cost end, the link between volume and money breaks. The gap between 1.5 percent and 42 percent in Ramp's data is the quantified version of that break.

This is not a script unique to artificial intelligence. Early cloud computing went through the same phase, with usage exploding while unit revenue fell steadily, and the eventual winners were not the companies with the most calls but the ones that redesigned the cost curve so the same workload cost less. Artificial intelligence is replaying that sequence with the time compressed from years to months. The problem today is not a shortage of demand but a value-capture mechanism that is shifting from model scarcity toward cost efficiency.

The timing is what makes this particularly uncomfortable. Anthropic's listing window sits exactly on the hinge point. Reporting from several outlets has its target valuation cut from $1.5 trillion to roughly $1.2 to $1.3 trillion, with fundraising goals trimmed as well. If the pricing logic shifts from growth rate to unit economics, the same growth rate earns a materially different multiple. The market is not asking whether this is a good company. It is asking whether the revenue function for the category still holds.

The customer concentration deserves even more attention than it is getting. Ramp notes that almost all spend comes from venture-backed high-growth tech companies, whose spending cadence is tightly linked to financing conditions. The current moment is one in which the AI trade has just absorbed a violent drawdown and risk appetite has contracted. The pullback at the top 1 percent may therefore not be natural use-case saturation but an exogenous transmission from tighter financing. Once that transmission starts, it removes the highest-multiple, highest-growth-narrative segments first.

As for the most optimistic assumption, that a new technical revolution will reignite spending, the current evidence does not support it. Coding agents emerged only about eighteen months ago as the most commercially viable product category and carried two companies to trillion-dollar valuations. Personal agents have now shipped from every major vendor, but Kharazian's point deserves weight: their economic output is materially lower than coding agents'. Firms can buy plenty of personal agents, but they are not obviously willing to pay coding-agent premiums for them. If that holds, the engine the sector needs is not a smarter model but a method of making the existing models ten times cheaper.

Four indicators deserve watching: whether month-over-month token spend growth holds in the positive single digits for three consecutive months; whether the top 1 percent recovers its roughly $10,000 per-employee monthly spend level; where Anthropic's final listing price lands; and the share of enterprise calls routed to lightweight models. The last of these decides whether price cuts expand the market or merely thin the margin.
