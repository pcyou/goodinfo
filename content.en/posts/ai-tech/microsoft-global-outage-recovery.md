**[Summary]**

Microsoft confirmed that the major technical failure that prevented millions of users worldwide from accessing core services such as Outlook email and Teams meeting tools has been fixed, with services gradually returning to normal. The productivity loss from this disruption is estimated to exceed $7 billion.

**[Details]**

In a statement, Microsoft said it had resolved the "underlying issue" causing the widespread outage and was seeing broad recovery and stability across services in various regions. The company apologized, stating that the outage was caused by a technical issue rather than a cybersecurity breach.

Microsoft explained that "the incident was introduced by an issue during an earlier network capacity upgrade that surfaced following a recent network change." The company said it would release a full post-incident report after review.

The outage began around noon ET on Thursday, affecting users in 74 countries and territories worldwide. Banks, government agencies, healthcare services, railway systems, and courts all reported disruptions related to Microsoft products.

Estimates from the Michigan-based think tank Anderson Economic Group suggest that the productivity loss from this disruption could approach $7 billion. Patrick Anderson, the firm's president, stated that operations of possibly 26 million users on Thursday and 51 million users on Friday were actually affected, causing economic losses.

Anderson also estimated that Microsoft might need to refund customers approximately $800 million per day in subscription fees, as users could not access services they paid for. While not all customers will seek refunds, those who do could push the total cost of the outage above $7 billion.

In the United States, multiple city governments reported services returning. Jacksonville, Florida, stated it was "seeing some services restored this morning and remain cautiously optimistic." Austin and Dallas also reported progress in restoring email services. Arapahoe County Sheriff's Office in Colorado indicated the issue was resolved.

In the UK, several police forces advised the public to report crimes or issues via telephone rather than through their websites. Government employees in Japan resorted to creating physical copies of documents, and courts in the Philippines were hindered by the outage. Schools, courts, and government services in Australia all reported disruptions.

In its latest statement, Microsoft said that queues of emails waiting to be delivered are "significantly reduced and approaching normal levels," but warned that some users may still experience intermittent access issues or delayed email delivery.

**[Perspective]**

This Microsoft global service outage once again highlights the high dependence of modern enterprises on cloud services and its potential risks. From a technical architecture perspective, Microsoft 365, as the most widely used enterprise office suite globally, directly impacts billions of workflows and business operations when its stability is compromised.

From an economic impact standpoint, the $7 billion estimate reveals the vulnerability behind digital workplaces. When core infrastructure fails, it not only causes direct productivity losses but can also trigger chain reactions affecting supply chains, customer service, financial transactions, and other areas. This incident reminds enterprises of the need to establish more robust business continuity plans and backup solutions.

From an industry trend perspective, this outage may accelerate enterprise adoption of multi-cloud strategies and hybrid cloud architectures. More organizations are beginning to realize that over-reliance on a single cloud service provider poses systemic risks. In the future, we may see more enterprises adopting redundant designs for critical business systems to ensure basic operations can be maintained during similar incidents.

Additionally, this event has sparked discussions about cloud service liability definitions. While Microsoft provides certain guarantees through service level agreements, compensation mechanisms often fail to fully compensate enterprises for actual losses when large-scale outages occur. This may prompt regulators to strengthen oversight requirements for cloud service providers and promote further refinement of industry standards.
