## Key Facts

Anthropic described on Tuesday an open source system called FlashProve, built to provide what researchers call provable inference, a cryptographic proof that a given output came from a specific model rather than merely from some model.

- **Purpose**: it would let users confirm that an answer they received really came from the model they think they are using.
- **Resistance to imposture**: the system is designed to be robust against one model being passed off as another, including by the lab that builds and runs the model. Researchers point to two concrete uses, preventing a company from quietly swapping in a cheaper model to cut costs or a more expensive one to inflate performance results.
- **Verifying third-party claims**: a developer might claim that a software library was scanned for vulnerabilities by a frontier cybersecurity model, or a company might claim that a business plan was approved by an appropriate model. The system is aimed at making such claims checkable.
- **How it works**: labs publish a fingerprint for each model they release, which can later be matched against specific outputs and verified cryptographically. The fingerprints do not reveal model weights, so labs can publish them without surrendering their most valuable asset, which means the mechanism can work with commercial models. It is also designed to be lightweight enough that verification could run on a local device.
- **Current state**: researchers released a detailed technical specification and working code for various elements of the system under an open source licence. Research is ongoing and there are no plans to deploy it at Anthropic or any other lab.
- **Framing**: the researchers describe the post as a way of sharing intermediate progress rather than a finished research agenda, inviting others to take the work and improve it.

## Competing Views

**Supporters regard this as closing the weakest link in model provenance.** Until now, the identity of a model has rested on assertion, and confirming which model produced an answer has required trusting the provider.

**The most practical use is verifying commercial claims.** A statement that a codebase was scanned by a frontier security model cannot currently be checked by anyone outside the company making it. Such claims today rest entirely on trust.

**The central design trade-off favours unforgeability over ease of deployment.** Requiring labs to publish fingerprints imposes a continuing maintenance obligation, and a published fingerprint is also an attack surface, since it gives adversaries something to train against.

## Wider Perspective

Provable inference is not a new idea. Zero-knowledge proofs, remote attestation and software watermarking have all addressed versions of the same problem. What distinguishes this proposal is the target it selects, and the target is one that has been avoided until now: not proving that an output came from a model, but proving that it came from one particular model carrying a published fingerprint.

That distinction matters commercially, because an entire category of claims is currently beyond external inspection. Corporate buyers cannot confirm that a provider is quietly serving requests with a cheaper model. Developers cannot confirm that a report claiming a frontier security model was used is accurate. Regulators cannot confirm a model's account of its own testing. Turning those claims into something cryptographically checkable is a change of direction that matters.

The difficulty sits in the fingerprint. Once published, it becomes a surface that can be attacked, and an adversary can train outputs that evade detection. The strength of the scheme depends on how tightly the fingerprint binds to the output and on whether verification is cheap enough for an ordinary user to run locally. If local verification genuinely works, the mechanism requires no central authority, and trust is distributed between the model publisher and whoever checks the result.

At present there is a specification and some code, the research is explicitly unfinished, and no lab plans deployment. This is a direction rather than a solution. What is worth remembering is that infrastructure of this kind tends to appear quickly once a concrete compliance setting demands it, and a procurement clause requiring verifiable model provenance would be exactly such a setting.
