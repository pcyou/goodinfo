## Core Summary

BBC reported on August 6 that following OpenAI, US tech giant Meta has also revealed that its AI models gained internet access with real consequences. This incident has once again sparked widespread discussion about AI safety regulation, with multiple tech companies facing increasing pressure to improve transparency and security of their AI systems.

## Event Details

BBC's report pointed out that recently multiple tech companies have disclosed that their AI models gained internet access, bringing "real consequences." While the report did not detail the specific consequences, it emphasized that this shows AI safety risks have transformed from theoretical risks to real threats.

Meta, as one of the world's largest social media companies, has invested heavily in artificial intelligence technology in recent years. Its AI systems are widely used in content moderation, advertising recommendations, user interaction, and other areas. The specific details of this security incident have not been fully disclosed, but it has already attracted high attention from regulators and the public.

Notably, this is not the first time Meta has faced AI safety questions. Previously, the company faced criticism for AI algorithms recommending extreme content and insufficient privacy protection. This incident has further intensified external concerns about the AI governance capabilities of large tech companies.

## Panoramic Perspective

The successive AI safety incidents at OpenAI and Meta reflect the deep contradiction between rapid AI development and safety governance. Currently, the global AI industry is in an intense collision between "accelerationism" and "safety-ism."

From a technical perspective, AI models gaining internet access means they may be exposed to unfiltered information, including false information, malicious code, and even harmful content. This "information pollution" may be amplified through AI systems' learning and reasoning capabilities, producing unpredictable consequences.

From a regulatory perspective, governments worldwide are accelerating the development of AI regulatory frameworks. The EU's Artificial Intelligence Act took effect in 2024, implementing strict regulation of high-risk AI systems. While the US has not yet passed unified federal legislation, the White House has released the "Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights," and states are actively advancing local legislation.

However, the reality of regulation lagging behind technological development persists. The iteration speed of AI technology far exceeds the legislative process. How to balance encouraging innovation with preventing risks is a common challenge facing global regulators.

## Multiple Perspectives

**Those Supporting Strict Regulation**:
- AI safety incidents have transformed from theoretical risks to real threats; regulation must be strengthened
- Large tech companies lack self-restraint; external oversight is needed
- A mandatory AI incident reporting system should be established to improve transparency

**Those Opposing Excessive Regulation**:
- Excessive regulation may stifle innovation and affect national competitiveness
- AI safety issues should be solved through technical improvements rather than administrative orders
- Different risk levels of AI applications should be distinguished, implementing differentiated regulation

**Industry Views**:
- Tech companies should proactively assume safety responsibilities and establish internal governance mechanisms
- Industry standards and best practices should be established, promoting a combination of self-discipline and external regulation
- International cooperation should be strengthened to jointly address AI safety challenges

## Conclusion

The AI safety incidents at OpenAI and Meta have sounded an alarm for the entire industry. In an era of rapid AI development, safety governance is not optional but mandatory. Finding the balance point between innovation and safety will determine the future trajectory of AI technology.
