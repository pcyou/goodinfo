## Key Facts

As technology companies try to teach chatbots to tell right from wrong, more of them are turning to religious traditions rather than relying only on rule sets engineered in-house. Reuters reports that some firms are working with philosophers and religious leaders to draw judgments about good and evil from different faith systems into the process of aligning models.

Chris Olah, a co-founder of Anthropic, said earlier this year during the presentation at the Vatican of the papal encyclical on artificial intelligence by Pope Leo XIV that these are not questions a laboratory can answer on its own. They are questions that traditions such as these have been dealing with for millennia. Public attention to what exactly these companies mean by religion, and more importantly to what they expect to gain from the approach, has remained limited.

## Comparison of Viewpoints

- **Technology camp**: Argues that models need a stable capacity for value judgment, and that quantifiable alignment metrics alone cannot cover the complexity of real-world situations.
- **Religious and philosophical camp**: Believes long-standing ethical traditions offer a more complete framework for answering such questions, while generally declining to endorse specific commercial products.
- **Media assessment**: Notes that the industry has reached outward for guidance while disclosing very little about its own intent, which makes external oversight difficult to formalize into standards.

## Panoramic Analysis

Bringing religious thought into the value calibration of artificial intelligence looks at first like a choice of technical method, but in substance it is an admission of the industry's own limits. When engineers find that models repeatedly confront judgments which cannot be dissolved by a loss function, the cheapest response is to keep stacking rules; the more honest response is to acknowledge that some questions genuinely require reference to long-standing external experience.

At least three consequences follow. First, the main arena of model alignment is shifting outward, from laboratory benchmarks to the discourse of philosophical and religious institutions, which means technical review will increasingly be judged against non-engineering standards. Second, the question of who defines values moves out of corporate conference rooms and into the public domain; once a model's moral preferences are grounded in human civilizational traditions, algorithmic governance inevitably pulls cultural and policy debates into the frame. Third, the borrowing is not free for companies: claiming to have adopted a value system means having to answer questions about real intent, and thin disclosure only amplifies outside scrutiny.

The report's central gap is not methodological but one of transparency. How the industry explains what it borrowed and what it wants from that borrowing will determine whether the path is read as responsible exploration or as rhetoric for repackaging controversy. That clarity of self-accounting may say more about technical maturity than which particular school of thought was adopted.
