## Key Facts

Media outlets have recently received pitches from data brokers offering to sell their historic work records to artificial intelligence labs. One pitch offered $350,000 to $1.8 million as a one-time fee for documents, emails, Slack messages and ticket archives for training better models. Another extended the list to include call transcripts and pushed the figure toward $2 million, noting that public data is exhausted and labs are spending over $45 billion on private data.

## Event Details

- **The pitch**: Pitches received by media outlets ranged from $350,000 to $1.8 million for historic work records. Another, adding call transcripts, reached a possible $2 million, stating that public data is exhausted and labs are spending over $45 billion on private data.
- **Domain expertise as the pitch**: A firm called Handshake confirmed the outreach and said it licenses professional documents across many fields so labs can test and train how models handle real knowledge work, noting that journalism is one of those fields. It also said a large part of its business is buying graduate students' papers and coursework.
- **Buying the ruins of failed companies**: A company called Sunset described its business as helping startups wind down, handling the full process, scrubbing and anonymizing the data so company people and identifying details are stripped out, then brokering the deal with the labs.
- **The leaders**: Handshake, Micro1 and Mercor are snapping up databases as if it were a modern gold rush. Mercor, founded in 2023, saw its three cofounders become billionaires last year, and claims partnerships with just about all major labs. Anthropic acknowledged a big market for data but said it does not buy workflow from live companies; OpenAI did not respond before publication.
- **Market size**: Micro1 CEO Ali Ansari said labs, enterprises and robotics companies pay about $40 to $50 billion a year for data, and that within two to four years this will exceed a trillion dollars annually. The reporting noted this figure could not be independently verified.
- **The biggest sale**: A corpus of 80,000 email accounts, 500 million internal messages, 20.6 million SharePoint files and a million time-card records drew a $10 million bid from Google before Micro1 topped it by $2.5 million, triggering an argument over who won. The sale is presently on hold.
- **Union objections**: The flight attendants' union objected to a sale involving their members' private information. Mayu Tobin-Miyaji, general counsel of the Electronic Privacy Information Center, said emails and Teams messages contain unstructured data that is harder to root out, and may include messages where employees discuss medical appointments or family issues.
- **The privacy assurance**: Every data company claims no personal identifying information enters the models. Mercor CEO Brendan Foody said personal information is stripped out when a business streams data to the company and that this de-identification can be verified.
- **Quality as the rationale**: Mercor began as an AI recruiting company and learned that the labs were frustrated by the quality of available data. Earlier approaches hired experts to simulate real workflow, but that does not capture the messiness of an actual operating business. Foody's framing is that big AI has two bottomless needs, compute and data.

## Viewpoints Compared

**The industry argument is about quality rather than quantity**. Foody's account is that labs were not short of data so much as disappointed by it, and that simulated workflows systematically miss the messiness of a real operating business. The framing is that compute receives enormous public investment while data gathering, equally essential, is barely discussed.

**The optimistic case on costs holds that falling prices and scale will open new use cases**. Ansari accepts that selling workflow data to big AI companies may end up more common than ads as a revenue line, but argues such objections will be manageable because workers will see that better models will make their own lives easier. He asks observers to weigh the upside and the abundance AI can produce.

**The critical case focuses on whether anonymisation actually works**. Tobin-Miyaji's objection is that emails and chat messages are unstructured data that anonymisation cannot fully purge, and that some messages contain medical appointments or family issues. Venture capitalist Gordon Ritter points at the missing standards: the AI industry is moving far faster than our ability to set standards, and it is partially intentional.

**The workers' position is the most direct of all**. The flight attendants' union president framed the trade plainly: this is data that became valuable in the age of AI in order to train models to replace jobs and commodify other portions of working in a work environment. Asked whether selling workflow data will become standard practice, Ansari answered affirmatively.

## Panoramic Analysis

What is most unsettling about this thread is that it inverts the grand narrative of the compute race into a contest over the raw material of labour. When industry discusses AI bottlenecks over the past two years, almost all attention has gone to chips and electricity, and few have wanted to face the second constraint: the absolute supply of high-quality data is running out. The marginal utility of public corpora has clearly decayed, while the documents, tickets, chat logs and transcripts generated inside real companies carry precisely the form of knowledge models find hardest to synthesise, namely specific judgements made by specific people under specific constraints. Buying these in bulk trains models on other organisations' institutional memory, which is a way of training replacements on someone else's material.

The most uncomfortable feature of this market is how the raw material is obtained. Data companies tell a consistent story: the material comes from closed businesses, academic paper archives and acquired team records, and therefore has nothing to do with current employees. That claim is technically defensible and ethically hollow, because the value of those records derives from the people who wrote them, and those people were neither informed nor asked nor compensated. The union objection is therefore not moral panic. It is a concrete question of ownership.

The second dimension worth interrogating is the credibility of the market forecast. A projection that a market of $40 to $50 billion a year will exceed a trillion dollars annually within two to four years is extraordinarily steep, and figures of this kind are common in current AI narratives, though the reporting explicitly notes the number could not be independently verified. The verifiable part is structural: the top five percent of firms are spending tens of millions of dollars per quarter, while many middle-market buyers remain in exploratory phases. If the demand really does come from large enterprises, the ceiling on this trade may be far lower than advertised. If middle-market companies begin treating workflow data as a routinely tradable asset, the growth source is not rising AI demand but the commoditisation of data itself.

The genuinely durable implication is the endgame logic that data brokers revealed without quite meaning to. Asked whether corporations will routinely sell their workflow data to big AI companies, Ansari said yes, and expects it to become more common than ads as a revenue line. Data brokers excel at turning one-off, irregular assets into standardised, repeatedly tradable commodities. Once email archives, ticketing systems and call records pass through that standardisation, the question stops being whether companies will sell data and becomes what your work data is worth, who is bidding, and what the take rate is.

The deepest contradiction is that this mechanism profits from the very labour it helps to displace. A freelance writer receiving an offer, a shutdown team being acquired, an editor being asked to hand over the archive of the editorial process are individually small choices, but at industry level they have been standardised into a supply chain. The union president's formulation gets to it: this is data that became valuable in the age of AI in order to train models to replace jobs. That is a self-consuming loop in which one group's working residue trains the tools that displace another group, with the intermediaries taking the entire premium.

Five indicators deserve tracking: whether the major labs publish data procurement and de-identification audit standards; whether regulators and unions force anonymisation verification into mandatory disclosure; the trajectory of legal action by teacher unions and flight attendants unions in particular; how long it takes for individual objections to become industry-wide standards; and whether middle-market companies end up as buyers or sellers in workflow data transactions. The last is the most revealing, because if only large enterprises sell data this remains a niche arbitrage, whereas if smaller firms sell as well it becomes infrastructure-scale restructuring.
