## Key Facts

- Anthropic's filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission disclosed 112.4 billion dollars in cash plus 19.3 billion in short-term investments as of June 30, a total of 131.7 billion.
- That total exceeds every non-bank US company except Berkshire Hathaway and Alphabet, and exceeds Amazon, SpaceX, Meta and Microsoft individually, as well as Nvidia and Apple combined.
- Amazon first backed Anthropic in 2023 with a commitment of up to 4 billion, then structured much of its additional investment as convertible notes, a form of debt that converts into shares at an IPO or sale.
- According to the filing, Amazon holds convertible notes with 62 billion in principal outstanding as of June 30, which is Anthropic's entire debt load.
- Amazon's own filings valued those note holdings at 97.9 billion as of June 30.
- Anthropic recorded the change on its income statement under the line for the fair value of convertible notes, derivative liabilities and simple agreements for future equity, totalling 72.4 billion for the first half of 2026. The charge is non-cash.
- Amazon's notes could convert into as many as 1.84 billion shares, but a cap limits Amazon to 12 percent of Anthropic's partially diluted share count. Amazon can waive the cap.
- Beyond the notes, Amazon invested 10 billion in nonvoting preferred stock in the second quarter, 5 billion in each of Anthropic's last two rounds, bringing its total investment to about 18 billion.
- Anthropic has committed to spend at least 110 billion on Amazon's cloud services over the next 10 years, while Google has secured cloud commitments from Anthropic of at least 114 billion through 2033.
- Anthropic's first-half compute and infrastructure expenses totalled 8.95 billion, while the company disclosed 321.4 billion in estimated future undiscounted lease obligations and 265.6 billion in expected future fixed or minimum-guaranteed commitments.

## Competing Views

The bullish case rests on how unprecedented the structure of this listing is. No company has ever entered the public market carrying a cash position of this magnitude, and the cap on the largest holder's stake protects other shareholders from dilution. The immediate reaction, with compute-related stocks rallying across the board, shows investors reading the filing as evidence of durable demand rather than as a disclosure of risk.

The cautious case watches the gap between two figures. First-half compute and infrastructure spending came to just under nine billion dollars, while the company disclosed estimated undiscounted lease obligations above three hundred billion and fixed or minimum-guaranteed commitments well beyond that, together exceeding half a trillion. The company buys compute twelve to eighteen months ahead of time and the expense only lands once capacity is delivered, so the current charge cannot describe the real burden. A large cash balance is not the same as a small risk; it means the financing ability is strong.

The public and regulatory concern lands on a risk the filing itself concedes. Public resistance to data centres is becoming organised, legislators in sixteen states are considering pauses on construction, and the company states plainly that its projects may attract particular attention. A general shortfall in electricity supply, at national as well as global scale, is listed alongside it.

## Panoramic Analysis

The most interesting thing about this filing is not the size of the cash pile. It is the structure of the financing that produced it.

Amazon's arrangement deserves to be taken apart. It did not take equity directly. It put most of its money into convertible notes, a form of debt that converts into stock at a listing or a sale. The arrangement can be engineered on two axes that matter. Before the offering the notes are debt and carry no vote; after it they convert automatically, but a hard cap of 12 percent limits how much of the company Amazon can ever hold. So Amazon captures nearly the entire gain from the valuation's climb without taking on the governance obligations of a shareholder, and without the dilution that a direct stake would have created. Corporate finance has offered this combination for decades through acquisition debt and minority stakes. What has changed is that it is now the standard way to finance a frontier laboratory, and that the party arranging it is also the vendor of the compute.

The second layer sits on Anthropic's own books. Seventy-two billion four hundred million in fair value changes ran through first-half results, which means the reported earnings of this company can no longer be read against operating performance. For two years the market priced artificial intelligence companies on gross margin and on how quickly losses were narrowing. Both measures become uninformative for a company carrying more than half a trillion in long-term commitments, because the commitments have not yet reached the income statement while the cash to pay for them already sits on the balance sheet. No ready framework exists for reading that accounting, and investors are about to get their first chance to try.

The third layer is the closed loop between models and compute. Anthropic has committed at least 110 billion to Amazon's cloud and Google has secured more than 114 billion. The companies the market treats as model labs now have their cost structures dictated by the capital plans of two cloud providers, while those same cloud providers depend on model companies for the narrative that justifies their own build-out. It looks like competition from the outside. Structurally it is closer to a supply chain that closes on itself: model companies buy compute, compute companies invest in model companies, and the model companies' valuations supply the story that keeps compute valuations funded.

Timing matters too. Anthropic has raised 141 billion in private financing in six and a half years, and is about to become one of the most cash-rich companies ever to list publicly. When a business must keep raising money to honour purchase commitments it has already signed, the right way to evaluate it stops being whether it earns money and becomes whether it can keep raising it. This offering will supply the first real measurement of that question.
