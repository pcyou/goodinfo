## Executive Summary

The Federal Reserve raised the federal funds rate by 25 basis points to a target range of 4.75%–5.00% on September 16, its first rate hike in three years. The decision triggered an immediate sell-off across U.S. equities, with the Dow Jones Industrial Average closing down roughly 700 points and the Nasdaq and S&P 500 posting similar losses. In his first press conference as Chair, Kevin Warsh warned that inflation pressures remain elevated, a hawkish tone that cemented market anxiety about the path of future tightening.

## What Happened

### The Hike Lands

The Federal Open Market Committee voted to lift the target range from 4.50%–4.75% to 4.75%–5.00%, ending three years of accommodative policy. The dot plot indicates one additional hike may be delivered before year-end. Officials cited persistent core services inflation and tight labor markets as justification.

### Warsh's Hawkish Debut

In his inaugural press conference, Chair Kevin Warsh declared that the "last mile" of disinflation "may prove more difficult than previously expected." He noted that core services inflation and wage growth remain above levels consistent with the 2% target. The tone caught markets off guard, with traders parsing his remarks as a clear signal that the Fed is prepared to tolerate slower growth to anchor inflation expectations.

### Market Reaction

Stocks fell sharply within minutes of the release. The Dow shed as much as 750 points intraday before closing near a 700-point loss. The S&P 500 dropped about 1.8% and the Nasdaq fell roughly 2.4%. Technology and energy led the declines, while banks posted modest gains on expectations of wider net interest margins. The dollar index rose above 104, and the 10-year Treasury yield climbed to about 4.45%.

## The Bigger Picture

The September meeting marks both a policy inflection and Warsh's market debut. His hawkish messaging signals a Fed that is willing to accept financial pain to defend price stability. Three implications stand out:

First, U.S. fiscal pressure will intensify. Net interest expense already exceeds defense spending in fiscal 2026, and a sustained 4.75%–5.00% policy rate will widen the budget squeeze further.

Second, global dollar liquidity will tighten. Higher U.S. rates will transmit through exchange rates, yield differentials, and asset prices to emerging markets, with Asian currencies likely to face renewed depreciation pressure.

Third, the era of "cheap money" for technology is over. AI startups dependent on low-cost capital will need to recalibrate their funding assumptions, and consolidation across the sector may accelerate.

## Comparing Perspectives

**Bull case**: Goldman Sachs chief economist Jan Hatzius argues the hike was fully priced in and Warsh's hawkish remarks are a credible-commitment device rather than an extra tightening signal. He views the equity pullback as a healthy de-risking.

**Cautious case**: Morgan Stanley strategist Mike Wilson warns that sticky core services inflation gives Warsh limited room to pivot dovish. He sees meaningful downside risk to fourth-quarter earnings estimates and prefers defensive positioning.

**Policy view**: NEC Director Kevin Hassett said on social media that the "last mile" is typically the hardest part of a tightening cycle and reaffirmed his support for Fed independence.
