**[Key Facts]**

The UK chancellor rejected calls to align capital gains tax with income tax rates on October 20, a proposal pushed by influential figures within the Labour Party. Critics had warned that such a move could drive wealthy individuals out of Britain. Chancellor John Healey will set out his tax plans in the Budget on October 28.

**[What We Know]**

- **The decision**: The chancellor declined to equalise capital gains tax rates with income tax rates, a demand previously advanced by influential Labour figures.
- **Primary concern**: Critics argued that equalising the rates would raise the cost of realising gains and could prompt high-net-worth individuals to relocate capital overseas.
- **Timing**: The Budget will be delivered on October 28, when the specifics of the tax package will be set out.

**[Wider Perspective]**

The choice of capital gains tax rates is ultimately a trade-off between fiscal take and the stability of the tax base. Equalising rates could raise the headline yield substantially, and because it touches a narrow group of high-net-worth individuals it carries strong political framing. But asset values are highly sensitive to rate changes, and with cross-border capital mobility now easier than at any point in recent decades, a higher charge on realisation is more likely to shift the tax base than to expand it. The chancellor's decision reflects a clear-eyed recognition of that constraint: for an economy with strengths in financial and professional services, the mobility of the tax base matters more than the level of any single rate.

The episode also illustrates how constrained the Budget has become. With growth under pressure and government borrowing costs elevated, the Treasury must navigate a narrow space between sustaining public services, easing cost-of-living pressure and containing the deficit. Deferring any adjustment to capital gains tax means this revenue line cannot be expected to contribute meaningfully in the near term, pushing attention onto alternative ways to close the gap, including spending reprioritisation and selective tightening of other tax bases. Debate around the October 28 Budget is therefore likely to centre on which groups are asked to bear more, rather than on whether taxes rise at all.
