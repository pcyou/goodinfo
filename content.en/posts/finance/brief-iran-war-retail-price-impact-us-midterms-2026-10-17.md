**[Core Summary]**

U.S. retail and industry data show that the energy-price spike triggered by the Iran war is being passed through to the retail side, with broad increases in gasoline, household goods, and food prices. U.S. households face continued consumption pressure in the weeks before the midterm elections, with some voters treating inflation and disposable-income concerns as their top voting priorities.

**[Event Details]**

- **Energy pass-through**: Affected by the Iran war, global crude-oil and shipping costs have risen, U.S. gasoline retail prices hit a year-high in many states, increasing household travel and logistics expenses.
- **Food and household goods**: Prices for edible oils, processed food, and household goods have risen in tandem, with retailers passing some energy and transport costs on to consumers.
- **Consumer pressure**: Multiple consumer-confidence indices show that low- and middle-income households are markedly more worried about essential spending, with some families cutting discretionary purchases to keep to budget.
- **Political agenda**: Inflation and affordability have become the central issues before the midterm elections, with both parties' candidates fiercely debating energy policy and the cost of military action against Iran.
- **Policy response**: The White House and Congress are discussing short-term tools such as fuel subsidies and strategic-reserve releases, but market expectations are for a relatively restrained policy response that cannot quickly reverse the upward price trend.

**[Panorama]**

The Iran war's retail-price pass-through reveals the amplifying effect of energy-price shocks in political economy. Geopolitical conflicts act on household budgets through the energy market and then on election politics through voter sentiment; this transmission chain has appeared repeatedly in past energy crises. In the short term, measures such as fuel subsidies and strategic-reserve releases can relieve price-upward pressure but cannot change the underlying supply-demand picture. In the medium term, the pace of U.S. energy-mix diversification and manufacturing reshoring will directly affect household sensitivity to inflation, while election politics may further polarize the direction of federal energy policy. Over the long term, the speed of the energy transition and grid modernization will determine U.S. resilience to external geopolitical shocks, and the related policy games are likely to become central to the U.S. political-economy agenda in coming years.
