## Core Summary

Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney and US President Trump announced on August 20 that both countries have made "significant progress" on long-standing trade disputes, working to finalize details before the weekend tariff deadline.

According to Bloomberg, the agreement's core includes the US halving Canadian steel and aluminum tariffs from the threatened 50% level, while Canada makes concessions on dairy market access and digital trade rules. This progress temporarily defuses the imminent trade war escalation, but parties remain divided on the agreement's substance and long-term impact.

## Key Details

### Breakthrough Timing

Canadian PM Carney stated at an Ottawa press conference that the past 48 hours of negotiations achieved a "substantive breakthrough." He said both teams are working "around the clock" to complete text before the weekend deadline. Trump claimed on social media it was "a great deal," emphasizing benefits for American workers and farmers.

### Steel and Aluminum Tariff Compromise

Bloomberg, citing sources, reports the core compromise lies in steel and aluminum tariffs. The Trump administration originally threatened 50% punitive tariffs on Canadian steel and aluminum, with the final plan reducing this to approximately 25%, still above previous levels. In exchange, Canada agreed to concessions on dairy import quotas and digital services tax.

### Reactions

Canadian business cautiously welcomed the deal, calling avoidance of 50% tariffs the "best outcome," but expressed concern about dairy market opening. US steel unions criticized the deal as "not strong enough," arguing 25% tariffs remain insufficient to protect American workers.

## Panoramic Analysis

The temporary resolution of Canada-US trade disputes reflects the Trump administration's consistent "pressure to negotiate" pattern. By setting ultimatums and threatening extreme tariffs, trading partners are forced into concessions. This strategy indeed secures better deal terms for the US in the short term, but the long-term cost is continued erosion of ally trust.

From an economic structure perspective, Canada-US trade relations are highly integrated. Bilateral trade exceeds $700 billion annually, with deeply intertwined supply chains. Any tariff barriers produce a "boomerang effect" — harming the other side while also harming oneself. The halved rather than eliminated steel and aluminum tariffs mean manufacturers in both countries will still face additional compliance costs and uncertainty.

The deeper impact lies in this agreement potentially setting a template for Trump's negotiations with other trading partners. The EU, Japan, South Korea and others are closely watching the Canada-US deal details, assessing potential pressure they may face. If this "bilateral pressure, divide and conquer" strategy continues succeeding, the global multilateral trading system will be further weakened.

## Multiple Perspectives

**Canadian Government**: The Carney administration characterized the deal as "successful negotiation protecting Canadian workers' interests," emphasizing the worst case was avoided, but opposition parties criticized excessive concessions in dairy markets.

**US Government**: The Trump team claimed another "America First" policy victory, emphasizing steel and aluminum tariffs will protect American manufacturing employment.

**Canadian Business**: The Canadian Chamber of Commerce expressed "relief" but warned tariff uncertainty will still affect business investment decisions.

**US Unions**: Steelworker unions expressed dissatisfaction with 25% tariffs, arguing this is insufficient to counter Canadian "unfairly subsidized" steel products.

**International Observers**: Most analysts believe the agreement is only a temporary respite — core structural issues (digital trade, subsidy rules) remain unresolved, and future friction will continue.
