## Key Facts

- Zongyuan Zoe Liu, a senior fellow for China studies at the Council on Foreign Relations, writes that Beijing does not want the renminbi to dethrone the dollar as the dominant currency.
- Truly replacing the dollar would require open capital markets, which would threaten the party's control over who gets capital and how, she explains.
- Many elites rely on offshore dollar holdings as a form of insurance against domestic political persecution, which makes that path too risky.
- The real reason for promoting the yuan abroad is to prevent the US from weaponising the financial system, similar to how Russia was shut out after it invaded Ukraine in 2022.
- A yuan network international enough to bypass dollar-based sanctions would suffice, without being dominant, and would constrain Washington's ability to impose sanctions unilaterally.
- China facilitates renminbi use among firms in more than a hundred countries and across a growing array of commodity markets.
- As of June, the Cross-Border Interbank Payment System had 1,829 participants able to send payments to more than 5,200 banks in 191 countries.
- Chinese exporters still invoice in dollars and convert to yuan just before bringing goods into China.
- Beijing is promoting yuan benchmarks on domestic commodity exchanges, and has succeeded with lithium.
- Chinese banks more than doubled their yuan lending to overseas borrowers between 2023 and mid-2026.
- The People's Bank of China has reached currency swap agreements with 32 foreign central banks and monetary authorities.
- State firms have begun settling cargo insurance in yuan, a modest step in risk-transfer markets.
- Reinvestment is the weakest link: in March, foreign investors held just 1.6 percent of the Chinese bond market against 27 percent in the US bond market.

## Competing Views

**The central argument is a cost-benefit comparison.** Liu argues that establishing a respectable second or even a consequential third position would suffice to break the dollar's effective monopoly and constrain Washington's ability to impose sanctions unilaterally, while saving China the effort and cost of sustaining currency supremacy. This explains why China has expanded renminbi settlement networks quickly while advancing capital account opening cautiously.

**The scale gap remains substantial.** 1,829 participants and 5,200 banks sounds large, but it is a small fraction of what the US-dominated system processes. A global currency also entails pricing, financing, risk transfer and reinvestment. The renminbi has made progress on the first three, while reinvestment is the weakest link, because recipients would need to hold and reinvest balances rather than convert them.

**The industrial route is a second path.** By the same logic that tied the petrodollar to a critical commodity, the yuan could dominate trade in an essential good China controls. Liu points to critical minerals, clean energy equipment and the electrical infrastructure used in green energy technology, where China has already established substantial dominance.

**That route depends on sustained cost advantage.** If China consolidates its position across that industrial ecosystem, using scale and cost advantages to stop competitors catching up, it could gain enough leverage to price those hard-to-substitute exports in renminbi, creating the foundation for a green yuan that might eventually replace the petrodollar. On this reading, currency standing can be built through industry rather than through financial markets.

## Panoramic Analysis

The most important thing about this analysis is that it reframes China's monetary objective from replacement into constraint, and that reframing changes the yardstick against which progress should be measured.

The conventional competition frame is zero-sum: either the renminbi becomes dominant or the dollar keeps its monopoly. The constraint frame is not. A substitute of sufficient scale that remains usable in a crisis already weakens the United States' ability to impose financial sanctions unilaterally, and reaching that result does not require Beijing to carry the full cost of currency supremacy. For policymakers that path is far less risky. It does not require opening capital accounts, does not carry the fiscal and macroeconomic responsibilities of a reserve issuer, and does not mean accepting the political constraints that come with large foreign official holdings of domestic debt.

That is why the real pace of renminbi internationalisation is cautious. Rapid expansion in settlement and payment rails alongside glacial movement on capital accounts is not a contradiction. The former serves trade and commercial needs. The latter touches the core of political control, and once opened, cannot easily be closed again.

At a deeper level, the bottleneck for renminbi internationalisation ultimately lands on reinvestment. Foreign investors hold just 1.6 percent of the Chinese bond market against 27 percent in the US market. Currency that is settled and then immediately converted back does not build a currency ecosystem, it builds a channel. The real test is whether recipients are willing to reinvest renminbi balances into renminbi assets, which requires a predictable policy environment, an exit path and enough secondary market liquidity. All three point at the same precondition: capital controls must relax.

Relaxing capital controls is precisely what decision-makers are least willing to do. The obstacle is not technical but political. Offshore dollar holdings function as insurance for many elites against domestic political risk. When a currency carries both a commercial settlement function and a private wealth haven function, opening its account means opening an uncontrolled channel for capital flight, a risk far larger than the economic return alone can justify.

That sets up an implication for energy and geopolitics. The observation that Deutsche Bank flagged in March is worth keeping on the record: if arrangements for passage through the Strait of Hormuz end up linked to oil payments in yuan, the conflict may be remembered as a key catalyst for petrodollar erosion and the beginning of a petroyuan. The significance is not that this is imminent. It is that it identifies currency substitution as potentially following an industry-first path, where pricing habits are established in critical commodities before the financial plumbing is built. For anyone tracking renminbi internationalisation, the more useful early signal may be the appearance of long-term contracts denominated in yuan across critical minerals and clean energy supply chains, rather than settlement volume alone.
