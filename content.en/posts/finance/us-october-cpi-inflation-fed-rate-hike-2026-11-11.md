## Key Facts

- **The headline number**: The Consumer Price Index rose at a monthly pace of 0.4% in October, the Labor Department reported on Tuesday, November 10.
- **Cooling but hotter than expected**: October's increase was down from September, when headline inflation rose 0.6%, but the figure came in above the 0.3% monthly rise economists polled by FactSet had predicted.
- **Core prices**: Housing-excluded core prices rose 0.3%, the third consecutive month at that pace.
- **Shelter**: Shelter costs rose 0.3% in October and accounted for more than 30% of the monthly all-items increase, the Labor Department said.
- **Food**: Food prices rose at a monthly pace of 0.5% last month, after remaining flat in September.
- **Energy**: Energy costs rose 0.7% in October, compared with 5.2% in September — a much smaller contribution to the monthly increase than in previous reports.
- **No year-over-year figure**: The Labor Department did not report a year-over-year inflation rate for October 2026 because the 2025 government shutdown left it without October 2025 consumer price data for comparison.
- **Market reaction**: Major equity indexes fell, the 10-year Treasury yield moved back near 5%, and the dollar strengthened.
- **Policy context**: This was one of the last inflation reports before the December 8-9 Federal Reserve meeting, and Fed Governor Waller had said hours earlier that he was leaning toward a rate increase and would consider half a point if the data came in hot.
- **The structural shift**: Much of recent inflation has been driven by higher energy prices, but October's data shows other categories of goods and services rising as well.

## Competing Views

**The doves and the hawks are reading different parts of the same report.** Optimists point out that headline inflation fell back from 0.6% to 0.4% and that the energy contribution collapsed from 5.2% to 0.7%, which suggests the cost shock from the Iran war is fading. The cautious camp points to core prices stuck at 0.3% for a third straight month, shelter accounting for more than 30% of the increase, and food prices accelerating from flat to 0.5% — the evidence that underlying pressure has not let go.

**The change in composition matters more than the level.** Energy drove most of the inflation increase in recent months, and October shows costs for other goods and products rising as well. That means the driver of inflation is shifting from an exogenous geopolitical shock toward domestic demand and cost, which is precisely the category the Fed finds hardest to resolve by waiting.

**The missing year-over-year rate creates a real communication problem.** With the year-ago baseline erased by the shutdown, policymakers and markets can only read the trend off the monthly sequence, and the monthly sequence is more sensitive to seasonal adjustment. That amplifies volatility on release days and makes inconsistent year-over-year comparisons more likely in subsequent communication.

**For households, the lived experience and the data are drifting apart.** Gasoline has fallen back below $4 a gallon for the first time in nearly four months, which is a genuine improvement in commuting and transport costs. But the stickiness sits in shelter and food — the two largest and slowest-moving lines in a household budget — so the relief is narrower than the headline suggests.

## Panoramic Analysis

The most important thing in October's inflation report is the divergence in direction between the headline and the core. Headline inflation fell back to 0.4% while core stayed pinned at 0.3% for a third month, shelter and food still pushing upward. The drop in the energy contribution from 5.2% to 0.7% accounts for essentially all of the headline relief. That matters because it means the path of inflation over the coming months will no longer be determined by oil prices — and oil, geopolitical risk premium included, has been the most forecastable and most politically volatile variable of the past year.

Waller put a half-point move on the table hours before the release, at a moment when markets priced it at zero. A hotter-than-expected print hands that option its first support. His argument was never really about how much to add but about promises being carried out, and after this reading that argument becomes easier to make: if core prices refuse to fall for a third consecutive month, the cost of not delivering is no longer a market repricing but a discount on the institution's own credibility.

The absent year-over-year figure will keep generating noise for months. Every judgement about how far inflation remains from target now has to be rebuilt on the monthly sequence, which means the next Fed meeting carries more information than usual — and that release-day volatility is more likely to reflect ambiguity in the measurement than a genuine turn in the trend.

For households and businesses, the divergence shows up as two spending categories pulling apart. Gasoline below four dollars is a real cost relief, but the stickiness of shelter and food means this is not a broad-based easing. And the caveat on the one clearly improving line is the geopolitical risk premium: if it returns, the single component that made October look better reverses immediately.
