**[Key Facts]**

Oil prices climbed on October 20, 2026 as investors tracked escalating tensions in the Middle East. Front-month US West Texas Intermediate futures rose about 4.2% to around $107.50 a barrel as the contract neared expiry, while December Brent gained roughly 1.9% to $111.42, its highest level since May 19.

**[What We Know]**

- **Price action**: US front-month crude futures rose about 4.2% to around $107.50 a barrel on the day, with the contract expiring that same day. November WTI contracts are on track for a ninth consecutive session of gains, up nearly 20% for the month. December Brent rose about 1.9% to $111.42, the highest since May 19.
- **Escalation in the strait**: According to ship-tracking firm Kpler, at least 18 commercial vessels have come under fire while transiting the Strait of Hormuz over the past two weeks, a marked expansion in scope.
- **Tankers ablaze**: Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps said two “non-compliant” oil tankers were set ablaze in the strait after hitting mines laid by its naval unit. Foreign Ministry spokesperson Esmaeil Baqaei said Tehran would respond to any American strike “in equal measure, if not more forcefully.”
- **Market context**: The rally is driven by transport risk premiums rather than a supply disruption. Physical supply has not materially declined, but insurance rates, rerouting costs and schedule delays have all raised landed costs.
- **Policy expectations**: US President Donald Trump said earlier this month that American forces will not attack Iran before the midterm elections on November 3, providing the market with a relatively clear time window.

**[Wider Perspective]**

The most notable structural change in this rally is the way shipping risk in the strait has shifted from “incident” to “pricing variable.” Traditionally, the Strait of Hormuz carried supply-side weight in crude price models, with potential disruption estimated by measuring average daily transit volumes. Current market behaviour differs: even with no material decline in actual loadings, risk premiums are being embedded in prices for as long as attacks continue. Crude futures are effectively redefining the strait from a physical corridor into a financial instrument, sharply raising the sensitivity of the price curve to security events.

For global supply chains, this transmission path is more damaging than a simple price increase. The strait carries a substantial share of seaborne crude and liquefied natural gas shipments. If risk premiums become structural, the first parties under pressure are not end consumers but intermediaries: higher marine insurance rates, inventory buffers eroded by schedule delays, and spot purchasing by refiners attempting to secure runnable capacity. Differences in import dependence between Europe and East Asia will produce very different room for policy response to the same price signal.

The deeper question is persistence. The logic of this rally rests on attacks continuing rather than supply actually stopping, which means its reversal depends equally on the security situation rather than capacity returning to market. If US military action stays on hold through the midterm elections while Iranian maritime activity holds at its current level, prices could form a prolonged plateau above $110. Only a direct strike on production facilities would shift the pricing logic onto the supply side. This also explains the pattern of sharp advances followed by frequent givebacks: the market is pricing in risk while continuously assessing the point at which political decisions end it.

**[Viewpoints Compared]**

- **Iranian position**: Iranian officials frame action against “non-compliant” vessels as enforcement and deterrence within sovereign waters, and their language clearly reserves room for further escalation.
- **US government position**: Trump has said American forces will not strike Iran before the midterm elections, objectively giving markets a time window, while also signalling that the military option has been deferred rather than removed.
- **Shipping and insurance**: Tracking data shows attacks have affected at least 18 commercial vessels; industry attention centres on sustained tightening of premiums and underwriting terms, and on real capacity loss from crew safety constraints.
- **Market commentary**: Some analysts attribute the rally mainly to risk premiums combined with contract-roll effects, with no material supply tightening, leaving room for givebacks if sentiment eases. Others argue that a structurally elevated strait risk has shifted the price centre upward on a lasting basis.
