## Core Summary

Yahoo Finance reported on September 15 that market expectations for the September FOMC meeting are highly aligned, believing the Fed will launch its first rate hike since 2023 to address inflation pressure that has not fully receded and a still resilient job market. This decision will formally end a three-year rate-cutting cycle, marking a new tightening phase in the global monetary policy environment.

## Event Details

The FOMC will hold its regular September meeting this week. According to CME FedWatch tool data, the implied probability of a rate hike at this meeting has exceeded 85%, far higher than the less than 50% reading just one month ago. The rapid shift in market expectations primarily stems from the sticky performance of August inflation data, the strong rebound in non-farm payroll data, and the hawkish statements from multiple Fed officials at the Jackson Hole central bank symposium at the end of August.

Despite the highly aligned expectations for a September hike, significant divergence exists on the subsequent pace of hikes. The latest forecasts from JPMorgan and Goldman Sachs believe the Fed will take a "wait-and-see" cautious path after this meeting, raising rates only once this year; Morgan Stanley is more hawkish, predicting the Fed will hike rates consecutively in both September and November. The divide between Fed "hawks" and "doves" focuses primarily on two variables: the speed of labor market cooling and the stickiness of core services inflation.

Driven by Fed rate hike expectations, government bond yields in major global economies all refreshed multi-year highs on September 15. German 10-year bond yields rose to their highest level since 2008, Japanese 30-year yields broke through historical highs, and Italian and Greek yields also moved higher simultaneously. The synchronized pressure on global bond markets reflects market concerns about refinancing pressure on major global borrowers in the second half of 2026.

The rising dollar interest rates and strengthening dollar index are having a significant impact on emerging market capital flows. Institute of International Finance (IIF) data shows that in the first week of September, emerging market bond funds saw net outflows exceeding $4 billion, the largest single-week outflow since 2025. The Brazilian real, Turkish lira, and other high-yield currencies are under pressure against the dollar, forcing some emerging market central banks to hike rates early to stabilize their currencies.

## Panoramic Analysis

The Fed's imminent rate hike is by no means a routine monetary policy tightening, but rather a critical turning point as the global monetary policy environment enters a new phase.

From the US domestic economic perspective, the core driver of the restart of this rate hike cycle is not overheating economic growth but the dual effect of inflation stickiness and fiscal deficit pressure. Since 2025, US core services inflation has failed to fall back to the 2% policy target, partly due to the lagged transmission of housing inflation and structural tightness in the labor market. Starting rate hikes while inflation has not fully receded effectively amounts to the Fed acknowledging that the "soft landing" path has reached its end and that a certain cost of economic growth must be exchanged for inflation to return to target. This policy choice will affect the political and economic narrative of the 2026 US election cycle.

From the perspective of global monetary policy coordination, the Fed rate hike cycle restart will further widen the policy direction divergence between the US and the eurozone and Japan. The European Central Bank has cut rates multiple times over the past year, the Bank of Japan has begun raising rates but at an extremely slow pace, and the monetary policies of major US ally economies such as Australia and Canada also remain in easing cycles. Against the backdrop of rising dollar interest rates, global capital will further flow back into dollar assets, exerting persistent depreciation pressure on non-dollar currencies. This pattern will exacerbate the "dollar dependency" phenomenon in the global macroeconomy and have profound effects on international monetary system reform discussions.

From the perspective of global debt sustainability, the fundamental reason for the synchronized rise in global bond yields is that 2026 will see major global borrowers enter a new peak of debt maturity. According to IIF data, global government and corporate debt maturities in 2026 will reach a record $15 trillion, of which emerging market countries account for more than 30%. Against the backdrop of Fed rate hikes and rising global interest rates, debt refinancing costs for debtor countries will rise significantly, and some highly indebted countries may be forced to launch fiscal consolidation or even seek international rescue.

From the perspective of financial market structure, the rise in global bond yields is having profound effects on the global asset allocation pattern. On one hand, the relative attractiveness of fixed income assets is rising and some capital may flow back to bond markets from high-risk assets such as equities and private equity; on the other hand, major global sovereign wealth funds, pension funds, and other long-term institutional investors are reassessing their asset allocation duration and currency structure, and historical adjustments to over-allocated dollar assets may occur.

From the perspective of emerging market risk, the impact of higher dollar rates on emerging markets follows three superimposed paths: capital outflows, local currency depreciation, and imported inflation. Some emerging market countries with high external debt scales and insufficient foreign exchange reserves will face severe tests, and some countries may be forced to launch capital controls or currency depreciation. This pattern may give rise to a new round of IMF emergency loan negotiations and pose potential threats to global financial stability.

## Multiple Perspectives Comparison

**Inside the Fed**: Fed Chair Powell acknowledged in his remarks at the Jackson Hole symposium at the end of August that inflation still needs to fall further, but declined to make explicit statements about specific September policy actions. Multiple regional Fed presidents released hawkish signals in early September public speeches, arguing that labor market resilience provides room for continued rate hikes. A minority of dovish officials emphasized that rate hikes may cause excessive damage to low-income groups and the real estate industry, calling for interest rate stability.

**Wall Street investment banks**: JPMorgan's chief US economist characterized this rate hike as "cyclical tightening" in the latest report, believing the Fed will wait for further declines in inflation data after the hike and may not raise rates again this year. Morgan Stanley predicts the Fed will hike rates consecutively in both September and November, arguing that inflation stickiness requires a stronger policy response. Goldman Sachs holds a neutral stance, believing that after this meeting's hike, the Fed will decide on next steps based on data.

**International institutions**: The IMF warned in its latest World Economic Outlook that the Fed rate hike cycle restart may cause "asymmetric shocks" to emerging markets and developing economies, calling on major economies to strengthen monetary policy communication and foreign exchange market intervention coordination. The OECD emphasized that the synchronized rise in global bond yields reflects market concerns about debt sustainability, and governments should accelerate fiscal consolidation.

**Emerging market central banks**: The Brazilian central bank has already raised its benchmark rate to double digits at the end of August to stabilize the real and curb imported inflation. The Mexican central bank and the Indian central bank also expressed concerns about the Fed policy pivot at their early September meetings, and some central banks have begun deploying foreign exchange market interventions in advance.

**Financial market investors**: Major global asset managers including BlackRock and Bridgewater warned in their latest client reports that the rise in global bond yields may continue through the end of 2026, recommending reducing portfolio duration and moderately increasing allocations to safe-haven assets such as gold. Soros Fund Management is more optimistic in its latest public commentary, believing that the Fed rate hike pace will be faster than market expectations and dollar assets still have significant allocation value.
