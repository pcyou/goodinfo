## Key Facts

Apple reported fiscal 2026 fourth-quarter results for the quarter ended September 26 on Monday: revenue of $114.5 billion, up 12 percent year over year, setting September-quarter records for total revenue, iPhone revenue and earnings per share. Diluted earnings per share was $2.10, up 14 percent, including about five cents from tariff refunds. It was the first earnings report under chief executive John Ternus, and the same document also carried the bad news: inventory rose 154 percent year over year, and the company guided gross margin lower next quarter because of memory chip costs.

- **Top line**: quarterly revenue of $114.5 billion, up 12 percent; diluted earnings per share of $2.10, up 14 percent, including roughly five cents from tariff refunds.
- **Margin**: September-quarter gross margin was 47.9 percent, including about one percentage point of benefit from tariff refunds. The company guided the December quarter to 45 to 46 percent, attributing the reduction to higher memory costs.
- **Fiscal year**: fiscal 2026 revenue reached an all-time record of $479 billion with double-digit earnings per share growth; net income and earnings per share both set September-quarter records.
- **Cash and installed base**: $28 billion of operating cash flow in the quarter and $28 billion returned to shareholders; the active device installed base reached an all-time high across all major product categories and geographic segments. The board declared a cash dividend of $0.27 per share.
- **Inventory**: inventory rose from $11.09 billion in the third quarter to $14.54 billion in the fourth, up 31.1 percent quarter over quarter, and from $5.72 billion a year earlier, up 154.3 percent.
- **Memory costs**: Ternus told analysts that memory pricing right now is unprecedented, that the December quarter would carry even higher costs, and that beyond December memory costs would continue to increase. He also said the benefit from carrying memory inventory would diminish over time and that the company was increasing its purchases of the component.
- **Purchase obligations**: manufacturing purchase obligations increased by $33 billion between the June and September quarters, to be disclosed in the annual filing.
- **Product mix**: iPhone and Mac sales beat estimates. iPad was the only segment to decline, with revenue of $6.2 billion against analyst expectations of $6.7 billion, down 10 percent year over year, which the company attributed to supply constraints. Reporting also flagged soft China sales.
- **Management**: chief operating officer Sabih Khan joined the call to address memory, reiterated the higher purchase obligations and did not rule out further price increases. Apple had already raised prices on Mac and iPad at the end of June and on iPhone in September.

## Competing Views

**The company and the market disagree on cost, not demand.** The company framing is the best fiscal year ever: records for revenue, earnings per share, cash flow and installed base. The market framing is falling margin and exploding inventory. Both read the same report; the difference is whether you look at the revenue line or the cost line.

**The New York Times took the first route**, leading with profit growth despite component price worries. Business Insider took the second, pointing to soft China and iPad sales. These are two descriptions of the same two sets of numbers.

**Memory moved from a cost item to a strategic one on the call.** Two of Ternus's phrasings stand out: memory pricing right now is unprecedented, and costs will continue to increase beyond December. More telling is his admission that the benefit from carrying memory inventory will fade over time. Stockpiling delays the problem; it does not remove it.

**The presence of the supply-chain chief is itself a signal.** Bringing the chief operating officer onto the call to answer memory questions usually means this is no longer an ordinary margin guidance adjustment. Notably, he did not rule out further price increases, and prices have already gone up twice this year.

## Panoramic Analysis

The real information in Apple's report sits in the gap between two numbers: $114.5 billion in quarterly revenue, a 47.9 percent gross margin, and inventory up 154 percent year over year.

This is the classic shape of a report with excellent demand and tight cost. Records for revenue, double-digit earnings per share growth and an installed base at an all-time high in every category and region say the demand side has no problem at all. Yet one percentage point of that 47.9 percent gross margin came from tariff refunds, and the guidance for the December quarter is 45 to 46 percent, with the reduction attributed directly to memory prices. Strip out the one-off and product profitability is being squeezed from above.

The part worth tracking is that Apple hedged with inventory, and that hedge has a price. Inventory went from $5.72 billion to $14.54 billion in a year, close to a ninefold increase, and manufacturing purchase obligations rose a further $33 billion in two quarters. That is deliberate stockpiling during a memory price upcycle, a bet that prices keep rising or that supply stays tight. Management concedes the buffer decays over time and expects costs to keep climbing past December. Inventory therefore pushes margin pressure from this quarter into the next; it does not cancel it.

That produces a genuinely interesting structural observation: Apple's pricing power is being used to pass cost through rather than to protect profit. The June increases on Mac and iPad and the September increase on iPhone were meant to defend gross margin, but a price rise is itself a marginal negative for demand. Sales beating expectations does not fix the problem, because the beat is multiplied by a higher cost. It is notable that Apple raised prices in two consecutive quarters while simultaneously stockpiling, which says management believes demand can absorb the increases. Set that against soft China sales and the next question becomes clear: how much elasticity is left between price and volume.

For shareholders this is the familiar shape of growth covering cost, with the valuation waiting on delivery. Record revenue has to pass through a memory-price funnel before it becomes profit, and the funnel is still narrowing. What matters now is not whether the revenue record keeps being broken but three specific indicators: when memory costs peak, the actual elasticity of volume after price increases, and whether inventory climbs again next quarter. If all three move together, Apple has to make a real choice between volume and price.
