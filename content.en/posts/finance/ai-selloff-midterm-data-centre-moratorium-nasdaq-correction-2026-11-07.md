## Core Summary

Artificial intelligence and chip stocks sold off sharply in the wake of the United States midterms. On Thursday Nvidia fell 9.3 percent, AMD 17.3 percent, Micron 17.5 percent, Broadcom 10.4 percent, Microsoft 3.6 percent, Tesla 10.8 percent and SpaceX 9.1 percent. The neoclouds that sell compute capacity took it harder, with Nebius down 15 percent, Iren down 17 percent and CoreWeave down 10 percent. The Nasdaq Composite dropped more than 5 percent to close more than 11 percent below the record it set last month, its steepest single-day fall in nineteen months and a confirmed correction.

Nothing in a single earnings report triggered the move. Democrats swept both the House and the Senate on Tuesday, and the vote included clear voter opposition to data centres. On the same night, dozens of local measures in ten states went to the ballot, restricting data centre construction in various ways.

## Event Details

- **One-day moves**: Nvidia 9.3 percent, AMD 17.3 percent, Micron 17.5 percent, Broadcom 10.4 percent, Microsoft 3.6 percent, Tesla 10.8 percent and SpaceX 9.1 percent.
- **Neoclouds bore more**: Nebius 15 percent, Iren 17 percent and CoreWeave 10 percent, all businesses whose economics depend directly on large data centre projects.
- **An early bounce**: US tech stocks showed signs of recovery on Friday morning, which most analysts read as technical rather than a trend reversal.
- **Local ballots**: Dozens of measures across ten states were voted on the same night, limiting data centre construction in different ways.
- **Measured historical resistance**: Research by the International Data Center Authority found that between January 2024 and May 2026, at least 46 artificial intelligence data centre projects across twenty US states, representing more than $170 billion in announced investment, were blocked, withdrawn or stalled after community opposition.
- **The size of the prize**: Goldman Sachs estimates total artificial intelligence related capital spending could reach about $7.6 trillion between 2026 and 2031, across computing, data centres and power.
- **An earlier peak**: One analyst moved the date on which large technology companies' capital expenditure growth peaks forward, from a previously expected 2028 or 2029 to 2027.
- **Market losses**: Tesla and SpaceX together shed more than $420 billion in market value after the midterms.
- **The historical pattern**: Across ten corrections in the past decade, the Nasdaq was back in rally mode within roughly six months in all but one, the exception being 2022. In thirteen midterm cycles since 1974, the index stood higher twelve months after the election in all but one.

## Comparison of Viewpoints

**The most direct concern runs from the election result to policy**. Dan Ives of Yorkville Ives and Co wrote on Wednesday that the central question for technology investors is whether the midterms slow the pace of the planned eight hundred to one thousand data centres across the United States that fuel the artificial intelligence revolution, and that in his view the answer is a clear yes. He added that Democratic control, particularly in the Senate, will create major hurdles for artificial intelligence over the next two years.

**The bearish view sees resistance running well beyond the election**. Senior financial market analyst Kyle Rodda of Capital.com wrote that the regulatory and political risks arrive at a moment when the artificial intelligence trade was already on shakier foundations, pointing to an earnings season that was an overall disappointment in which the Magnificent Seven undershot growth expectations and flashed warning signs that the pace of spending was slowing. He framed the tail risk as measures as potentially disruptive as moratoriums on new data centre construction, with investors rushing for the exits.

**The most pragmatic view comes from the credit market**. Dominique Toublan, head of US credit strategy at Barclays Investment Bank, argued that the pushback against artificial intelligence is bipartisan but local, making the build-out pricier rather than stalling it, and put it in a phrase: the race shifts from a prisoner's dilemma to a war of attrition, in which scarce power favours the biggest balance sheets. On this reading, resistance does not change the direction of travel, it redistributes share.

**A historical lens supplies the time dimension**. Frank Cappelleri, founder of CappThesis, counted thirteen midterm cycles since 1974 and found the Nasdaq higher twelve months after the election in all but one, though the path can be volatile. He pointed to 2018, when the index rallied the day after Democrats won the House, sold off sharply that December, and still stood about 15 percent higher a year after the vote.

## Panoramic Analysis

What this selloff repriced was not next quarter's earnings but the approval risk attached to compute supply, and that distinction separates it from an ordinary earnings-driven pullback. An earnings correction revises expectations about a business that exists. Political resistance revises the physical precondition of the business: when whether an industry can expand depends on ballots, state legislation and congressional posture, the discount rate applied to its capital expenditure has to carry a risk premium that did not previously exist. Once priced, that premium does not evaporate because one election has been settled.

The scale of that risk is now partly quantified. The International Data Center Authority found that between January 2024 and May 2026, at least 46 projects across twenty states, representing more than $170 billion in announced investment, were blocked, withdrawn or stalled after community opposition. This resistance has been present for years, but it arrived mainly as discrete local events that capital markets habitually filed as noise. With Democrats holding both chambers and data centre construction explicitly in the political crosshairs, the same resistance is beginning to take the shape of national policy, and its pricing meaning changes with it.

Goldman's estimate of $7.6 trillion in artificial intelligence capital spending between 2026 and 2031 supplies the scale reference for the selloff. A number of that magnitude means the industry needs continuing local and federal permission to convert announced investment into actual facilities. Committing half of a decade of planned capital expenditure to a political environment is a risk exposure that did not exist in the previous cycle.

The most important single judgement is Barclays' line about scarce power favouring the biggest balance sheets. The distinction between a prisoner's dilemma and a war of attrition is that in the former every participant has an incentive to defect first, while in the latter advantage accrues over time to whoever can bear the highest cost. Local resistance applies the same rules to everyone, so it does not prevent expansion, it raises the entry cost, and it systematically favours those with thicker balance sheets and cheaper capital. That also explains why the neoclouds fell harder than cash-generating large technology companies in the same session: they depend on rolling external financing, and cannot easily absorb a construction timeline that stretches out.

What makes this adjustment uncomfortable is where it starts. Chipmakers and neoclouds had already sold off through October on rate and overspending concerns, delivering the Nasdaq's worst month since March 2025, so the midterms layered a new variable onto an already fragile base. If upcoming earnings continue to confirm a slower spending pace, political resistance and fundamental deceleration will be pressing on both ends of the valuation at once.

Five indicators deserve watching: the final results and severity of state and local data centre ballot measures; whether large technology companies cut capital expenditure guidance alongside earnings; actual wait times for grid interconnection; the cost and rollover terms of neocloud financing; and whether the Nasdaq recovers toward its pre-correction high within three months. The second and third matter most, because they decide whether this was a brief release of political premium or a genuine downshift in the model of compute expansion.
