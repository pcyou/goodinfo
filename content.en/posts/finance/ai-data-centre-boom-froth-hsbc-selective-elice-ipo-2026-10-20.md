**[Key Facts]**

The artificial-intelligence-driven data centre investment boom is showing its first clear signs of cooling. HSBC chief executive Georges Elhedery said AI is a transformative technology worth investing in, but that investors must be selective in the infrastructure underpinning it, focusing on the quality of tenancy agreements and contract pricing rather than assuming every project is sound. At the same time, South Korean data centre builder Elice fell about 30% on its trading debut, a signal that investors are repricing the sustainability of the compute build-out.

**[What We Know]**

- **Bank chief's comments**: Elhedery described AI as “a major enabler to re-engineer and reimagine all our businesses, all our activities,” arguing it will give colleagues significant new power through productivity gains and transform the bank. But on infrastructure, he advised that “one has to be selective,” examining the quality of tenancy agreements and the pricing of contracts — not all of them are at a level of quality that can be strongly supported, and some may be more challenging.
- **The bank's dual role**: HSBC is both a financier and an operator of data centres. Ranked seventh globally and the largest lender in Europe by S&P Global last year, its assets approach $3 trillion. Its annual report states an ambition to become “the leading bank for fast-growing transition ecosystems,” with customers spanning data centres and AI.
- **IPO performance**: South Korean data centre builder Elice fell roughly 30% on its trading debut, becoming the most closely watched new listing among technology names. Several financial media outlets connected the move to debate over the sustainability of AI infrastructure spending.
- **Market size estimates**: Global data centre spending could reach $7 trillion by 2030, according to McKinsey, with Big Tech increasingly tapping private credit and taking on debt to fund the facilities.
- **Industry backdrop**: Banks have already shown caution toward AI-related bonds and loans, with investors describing the divergence in the financing cycle as a survival-of-the-fittest process.

**[Wider Perspective]**

The significance of this signal lies in its origin: it comes from the supply of capital rather than from demand. The current debate is not whether AI will keep growing, but whether the infrastructure investment supporting that growth has been priced too richly. HSBC's position gives its view unusual weight, because the bank is simultaneously a lender and a data centre operator — it knows both the credit quality of tenants and the true returns on its own assets. That dual position makes its assessment harder to dismiss as a conflicted sell-side view.

Structurally, the pressure is concentrated on the quality of leases. High-leverage data centre projects typically rely on long-term pre-let agreements to cover very large construction costs and debt. If a major tenant's credit rating is cut or lease terms are renegotiated, the project's cash-flow assumptions can unravel quickly. Artificial-intelligence-related financing expanded rapidly in recent years and became somewhat decoupled from the pace of real-economy returns, and the cash-flow modelling of some projects has drawn analyst scrutiny. A debut that breaks sharply on its first day provides a public market anchor for those assumptions.

Over the long run, the infrastructure cycle looks more likely to show divergence than an outright end. A gap exists between genuine growth in compute demand and excessive expansion in investment, but that gap will not be distributed evenly: assets with strong tenants, manageable grid-connection costs and locations near power and network hubs will diverge sharply from facilities dependent on a single customer, sited in power-constrained regions and financed with high leverage. Market pricing from here is likely to shift from asking whether a deal belongs to the AI theme toward asking whether its cash-flow structure can withstand both interest rates and demand.

**[Viewpoints Compared]**

- **Banking leadership**: HSBC's chief executive endorses the long-term value of AI while stressing that infrastructure exposure requires scrutiny of lease quality and contract pricing, pointing to clear differentiation where high-quality assets remain attractive.
- **Market view**: The roughly 30% fall on Elice's debut is read by some investors as a sign that AI compute expansion is entering a repricing phase, while others regard it as a routine correction after an over-rich individual valuation.
- **Industry view**: If technology giants continue funding facilities through private credit and debt, data centres shift from project finance toward structured finance, raising sensitivity to construction costs and financing conditions.
- **Cautious view**: Banks have already turned more cautious on AI-related financing, with investors describing a “Darwin moment” in the cycle in which projects lacking real returns come under pressure first.
