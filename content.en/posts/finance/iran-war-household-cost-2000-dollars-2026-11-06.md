## Key Facts

The Iran war has become a cost war as well as a military one. United States military spending on the conflict has passed 47 billion dollars, up sharply from an estimate of 37.5 billion dollars made in July, and Mark Zandi, chief economist at Moody's Analytics, estimates the war has cost the average US household nearly 2,000 dollars.

- **Origin**: United States and Israeli strikes on Iran began on 28 February, according to the Congressional Research Service, triggering a regional war. Iran has retaliated against targets across the Middle East, including a pumping station on the Saudi East-West oil pipeline, and has used attacks on commercial shipping to assert control over the Strait of Hormuz.
- **The diplomatic arc**: the sides agreed a ceasefire in April and signed a memorandum of understanding on 17 June, but the agreement collapsed within weeks and formally expired in August. By September the Congressional Research Service was describing the conflict as a war of attrition.
- **Renewed strikes**: the recent strike on Qeshm Island was not isolated. Officials told ABC News that limited defensive strikes had been carried out in recent weeks and not publicised, to avoid signalling a return to major combat operations.
- **Military cost**: Reuters reported on 27 October, citing Pentagon data, that military spending has exceeded 47 billion dollars, of which more than 29 billion dollars goes to replacing expended munitions.
- **Household cost**: Zandi estimates roughly 1,100 dollars of the household burden comes from higher energy and transportation costs, with higher interest rates and a share of military spending making up the rest, totalling about 265 billion dollars nationwide.
- **The chokepoint**: the Strait of Hormuz carried about 20 million barrels of oil a day before the war. Qeshm Island, roughly 14 miles from the Iranian port of Bandar Abbas, is central to Iran's ability to control traffic through it, and Iran has stepped up attacks on commercial ships transiting the strait in recent weeks.
- **Limited workaround**: Gilbert Metcalf, a visiting professor at the MIT Sloan School of Management and a former deputy assistant secretary at the Treasury, says Saudi Arabia's plan to send 10 million barrels of oil a day around Hormuz may barely move prices.
- **Political consequence**: energy costs ran through the whole campaign. Democrats won control of both the House and the Senate on 3 November, and Vice President JD Vance was blunt about the cause: "The reason we lost is the war."

## Event Details

Fortune reported that officials described the Qeshm attack as a defensive response to Iranian forces using surface-to-air missiles against US assets, and that in a Fox News interview that night the president suggested there had been no recent fighting. Officials said the strikes are small next to earlier phases of the war, but they come at a fragile moment for energy markets, which had just priced in hopes of a diplomatic exit, and as the United States heads into heating season.

Metcalf said the strikes raise the odds of the outcome markets fear most: "The risk to global oil markets and, by extension, the global economy as we head into winter is the potential for Iranian strikes against shipping in the Strait of Hormuz or against oil and gas infrastructure in the region." He added that even restraint from Tehran may not help much, because any increased military activity in the region heightens the risk of retaliation and oil prices will remain persistently high in the face of these risks.

At the same time, the market was pricing two directions. CNBC reported that oil prices rose on Friday as investors weighed the risk of renewed US-Iran military escalation after the midterms, alongside prospects for a diplomatic breakthrough. In the same session, Rapidan Energy's Bob McNally predicted that both Brent and West Texas Intermediate futures would jump above 100 dollars a barrel.

## Competing Views

The bearish view is concentrated in risk pricing. Metcalf's assessment is that a limited strike does not reduce the risk premium so much as relocate it, because the largest threat is an attack on shipping or on regional oil and gas infrastructure, and continued strikes on Iran only make such an attack more likely even if each individual strike is called defensive. The trading desk view is harsher still, with a forecast that both benchmark contracts move above 100 dollars.

The optimistic reading relies on the diplomatic track surviving at least partially, and the market's behaviour is the evidence: oil fell after the election, which means part of the worst-case premium was unwound even before any strike occurred. Both readings were live in the same session, which is itself a useful signal about how thin confidence has become.

A less discussed issue is the attribution of the cost. Zandi's figure bundles energy, interest rates and military spending into one household number, and those three lines have different drivers and different decision-makers: one is market pricing, one is monetary policy, one is the federal budget. Combining them produces an intuitive headline but flattens the question of who can actually respond to which part.

## Panoramic Analysis

The most valuable feature of this cost accounting is that it converts an abstract geopolitical conflict into household bills that can be checked, and that is precisely why the room for policy manoeuvre has narrowed.

The four phases since February, open hostilities, the April ceasefire, the June memorandum and the August expiry, read as a diplomatic curve in official narratives but as a monotonically rising curve in household bills. 47 billion dollars of military spending, more than 29 billion dollars of munitions replenishment and nearly 2,000 dollars per household form a set of numbers that cannot be offset by pointing to negotiations still under way. When the vice president attributes the election result directly to the war, the equation between bill and ballot has been put on the public record.

The compounding of transmission channels matters as much as the total. Roughly 1,100 dollars of the household burden comes from energy and transportation, with the remainder coming from interest rates and a share of military spending, which means the war reaches the same household cash flow through several channels at once. None of those channels requires a major escalation to keep amplifying. Heating demand arrives precisely as this curve enters its seasonal peak, removing the possibility of a natural decline.

That is also why Metcalf regards supply-side workarounds around Hormuz as limited. Even an extra 10 million barrels a day of rerouted supply may move the marginal price by less than a single headline about risk in the strait produces. What compresses the bill is not more supply but a decline in the probability of attacks, which makes the frequency of military action near Qeshm and Bandar Abbas, the number and scale of attacks on transiting shipping, and how long Brent spends near 100 dollars a barrel the three indicators worth tracking.
