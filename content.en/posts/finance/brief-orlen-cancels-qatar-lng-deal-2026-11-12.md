## Key Facts

- **The event**: Orlen has terminated its long-term LNG supply contract with QatarEnergy.
- **The legal basis**: Force majeure.
- **What preceded it**: The two sides had been at loggerheads over the issue for months.
- **The precedent**: The first known cancellation of such an agreement since the war began.
- **The structure of the deal**: A long-term LNG supply contract, the classic form of a long-term offtake arrangement.

## Wider Perspective

The termination of a long-term LNG contract matters far more in the energy market than a routine price move. Long-term contracts are the plumbing underneath energy diplomacy: the volatile part of the price is left to spot markets, and what is actually locked is supply security. The buyer pays a premium for that certainty, and the seller uses it to underpin project financing. Once force majeure becomes executable in a war environment, the pricing logic of the contract changes, because every side re-evaluates not the price but how much of that promised security can actually be delivered.

What is unusual here is that the standoff itself lasted months. Force majeure is never self-executing. It requires proof that the event was beyond the parties' control and that performance became legally impossible rather than merely inconvenient. Reaching termination means the contract's dispute-resolution machinery has been run end to end. The reference value for later negotiations lies there: the buyer chose termination over renegotiation, which indicates a view that the original framework could no longer offer acceptable certainty.

At a broader level, Europe's energy procurement structure has been debated repeatedly, with concentration, route diversification and long-term contract coverage as the core metrics. This termination does not change the fact that Europe imports from the Middle East, but it provides a new line of observation. With a supplier exposed to sustained military risk, a long-term contract does not automatically equal supply security. It equals precisely the layer of security the contract language describes, and the gap between those two things has until now mostly sat inside the assumptions of risk models. It has now landed in a specific document.
