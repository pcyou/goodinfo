## Key Facts

- **When and where**: November 16, in San Francisco.
- **Convenor**: Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh.
- **Subject**: Whether artificial intelligence will lower prices, push them higher, or replace workers.
- **Participants**: Technology executives, economists and employers.
- **Why it matters**: The conclusions could influence how the Fed approaches interest rates, which is a major factor in digital asset price movements as investors weigh risky holdings against safer alternatives.
- **Industry spillover**: Technology companies and data centres compete for electricity, and Bitcoin miners feel the same pressure from that competition.

## Competing Views

**One view treats the forum as a trial run in policy communication.** When the Fed convenes outside discussion ahead of a formal decision, it is usually trying to build a shared understanding or to test market reaction. Bringing industry directly into the room is itself a signal, because it says the decision-makers think the question has moved beyond the purely academic.

**Another view holds that the output is limited and the real variable is the data.** A public discussion can supply a narrative frame, but it does not change what the inflation prints show. If energy costs rise because of data centre demand, or if productivity gains fail to materialise, monetary policy will still follow the data. The forum's real function is to give markets a moment at which to reprice.

**A third view starts from how the gains and costs are distributed.** Electricity is the actual constraint running through this chain. Demand from technology companies and data centres is pushing up local power prices, which feeds into inflation readings and into the marginal cost of every electricity-intensive business. Mining sits at the far end of that chain, with a cost curve that is highly sensitive to the price of power.

## Panoramic Analysis

The importance of this forum lies not in the conclusions it might produce but in the fact that it places a variable already affecting pricing formally on the policy agenda.

The first layer is a change in the policy frame. For years the Fed discussed artificial intelligence largely as a neutral input into productivity. Listing inflation, output and employment side by side signals that officials now accept that the direction of pass-through from artificial intelligence into prices is itself uncertain. When the direction is uncertain, disagreement about the rate path widens, and widening disagreement shows up first in long-end yields and in the discount rates applied to risk assets.

The second layer is the cost-side transmission. The clearest channel at present is electricity: demand from data centres and technology companies is pushing up local power prices, which affects both inflation readings and the marginal cost of every electricity-intensive sector. Mining sits at the end of that chain, with a cost curve highly sensitive to the price of power.

The third layer is the sequencing risk between narrative and data. The forum gives markets a moment to reprice the idea that artificial intelligence affects inflation, and narrative typically moves ahead of the prints, which amplifies volatility. In past episodes of this kind, the market's reaction to a policy discussion has often been larger than the information contained in the discussion itself.

Four indicators are worth tracking. What the forum transcript and question periods say about power costs and productivity. How the language of Fed speakers changes after the forum. The volume and queue position of data centre power applications. And the readings for electricity and related services inside the inflation data.
