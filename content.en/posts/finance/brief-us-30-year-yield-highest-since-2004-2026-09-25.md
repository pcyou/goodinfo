## US 30-Year Treasury Yields Hit Highest Level Since 2004 as Global Bond Selloff Deepens

## Core Summary The global bond selloff deepened further with U.S. 30-year Treasury yields climbing to the highest level since 2004, while long-end government bonds across multiple countries came under simultaneous pressure, according to Reuters and other outlets. The move reflects markets repricing expectations on the inflation path, central bank policy, and fiscal outlook, with volatility across global capital markets rising notably.

### Event Details

**Yield trajectory**: The 30-year Treasury yield has breached a multi-year resistance level, hitting its highest since 2004, while the inversion with short-end rates eased modestly. Analysts say the renewed expansion of term premium is the core driver behind the selloff, driven by repeated shifts in inflation expectations, fiscal deficit projections, and marginal changes in foreign demand.

**Cross-market linkage**: The bond selloff is not a U.S.-only phenomenon, with long-end yields rising in tandem in the UK, Japan, and Germany. Cross-market correlations have moved notably higher, indicating that global investors are reassessing the long-term level of developed-market interest rates.

**Potential impact on asset prices**: Analysts cited in the coverage say that the rise in long-end rates puts pressure on risk-asset valuations, mortgages, corporate funding costs, and emerging-market capital flows, while some capital is rotating into gold and inflation-hedging themes. Defensive allocation tendencies are clearly on the rise.

## Big Picture A long-end rate breaking through a historical level typically signals that markets are going through a phase of recalibrating their view of future growth and inflation. For years, markets priced assets under a "inflation falls, rates fall" narrative; this round of selling is forcing investors to revisit the tail risks of central bank policy normalization. For investors, a structural lift in long-end rates will impose new requirements on bond duration management, equity discount-rate assumptions, and the risk premium on alternative assets. In the short term, the core tension is between the inflation path and growth resilience; in the long term, fiscal sustainability and the evolution of the global reserve currency system will be the deeper drivers.

## Multiple Viewpoints Central banks and officials: Officials at several major central banks stressed that policy paths will remain data-dependent, expressed concern about the risks of further long-end rate increases, and gave no clear signal of intervention.

Institutional investors: Some large asset managers argue that the rise in long-end rates is not yet over and recommend reducing portfolio duration and increasing inflation-hedging exposure; other institutions still prefer to add on dips, arguing that the inflation regime and terminal rate may not be as high as current market pricing implies.

Academic and commentator views: Most economists argue that the structural level of long-end rates is tied to long-term variables like demographics, technology, and global savings, and that within-year movements mostly reflect cyclical factors; commentators warn that markets should not underestimate the tail impact of fiscal and policy uncertainty.
