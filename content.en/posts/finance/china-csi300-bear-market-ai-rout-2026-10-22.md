**[Core Summary]**

China's benchmark CSI 300 index has fallen about 20 percent from its June peak, formally entering bear-market territory after ten consecutive weeks of declines. The Financial Times, Bloomberg and other outlets attribute the move to two main lines: a marked cooling in the artificial intelligence trade that had supported the market, and stabilisation expectations that failed to redirect capital flows. Asian equities weakened together, as regional technology sentiment and geopolitical tension combined to depress risk appetite.

**[Event Details]**

- **Index performance**: The CSI 300 is down roughly 20 percent from its June peak, meeting the technical definition of a bear market, and has posted losses for ten straight weeks.
- **Drivers**: Multiple outlets point to a systematic pullback in artificial intelligence supply-chain names that had led the index, with capital exiting that theme as a direct accelerant.
- **Policy backdrop**: Investors had been anticipating stabilisation measures. Reporting suggests those statements did not redirect flows, and some participants chose to take profit once the announcements landed.
- **Regional spillover**: Reuters reported Asian equities sinking to a three-month low, with weak technology sentiment and Gulf tension combining as sources of pressure.
- **Institutional view**: Some research houses argue that state buying on the current scale is not sufficient to turn the trend, and that larger intervention is required to stabilise the market.

**[Panoramic Analysis]**

What makes this episode notable is that the trigger was not a disappointing economic data point but a shift in the valuation framework itself. Entering bear-market territory essentially means the pricing centre for this economy's future earnings path has been marked down, and such markdowns normally require fresh evidence to repair. Compared with an earnings-driven decline, a move in the valuation centre is far slower and considerably harder to reverse, which implies that the policy space available is narrower than market sentiment tends to assume.

The artificial intelligence trade acted as an amplifier along this chain. That theme had been serving as a refuge for capital whenever liquidity was plentiful and other destinations were uncertain, so growth assets became an overweight concentration. When those positions began to loosen, their scale was sufficient to weigh meaningfully on the index. It is worth noting that such unwinding does not stay confined to technology: pricing for Chinese assets is often completed through a handful of representative sectors, and when those sectors enter a valuation-reset channel, the overall risk premium gets reassessed, often faster than fundamentals themselves change.

The third layer is the interaction between external constraints and policy space. Regional markets falling together indicates this is not purely a domestic flow problem, yet with no clear turn in the external rate environment, global capital available as a hedge is not abundant. Policy tools therefore depend more on execution speed and scale than on announcement alone. Market confidence has already been dented by unmet stabilisation expectations, which means any future measure will need verifiable buying behind it; statements alone risk accelerating capital outflow rather than slowing it.

**[Viewpoints Compared]**

- **Policy position**: Some views hold that the scale of state buying remains insufficient to reverse the trend and that larger intervention is needed, a judgement echoed by parts of the research community.
- **Market participant view**: Investors note that stabilisation statements failed to change flows and instead became the trigger for taking profits, reflecting the gap between expectation and actual intervention.
- **Foreign capital lens**: International analysis broadly ties the decline to the cooling of the artificial intelligence trade, characterising it as part of a global technology adjustment rather than a purely domestic phenomenon.
- **Cautious reading**: One line of argument warns that unmet stabilisation expectations themselves damage confidence, so the signalling effect of later measures may be weaker than equivalent actions in the past.
