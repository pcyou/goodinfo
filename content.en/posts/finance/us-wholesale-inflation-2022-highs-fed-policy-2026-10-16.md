**[Core Summary]**

US wholesale inflation climbed to its highest level since 2022 in September, according to Labor Department data released on October 15, with the producer price index rising far more than markets expected. Treasury yields and the dollar jumped after the print, US equity futures fell, and bets on whether the Federal Reserve will cut rates again this year split sharply. Sticky services inflation combined with energy price volatility has widened the divide inside the Fed over the path of disinflation, exposing global asset prices and cross-border capital flows to a fresh round of volatility.

**[Event Details]**

- **The data**: The Labor Department's September PPI report showed the largest year-on-year rise since 2022, with a month-on-month increase that also beat expectations. Services costs and core goods prices both contributed, and core PPI excluding food and energy also firmed.
- **Market reaction**: US 10-year Treasury yields jumped after the print, the dollar strengthened and US equity futures came under pressure. Spot gold pulled back modestly, and market pricing for Fed cuts this year was cut from two to one — or even zero.
- **Policy split**: Because PPI is a leading indicator for CPI, sticky services inflation combined with energy price volatility has widened the gap inside the Fed over the disinflation path: some officials favor holding restrictive rates for longer, while others worry about a cooling labor market.
- **Global spillovers**: Firmer US inflation expectations are spilling over into global asset prices. Eurozone and UK gilt yields have risen in tandem, emerging market currencies are under broad pressure and global financial conditions have tightened.
- **Corporate impact**: A persistently high PPI means corporate input-cost pressure continues to build. Some industries have already begun passing costs on to consumers, raising the risk of consumer inflation expectations becoming self-fulfilling.

**[Panorama]**

**First**, the print highlights the complexity of the last mile of US disinflation. With services wages, energy prices and supply chain resilience all in play, the final stretch of bringing inflation back to target is proving harder than markets had expected. The Fed's policy path is therefore subject to greater uncertainty and near-term financial market volatility could amplify. Over the medium term, the balancing act between inflation targets and growth support is becoming harder for central banks globally, and the risks of cross-border capital flow volatility and exchange rate swings are rising.

**Second**, the data further exposes structural tensions in the US economy. On one hand, the labor market, while cooling somewhat, remains resilient and unemployment is still near historical lows; on the other, after years of high rates, services inflation stickiness shows that demand-side resilience has also surprised to the upside. This suggests a true US soft landing is still some distance away, and macro policy is caught between fighting inflation and avoiding recession.

**Fourth**, the PPI print is likely to intensify domestic US policy fights. Sticky inflation is making the Fed more cautious about cutting, but the drag of high rates on housing, autos and small business credit is becoming more visible. The White House and Congress will debate fiscal stimulus and monetary policy more intensely. Over the short term, inflation data may become one of the defining economic issues of the 2026 midterm elections and will have a far-reaching impact on the trajectory of US macro policy.

**Multiple Perspectives**

- **Hawkish Fed officials**: read the September PPI as strong evidence of inflation stickiness, favor keeping restrictive policy in place for longer and warn that premature rate cuts could undo the progress already made.
- **Dovish Fed officials**: worry about a cooling labor market and weakening consumer confidence, and argue that rates should be cut in a timely manner as long as inflation stays controlled, to avoid an unnecessary hard landing.
- **Wall Street analysts**: split on the Fed path. Some firms have cut their year-end rate cut forecasts, while others maintain that the medium-term disinflation trend remains intact.
- **Business community and industry groups**: expressed frustration with the persistence of high rates and called on the Fed to adjust policy quickly to relieve corporate financing and labor market pressures.
- **Emerging markets and global institutions**: voiced concern over prolonged high US rates, warned that global capital flow imbalances could trigger another tightening of financial conditions, and called for stronger macro policy coordination among major economies.
