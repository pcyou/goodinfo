## Key Facts

- DoorDash announced on Thursday that it has agreed to acquire Instacart for $17 billion, combining its dominance in restaurant takeout with the grocery reach of its rival.
- DoorDash will buy Instacart for a total value of $65 for each Instacart share, and the deal has been approved by the boards of both companies.
- Instacart currently has a market capitalization of more than $13 billion, and its stock price is up 25 percent for the year to date.
- DoorDash plans to keep Instacart's brand, its enterprise technology products and its app, and the two brands will continue to operate as separate and independent companies until the transaction closes.
- DoorDash said the deal could help more grocers build their own online ordering platforms and advertising businesses using Instacart's technology, while grocers could also use DoorDash's fulfillment services to handle more online orders and offer lower-cost delivery.
- "Grocery is one of the hardest things to get right online," DoorDash CEO Tony Xu said in the announcement, adding that someone has to pick a ripe avocado, find a substitute a customer would choose, and get the ice cream there before it melts.
- The Instacart chief executive said the company had spent fourteen years transforming online grocery by helping retailers of all sizes compete and grow.
- Bloomberg previously reported that Instacart had been in talks with Uber, but that the company had walked away from those discussions.

## Viewpoints Compared

**The bull case** rests on the scarcity of fulfillment capability. The difficulty in grocery delivery is not getting the order there but getting it picked: choosing the right item, substituting for what is missing, handling the cold chain, and running enough order density to make each trip pay. Instacart has spent years accumulating that system, and what DoorDash is buying with $17 billion is the operating system rather than the user base.

**The bear case** is about price. Instacart's market capitalization is a little over $13 billion, so the deal embeds a premium of roughly thirty percent, and that premium sits with DoorDash shareholders. The dispute is over subsidy intensity: if a competitor chooses to compete for the same users with deeper subsidies, that premium can be consumed before the integration is finished.

**Merchant perspective** focuses on bargaining structure. DoorDash's public framing is that retailers will be able to use Instacart's technology to run their own ordering platforms and advertising businesses while also using DoorDash's fulfillment services. That positions DoorDash less as a traffic entry point and more as a layer of infrastructure, which is both a capability upgrade for retailers and a new dependency.

**The regulatory view** is not optional. Restaurant delivery and grocery delivery fall under different licensing and oversight categories in some jurisdictions, and a transaction of this size will enter antitrust review. Online grocery is not a concentrated market, but delivery density and the scale of temporary labour are natural reasons to combine, so the review will most likely focus on workers and on effects on competing services.

## Panoramic Analysis

The pricing logic in this deal is worth taking apart. Instacart's market capitalization is a little over $13 billion and the purchase price is $17 billion, a premium of roughly thirty percent. Paying a thirty percent premium for a fulfillment capability that is difficult to build and unexciting to describe is the standard way retail infrastructure gets priced: demand-side similarity is high and supply-side similarity is low.

The scarcity of fulfillment capability has been systematically underestimated during this round of instant retail competition. Restaurant delivery is hard because of dispatching capacity; grocery delivery is hard because of picking and shrinkage, and the second problem cannot be solved by better algorithms alone. It requires an operating system with genuine knowledge of the merchandise, and that system is built in years rather than quarters, because every improvement comes from a real out-of-stock item, a rejected delivery, a seasonal substitution. Such assets cannot be bought with subsidies and cannot be converted into compute. They can only be bought with time, which is why deals like this in retail infrastructure have historically supported premiums: the buyer is purchasing mistakes already made.

The real test comes after closing. DoorDash is keeping the Instacart brand, its enterprise technology products and its app, and the two brands run independently until close. That arrangement is necessary during a transition, but it sets up two opposite demands over time: one side wants the delivery fleets pooled to raise density, the other wants the brands kept distinct to protect merchant relationships. Whether the unit cost from higher density outweighs merchant attrition is what separates a deal that creates value from one that merely completes a financial combination.

There is a broader signal in the price. Selling a company worth a little over $13 billion for $17 billion says that capital now believes grocery delivery has moved past the phase of buying scale with burn and into a phase where unit economics can be discussed. That sits awkwardly against the two-year narrative in instant retail, where the most aggressive subsidy won. Two readings are available. Either the market has saturated and incremental growth must now come from efficiency, or competitors are pulling back toward the categories that actually carry margin. The two imply very different rhythms for what DoorDash spends next.

Finally, the deal signals something about the shape of the sector. If the leader in on-demand delivery chooses to acquire grocery capability rather than build it, competitors are left with two options: do the same, or compete inside a thinner profit pool for the same categories. Either path raises concentration over the coming year, and higher concentration eventually transmits to merchant commission rates and to the bargaining position of the workers who do the deliveries.
