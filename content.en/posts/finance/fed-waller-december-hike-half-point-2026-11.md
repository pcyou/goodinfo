## Key Facts

- **Where and when**: Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller spoke in London on Tuesday, hours before the October consumer price index release and less than a month before the December 8-9 policy meeting.
- **His core statement**: Waller said he "is leaning toward" a rate increase in December even if the October inflation data comes in softer than expected, and would support "more robust action" if readings come in higher than forecast.
- **The half-point option**: "In that case, I believe a 50 basis point hike in the policy rate should be considered at the December meeting," he said. "Hiking only 25 basis points would put our credibility at risk, a risk I am not eager to take."
- **On rhythm rather than sequence**: "When this communication is clear and credible, what really matters is that those hikes are eventually carried out — it is not necessary, for example, to raise rates at consecutive meetings."
- **The cost of not delivering**: "If, however, the hikes are not carried out, markets will reduce expectations of rate hikes and rates will immediately fall, which would loosen monetary policy in an undesirable way."
- **On urgency**: Higher rates "will be needed, sooner rather than later," Waller said, while adding that the precise timing does not matter as much as implementing the promise within a reasonable period.
- **The October decision he is answering**: The rate-setting committee passed in October on the view that "the accumulation of more information" would guide the path back to target. That decision was not unanimous — Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack dissented.
- **What the market had priced**: The CME Group's FedWatch tool showed better than an 80% chance of a quarter-point increase in December and no chance of a half-point.
- **The counter-warning**: Chicago Fed President Austan Goolsbee said on Monday the Fed has to be careful not to let the economy run too hot "because once it gets overheated, it's extremely painful to get rid of that kind of overheating."
- **The data due**: The Bureau of Labor Statistics releases the October consumer price index on Tuesday, one of the last inflation reports before the December 8-9 meeting.
- **His read on the economy**: Waller characterised the economy as growing "at a healthy pace" with a labor market that "has also been solid" if not dynamic in the current low-hire, low-fire climate.

## Competing Views

**Waller and Goolsbee are not disagreeing about direction; they are pricing the sequence differently.** Both accept that rates need to be higher. Waller fears a different failure mode, that a promise that is not carried out causes markets to reprice lower and thereby loosen policy exactly when the opposite is wanted. Goolsbee fears the opposite failure, that rates are raised into an economy that is already running hot and then have to be unwound at great cost. Putting both statements on the table on the same day, hours before the data, is itself informative about how unsettled the committee remains.

**Putting a market-priced-zero option on the table is active expectation management rather than a policy announcement.** FedWatch had assigned essentially no probability to a half-point move. Raising the option publicly, hours before the release, changes the variance of the market's reaction function even if the eventual move is a quarter-point. For anyone trading the December meeting, what changed on Tuesday was not the expected path but the width of its distribution.

**The political setting has quietly given Waller more room to argue about credibility.** With Democrats now holding both chambers after the midterms, direct pressure on governors from the executive branch has receded, and the new Congress arrives with a long oversight list. A governor who can now argue about credibility without being read as taking a side has an unusually strong position. That shift, from statement to argument, may matter more over the coming year than the half-point itself.

**For households and companies, the message is "for longer" rather than "faster."** Waller said explicitly that hikes need not come at consecutive meetings, which softens the near-term slope while making the destination more certain. Floating-rate corporate loans, commercial paper rollovers and prospective homebuyers are affected more by the certainty of the destination than by the pace of the climb.

**The asymmetry is the real risk.** If core inflation comes in clearly cooler, raising the half-point option ahead of the data may have been a communication exercise rather than a signal, and the credibility argument then runs in reverse, because the market has just been taught to expect larger moves. Correcting that would cost more than never having raised it.

## Panoramic Analysis

What matters about Tuesday is not the substance of Waller's sentence but its timing and venue. The path of the policy rate is set by the inflation data; the interpretation of that data is set by expectations management. By placing a half-point increase on the table hours before the release, Waller intervened directly in the probability distribution the market was about to resolve. Even if the eventual move is a quarter-point, the variance around the December decision has widened, and variance is what risk assets price most sensitively.

His argument deserves to be taken apart on its own terms. The case is not about how much to add but about whether promises get carried out. This is a direct lesson drawn from the 2025 easing cycle, when repeated deferrals were read by markets as evidence of a shallower path and rate expectations fell faster than the policy itself, forcing the committee to defend a communication it had already made. Credibility is a lever: it generates no new information, but it changes how the market reacts to the same information. After a cycle in which promised easing was repeatedly postponed, using credibility to justify tightening is a professional rather than an improvised choice.

The political backdrop is easy to miss. For most of the previous year, public statements from governors about the pace of cuts were read as alignment with the administration's position, and the severe midterm result has pushed the White House fully onto the defensive on personnel and messaging. The committee can now argue about the rate path itself rather than about political positioning. This shift from performing alignment to making an argument may prove more durable than the size of any single move.

A smaller detail carries real weight. The October hold was not unanimous, with Cook and Hammack dissenting. That means the committee's internal dispersion is wider than the communique suggests, and it means the market's baseline case of a quarter-point in December rests on something more solid than Waller's rhetoric: it does not require persuading every governor, only a majority that does not change its position once the data lands.

Four sets of numbers are worth watching. The housing-excluded core component of the October consumer price index will show whether inflation stickiness is confirmed. Other governors will say publicly in the run-up to December, and whether any of them echo the half-point option matters. The front end of the Treasury curve should steepen if the market has genuinely absorbed the wider distribution. And financing costs for mortgage applications and commercial paper will show whether any of this has begun to transmit. The third tells you whether the communication was absorbed; the fourth tells you whether it was anything more than talk.
