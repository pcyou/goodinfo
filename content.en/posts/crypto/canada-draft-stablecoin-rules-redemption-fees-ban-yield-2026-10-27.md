## Key Facts

- **Reserve requirement**: One-to-one backing, limited to cash and government debt maturing within three months or money market funds holding only such assets.
- **Custody**: Reserves must sit with a Canadian custodian in separate accounts that creditors cannot reach if the issuer fails.
- **Redemption**: Holders could redeem any amount within three business days, with fees capped at 1 percent.
- **Interest ban**: Issuers may not pay interest simply for holding a token, and routing it through third parties, including sharing issuer income, counts as indirect payment.
- **Use rewards**: Rewards tied to using a coin rather than holding it would remain permitted.
- **Marketing limits**: Issuers may not present a coin as a bank deposit or claim deposit insurance or a government guarantee.
- **Complaints**: Acknowledgement within seven days and resolution within 30 days.
- **Supervision**: The Bank of Canada registers and supervises issuers, with the notice assuming five issuers and about thirty payment companies under oversight.
- **Penalties**: Up to 1 million Canadian dollars for serious violations and 10 million for very serious ones, such as inadequate reserves or failure to redeem at par.
- **Timetable**: A 60-day consultation until roughly December 23, with the regulations intended to come into force in fall 2027.

## Comparison of Viewpoints

**The camp supporting the rules** emphasises payment efficiency. The Bank of Canada executive director for payments, supervision and oversight said at an Ottawa conference that there is a pressing need for faster, cheaper, more transparent and more accessible cross-border payment services. Strong constraints on reserves and redemption are what move a stablecoin from speculative asset toward payment instrument.

**The opposing camp** concentrates on the interest prohibition. The banking lobby argues that allowing platforms to let holders earn yield through third-party arrangements works around the core of the ban, and that deposits could drain from banks into crypto platforms. That dispute was divisive enough to contribute to the failure of the Clarity Act in the US Senate, a separate framework meant to set broader crypto market rules.

**A structural reading** follows the regulatory sequence. Canada is effectively following a path the United States has already taken: the Stablecoin Act became law on March 26, 2026, and the GENIUS Act was signed on July 18, 2025, both barring issuers from paying holders interest. What Canada adds at this stage is the operational detail on reserves, redemption and custody.

## Panoramic Analysis

What this draft records is that it settles in a single document the central question American legislation left open, and it gives a clear answer: no interest for holding, but rewards for using.

That distinction carries more weight than it appears to. In conventional banking logic, interest is the dividing line between a deposit and a money market fund. The moment interest is permitted, a stablecoin competes with deposits directly and the deposit base of the commercial banking system faces drain. Both the GENIUS Act and this Canadian draft therefore keep the prohibition. Reality is that platforms have already been substantively offering returns through membership rebates, points, cash back and third-party wealth-management integrations, and no regulator can forbid every form of it, only draw the line. The phrase tied to using is therefore a load-bearing guardrail: it lets platforms use rewards to pull genuine transaction volume while excluding pure holding arbitrage from the compliant category.

The second dimension worth watching is how concretely the reserve requirement is specified. Canada did not stop at a principle statement of full backing. It limited eligible assets to cash and government debt maturing within three months, required domestic custody in segregated accounts, and imposed bankruptcy remoteness. Taken together, these apply mature banking supervisory technique to a new form of money. The segregation and bankruptcy remoteness provisions are the most important, because they determine whether a holder is an ordinary creditor or takes priority when an issuer fails.

The third dimension is the cost of implementation and the transition window. A 60-day consultation, an expected coverage of five issuers and around thirty payment companies, and a commencement date in fall 2027 together indicate that regulators want existing business to adjust. That is pragmatic: an aggressive timetable would push issuance offshore to less transparent jurisdictions and reduce the reach of local supervision rather than strengthen it.

The fourth dimension is cross-border competition. Canada built its proposal around the exchange rate and interest rate environment, allowing issuers to keep what reserves earn provided reserves stay at least equal to tokens outstanding. In practice that provision is of limited consequence, because at current rates reserve income is negligible against a 1 percent redemption fee cap and against price volatility in the secondary market.

For observers, two nodes carry the real information. The first is whether the industry comments filed during the consultation concentrate on the boundary between interest and use rewards. The second is whether the final rules narrow that exemption. Together they determine how wide the compliant path actually is.
