## Core Summary

The Utah Attorney General's Office has filed a motion with the Third District Court asking to dismiss, without prejudice, fourteen criminal charges against prediction market platform Kalshi and its co-founders Tarek Mansour and Luana Lopes Lara. The company agreed to geofence Utah users and monitor access attempts from IP addresses in the state. The Attorney General's Office stressed that the move does not change its position: Utah law prohibits gambling, and prediction markets are gambling.

## Event Details

The charges comprise thirteen felony counts and one misdemeanor, filed on October 28. The Attorney General's Office opened its criminal investigation in September, alleging that Kalshi offered gambling to Utah residents. The two co-founders had been ordered to report to Salt Lake County Jail for booking ahead of their scheduled December 4 court appearance.

The motion follows the company's decision to suspend operations in Utah. In August, U.S. District Judge Robert Shelby ruled that federal commodities law does not prevent Utah from enforcing its anti-gambling laws against the platform. The Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals later denied Kalshi's request to block enforcement while its appeal proceeded. Kalshi subsequently announced a pause in Utah operations, noting that more than 177,000 Utah residents use the platform and that it would resume if its legal position were upheld.

Attorney General Derek Brown said the office had sent cease-and-desist letters to several Kalshi competitors, and that seven companies, including Kalshi, had agreed to cease operating in Utah. DraftKings, FanDuel and Fanatics have confirmed restrictions on event-contract trading in the state. Robinhood stopped offering sports, culture, election and political event contracts in Utah on November 2 while retaining financial and economic markets.

The state's civil lawsuit against Kalshi, filed last month, continues, with no settlement reached between the parties.

## Broad Perspective

This withdrawal is not a retreat of regulatory pressure but a change of gear. The state's rapid move from filing charges to requesting dismissal reflects the clear inefficiency of criminal enforcement when a company invokes federal jurisdictional defences. Even if the charges had held up, prosecutors would still have to prove case by case that prediction market transactions constitute gambling as defined under state law, a process easily bogged down in lengthy discovery and definitional disputes.

The without-prejudice dismissal was designed to preserve the option of refiling, giving both sides an off-ramp. For the state, civil litigation offers a far more controllable arena, where standards for civil wrong or unfair competition are more flexible and no criminal elements must be proven for each transaction. For Kalshi and its backers, continuing to assert federal commodities-market preemption against state law remains the essential path to operating nationwide.

The industry effect of seven companies exiting simultaneously deserves attention. Utah and Hawaii are the only two U.S. states with no legalised gambling of any kind, which has always made Utah the hardest market for prediction platforms to enter. When a state can use criminal charges to force a leading platform to geofence its own residents, and can bring an entire sector to heel within weeks, conservative state legislatures elsewhere are likely to view it as an enforceable template. This state-by-state zero-sum dynamic is structurally raising the compliance costs of the prediction market industry.

The decisive authority still rests with the U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission, which has filed an emergency request on the question of state enforcement power while the federal jurisdictional appeal proceeds. Kalshi general counsel Yaakov Roth said good-faith jurisdictional disagreements should never be criminalised. This dispute, framed as a clash between state and federal authority but centred on the legal characterisation of prediction markets, is set to continue.

## Multiple Perspectives

**The Utah Attorney General's Office** said its position has not changed, that Utah does not allow gambling and prediction markets are gambling, and that charges would return if Kalshi did. Brown told the industry that every company in the space should take note: if they promote, support or enable gambling, the state will hold them accountable.

**Kalshi** welcomed the dismissal of the criminal charges while noting that civil litigation continues and that it will keep fighting in court. A company spokesperson said it looks forward to returning to Utah once its legal position has been vindicated.

**Co-founder Luana Lopes Lara** had previously challenged the criminal charges, arguing that prediction market transactions fall under federal commodities law and that state gambling law cannot be applied to federally regulated contracts.

**Industry observers** point to fragmented state-level enforcement as the sector's largest source of uncertainty. For smaller emerging platforms, meeting compliance requirements across multiple states simultaneously is prohibitively expensive, which may further consolidate the industry toward the largest players.
