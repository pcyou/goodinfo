## [Brief] CFTC Chair Says Crypto Exchanges Are Seeking Federal Licenses as State Patchwork Frustrates Newcomers

## Key Facts

- **Chairman on record**: Commodity Futures Trading Commission chairman Michael Selig said the agency is already seeing demand from crypto exchanges planning to opt into its newly proposed rules for digital assets.
- **The proposal**: In October the regulator proposed rules aimed at preventing abuse in crypto markets and codifying crypto transaction rules under a federal rulebook. Selig called the move a critical step in ensuring the United States remains the crypto capital of the world.
- **Venue**: Selig spoke at Cantor Convergence, an annual fintech conference held in Miami Beach.
- **Exchange structure**: Many major crypto exchanges are already registered with the commission because they offer financial derivatives, including perpetual futures, and would now be able to add spot trading.
- **Newcomer rationale**: Selig relayed that new entrants say signing up with 50 different state regimes is tough and that they would rather deal with one regulator and obtain a licence.
- **Legislative backdrop**: After the Clarity Act, a comprehensive market structure bill, stalled in the Senate in September, the commission and the securities regulator moved to establish federal frameworks using their existing statutory authority.
- **Scope and limits**: Oversight is opt-in for exchanges not currently offering margin, leveraged or financed crypto assets, which is not permitted under existing state regimes.
- **Political window**: The rules could face new scrutiny in 2027, with Democrats set to take control of both chambers after the midterms. Selig said he hopes the United States will not see a wave of software developers moving offshore.

## Event Details

The top US commodities regulator has begun telling investors that the wave of crypto companies seeking to register under its rules is real rather than speculative. Selig said many of the major exchanges are already registered because they offer perpetual futures and other derivatives, and that the framework unveiled in October lets them extend that registration to spot trading.

He also offered a clearer explanation of why newcomers want in. Quoting what he described as new entrants, Selig said that signing up with 50 different state regimes is tough and that these firms would rather come to one regulator and get a licence at the commission. That is a significant admission from a federal official, and it reframes the federalisation of crypto oversight from an abstract argument about efficiency into a concrete statement about a specific cost that companies are currently bearing.

The framework was built after the legislative route failed. With the Clarity Act stalled in the Senate in September, the commission and the securities regulator proceeded under the authority they already hold, rather than waiting for Congress to write new statute.

## Competing Views

**The regulator's logic is lower compliance cost.** One federal rulebook that can be read in one place is more predictable than fifty, and for a new entrant the effective cost of entry falls even if nothing else changes.

**From the exchange side, spot trading is a new revenue line.** A company already registered for derivatives can add spot under the same framework at modest additional cost, which is a meaningful competitive difference in a business where fee structures are thin.

**The cautious view is about what an opt-in mechanism leaves in place.** Coverage follows commercial incentive rather than legal compulsion, so the portion of the market that stays outside the federal perimeter remains governed at state level. The two-tier structure does not disappear; it changes shape.

## Panoramic Analysis

What is really happening here is a shift in how the United States builds its crypto regime, from waiting for legislation to writing rules under authority that already exists. With the market structure bill stalled in the Senate in September, two regulators chose to construct a federal framework from existing statute. The advantage of that route is speed. The cost is that the boundary of the authority being exercised will be questioned continuously, because it routes around Congress and places rule-making authority in the executive branch. The scrutiny these rules face in 2027 is a direct consequence of that procedural choice, and the question asked at that review will not be whether the substance is sensible but why this route was chosen at all.

A second layer is the structural tension inside an opt-in mechanism. For exchanges that do not currently offer margin, leveraged or financed products, joining the federal perimeter is voluntary, and because that business is not permitted under the existing state regimes, the economic case for opting in is considerable. But it follows that the actual reach of the federal framework is set by commercial motivation rather than by legal compulsion, and the part of the market that declines to opt in stays where it is. For an investor, that produces not uniform protection but a need to work out for themselves which set of rules governs the venue they are using.

The third layer is that the regulator chose to quote the commercial rationale in public. Relaying the complaint that fifty state regimes are too much to manage is an official admitting that state-level fragmentation has become a genuine barrier to entry. That is also the most persuasive argument available for a unified framework, because it is concrete where the usual efficiency case is abstract, and it comes from the firms the rule is supposed to attract rather than from the rule-makers themselves.

Four indicators are worth watching: how many spot-trading exchanges formally file to register; the actual compliance cost and inspection frequency experienced by companies already inside the federal framework; whether state regulators reduce enforcement intensity once federal licences are available; and whether the 2027 congressional review demands the complete rule-making record rather than only the resulting text.
