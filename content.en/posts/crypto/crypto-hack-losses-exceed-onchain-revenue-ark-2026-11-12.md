## Key Facts

- **Year to date**: Hackers have taken $8.7 billion from the crypto industry in 2026 so far, already above the roughly $5.6 billion in projected annual onchain revenue.
- **The headline ratio**: That works out to 57 cents lost for every dollar of onchain revenue generated by protocols since 2017.
- **Historical frame**: Since 2017 the industry has lost about $26.9 billion to hacks and earned roughly $47 billion in total onchain revenue.
- **The single largest event**: The $6.3 billion Binance incident alone exceeded Valente's estimate for full-year 2026 onchain revenue.
- **A first since 2020**: On current pace, value lost to hacks is on track to exceed onchain revenue by year-end for the first time since 2020.
- **Concentration this year**: A string of high-value incidents including a $387 million Bitget hack in September, a $320 million Liquid Network exploit in October and a $295 million Drift Protocol exploit in April.
- **Industry contraction**: Total value locked in decentralised finance has fallen nearly 10%, from $85.9 billion on October 29 to $77.4 billion this week, according to DeFiLlama.

## Competing Views

**One camp locates the problem in infrastructure.** JPMorgan analysts identified bridge security as a long-standing challenge for the industry in an April research note. On this reading the losses are not a pile of unrelated accidents but the same class of defect reappearing across different projects. Valente's phrasing, that security is the bottleneck, matches that judgement: when returns scale automatically while defence still depends on manual audit and after-the-fact recovery, the gap only widens with size.

**Another camp objects that losses and revenue are not directly subtractable.** The cautious position is that onchain revenue is a continuing protocol cash flow while stolen assets include one-off event shocks, and dividing one by the other produces a rhetorical figure rather than an accounting result. That criticism has force, but it does not explain why investor behaviour has changed accordingly.

**A third view comes from institutional appetite.** The mounting exploits are visibly weighing on institutional appetite for decentralised finance, and the decline in locked value since the October incident is nearly ten percent. That is not a mood-driven dip but a contraction in scale. When the revenue side shrinks while the loss side grows, the industry's capital efficiency problem stops being arguable.

**The structural comparison matters more than the absolute figure.** The distance between $8.7 billion and the 2020 reference point is what carries the meaning. While losses sat below revenue, security spending could be read as the cost of doing business. Once losses exceed revenue, it becomes a separate line that eats the industry's output. That changes the financing narrative for the whole sector and changes how regulators think about why tokenised assets need a different regime for custody and insurance.

## Panoramic Analysis

Placed on the same page, losses and revenue are what give this calculation its weight. Until now the industry discussed security through indicators such as the number of major incidents or the size of individual thefts. Those describe pressure but cannot answer the relationship between pressure and output. Valente's method changes the denominator to what the industry itself earned, so the question stops being how large the losses are and becomes what share of production they represent. The answer lands in an unambiguous place: the share has passed the level of value creation.

The structural effect on the industry is not sentiment but architecture. A stolen asset does not only cost the victim; it also carries weeks of contraction in locked value, delayed institutional entry, higher audit costs and increased regulatory appetite for rules on custody and listing. None of that appears in the loss figure, yet all of it reduces the industry's net output. Once the ratio of losses to revenue passes one to one, the industry in accounting terms stops creating value and begins transferring it. Value still moves along the chain, but it moves from participants to attackers.

A more invisible layer is the economics of scale on defence. The defending side must pay continuous human and audit cost for every chain, every bridge and every cross-chain message format, while the attacking side needs only one reusable gap. As the number of chains and protocols grows, the attack surface expands roughly linearly and defensive cost cannot fall in proportion. A small team running tokenised finance has almost no chance of protecting itself independently here. That is not a question of engineering skill but of economic scale.

Four indicators are worth watching. Whether the share of bridge and cross-chain vulnerabilities in total losses keeps rising. Whether institutional capital is pausing or exiting structurally. Whether net onchain revenue after security spend is being tracked at all. And whether the regulatory framework begins assigning custody and insurance responsibility to issuers explicitly. Any one of them producing verifiable change would show whether the industry is repairing its security model or merely redistributing risk. Until then, this $8.7 billion is better described as the first reading from a physical rather than an alarm.
