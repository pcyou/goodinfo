## [Brief] Coinbase National Trust Company Cleared to Open as Senators Demand Approval Records

## Key Facts

- **Final approval**: Coinbase announced on November 9 that its national trust company had received final approval to open. The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency granted preliminary conditional approval in April, and the company says the charter gives its custody business a consistent federal framework.
- **Earlier challenge**: On May 18, Senator Elizabeth Warren wrote to the currency regulator challenging at least nine crypto trust charter approvals, including Coinbase's earlier conditional approval, and asked for the applications, the legal analyses behind the approvals and any related communications.
- **New records demand**: Four days before the Coinbase announcement, Senator Richard Blumenthal renewed demands for records from Binance, Tether and two ventures linked to the current administration. He gave them until January 4, 2027 to turn over the requested records voluntarily and warned that his subcommittee could take other steps to obtain them.
- **Subject of scrutiny**: Lawmakers want to know how regulators justified new trust charters, how crypto companies handled compliance, and what dealings they had with people tied to the current administration.
- **Industry backdrop**: Crypto companies have been building compliant links to traditional finance through trust charter applications and, in some cases, acquisitions of licensed banking institutions.

## Event Details

Coinbase's national trust company has received final approval to open, giving the exchange's custody business a single federal framework under one roof. The conditional approval granted in April is now complete, and the announcement came with the company describing the charter as the thing that finally makes custody consistent rather than fragmented.

The congressional response moved in the opposite direction, from expanding a programme towards demanding the paperwork behind it. Senator Warren's May letter questioned at least nine crypto trust charter approvals and asked for the applications themselves, the legal reasoning behind each approval and any related communications with officials. Two weeks ago, Senator Blumenthal renewed demands for records from Binance, Tether and two ventures linked to the administration, setting a voluntary deadline of January 4, 2027 while noting his subcommittee could take other steps.

The two tracks are not unrelated. One expands the perimeter of what crypto companies may do under existing authority; the other asks whether that authority was applied the way the statute describes.

## Competing Views

**The regulator's logic is the normalisation of custody.** A trust charter gives custody a single federal framework, which is simpler for institutional clients than navigating a patchwork of state regimes, and the currency regulator has been the main route by which that consolidation has happened.

**The legislators' concern is about the boundary of the authority being used.** The charter itself is not what is contested. What is contested is whether the basis for approval is publicly verifiable and whether anything in the approval process needs explaining.

**A third view points to the absence of a decision standard.** There is no published rulebook and no compiled body of precedent, so each approval is decided individually, and once the number of such decisions is large enough, the absence of a standard becomes a problem in its own right.

## Panoramic Analysis

The real significance of this cluster of developments is that it turns crypto licensing from a series of approvals into a procedure that can be examined one decision at a time. Historically each trust charter came and went as an announcement. What the senators are asking for is different: the basis on which approval was granted, who made the call, and against what criteria. Once the number of such decisions is large enough to constitute a body of precedent, case-by-case approval generates its own unwritten norm, and that is exactly where a public record becomes useful.

The second layer is the separation between procedure and authority. Congress failed to move a comprehensive market structure bill in September, so the regulators proceeded under powers they already held in order to widen what these companies may do. That choice creates an authority boundary question, and the request for approval records and correspondence is in substance a request that the agencies demonstrate that the expansions are a natural extension of existing statute rather than a redefinition of its limits. The distinction may look narrow in any single case. It is not a narrow distinction institutionally.

The third layer is the realism of the timeline. A voluntary deadline of January 4, 2027 is generous, and generous deadlines usually indicate an expectation that the request will not be met voluntarily, with the likely route being a subpoena or a committee compulsion. That makes it more accurate to read these demands not as routine information gathering but as senators building a documentary foundation ahead of a formal investigation that may become possible in 2027.

Four indicators are worth watching: whether the currency regulator publishes the approval standard and any compiled precedent for trust charters; whether Binance, Tether and the affiliated entities submit records within the stated window; whether a formal subpoena or committee compulsion follows; and the scope and witness list of whatever review a new congressional majority conducts in 2027.
