## Core Summary

Senate Republicans on September 14 released the "final draft" of the long-pending Clarity Act for digital assets, while the White House confirmed that President Trump has accepted most of the bill's ethics provisions. The new draft makes significant adjustments to digital-asset classification, the allocation of regulatory authority between the SEC and CFTC, stablecoin reserve requirements and disclosure of conflicts of interest. Industry analysts say the release breaks a multi-month stalemate that had previously been described by industry observers as a Schrödinger-style legislative limbo, and could clear the path for a full Senate vote in the coming weeks.

## Event Details

**Key adjustments in the final draft**: On September 14, core Republican members of the Senate Banking Committee and the Senate Agriculture Committee jointly released a hundred-plus-page "final draft" of the Clarity Act. The draft makes new and explicit determinations on a series of contested questions: whether digital assets should be classified as securities or commodities, how stablecoin reserves should be custodied and disclosed, and whether spot and derivatives markets in digital assets should be supervised by the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) or the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC). The draft also adds new conflict-of-interest disclosure requirements for executives and lobbyists of crypto firms, restrictions on related-party transactions, and "revolving-door" cooling-off provisions for former federal officials entering the industry.

**Shift in the Trump administration's posture**: White House officials and the House Republican Conference chair confirmed on the same day that President Trump has accepted "most of the ethics provisions" in the draft, despite earlier reservations expressed by Trump himself and the White House Counsel's Office about some of the stricter conflict-of-interest provisions. The shift is widely viewed as a critical political signal that the bill can move forward in the Senate. Multiple U.S. political media outlets argued that the change reflects Trump's interest in delivering on a campaign pledge to the crypto industry to establish a clear regulatory framework before the end of the year.

**Contested points in the draft**: First, the securities-commodity boundary. The draft leans toward placing more crypto assets under CFTC supervision, lowering SEC reach into spot markets, while retaining a backstop that treats tokens with financing characteristics as securities. Second, stablecoin reserve requirements. The draft requires issuers to meet higher standards for reserve composition, custodian qualifications and audit frequency, while offering compliant issuers a limited federal payments-charter pathway. Third, conflict-of-interest disclosure and revolving-door restrictions. The draft requires crypto executives and lobbyists to periodically disclose contacts with foreign governments, members of Congress and White House officials, and imposes cooling-off periods on former federal officials entering the industry.

**Coordination between the White House and Congress**: The office of the Senate Majority Leader said it would hold multiple rounds of consultations with the White House Office of Legislative Affairs in the days following the draft's release, with the goal of placing the bill on the Senate's legislative calendar during the post-recess window. The chair of the House Financial Services Committee said on the same day that the House would move quickly to convene a conference once the Senate passes its version, to avoid prolonged bicameral differences.

**Industry reaction**: Coverage by CoinDesk and The Block cited initial feedback from crypto firms and law-firm partners indicating that the final draft relaxes some requirements on stablecoin issuers and spot-trading platforms compared with the prior House version, but introduces conflict-of-interest and revolving-door provisions that the industry had not previously anticipated. Some of these provisions could affect the day-to-day operations of certain crypto investment funds and family offices. AI and compute-related firms are also watching additional provisions in the draft on data-center energy use and carbon-emission disclosure.

**International regulatory signals**: The European Commissioner responsible for financial services said at a press conference on September 14 that the Commission is closely tracking the U.S. legislative progress and will consider adjusting parts of the Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCA) during its 2027 midterm review. The Monetary Authority of Singapore and the Securities and Futures Commission of Hong Kong have, in different settings, said they are holding informal discussions with U.S. regulators on cross-border stablecoin coordination.

## Full-Spectrum Perspective

The release of the "final draft" of the Clarity Act is the most substantive progress in years in the long-stalemated U.S. digital-asset regulatory process. Its impact extends well beyond the crypto industry itself and will reshape the U.S. financial regulatory architecture, the relationship between Washington and Silicon Valley, and the pace of global digital-asset regulatory coordination.

**A historic breakthrough in the long U.S. digital-asset regulatory stalemate**. For nearly a decade, U.S. regulators — including the SEC, CFTC, Treasury and the Department of Justice — have oscillated in their approach to crypto, with enforcement actions standing in for legislation. Coinbase, Ripple and Binance have all been mired in protracted legal battles. If the final draft is enacted, it would for the first time provide federal-level answers to the securities-commodity boundary, stablecoin reserves and the allocation of spot-versus-derivatives supervisory authority, marking a historic shift from "enforcement instead of legislation" to "rules instead of ambiguity."

**The Trump administration's policy shift has a deep political-economic logic**. Trump's earlier reservations about the ethics provisions of the Clarity Act stemmed primarily from concerns that strict conflict-of-interest disclosure and revolving-door restrictions might impede the White House's close ties with the crypto industry. The decision to accept "most of the ethics provisions" reflects, on one hand, the desire to deliver on a campaign pledge to establish a clear regulatory framework before year's end, and on the other, a judgment that the political cost of failing to deliver outweighs the cost of accepting the ethics provisions. The calculation further entrenches the crypto industry's position as a key swing constituency in U.S. elections.

**The securities-commodity boundary is a defining question for global digital-asset regulation**. As the world's largest digital-asset market, the U.S. regulatory path has a strong spillover effect on the global industry. The draft's tilt toward CFTC supervision of more crypto assets would reduce SEC reach into spot markets and give more tokens a path to compliant listing and trading. But if the U.S. classification deviates too sharply from those of other major economies, cross-border compliance conflicts could intensify, leaving multinational crypto firms to navigate complex regulatory and arbitrage challenges across jurisdictions.

**Stablecoin reserve requirements and federal charter pathways are pivotal for the stablecoin industry**. The draft offers compliant stablecoin issuers a limited federal payments-charter pathway, marking the first time stablecoins have been brought under a unified federal framework. The shift could move stablecoins from "grey-market financial infrastructure" to "regulated payment instruments," accelerating adoption by traditional financial institutions, e-commerce platforms and cross-border payment networks. At the same time, higher capital, reserve and audit requirements could squeeze smaller issuers.

**Conflict-of-interest disclosure and revolving-door restrictions create a new compliance frontier for crypto**. The draft is the first to bring federal-level conflict-of-interest and revolving-door rules into the crypto industry. It would require periodic disclosures of contacts with foreign governments, members of Congress and White House officials, and impose cooling-off periods on former federal officials entering the industry. These provisions could significantly affect crypto investment funds, family offices and lobbying firms with close U.S. political ties, and may inspire other major economies to strengthen "firewalls" between politicians and the crypto industry.

**A window for global digital-asset regulatory coordination is opening**. The U.S. legislative progress will have important implications for the EU's MiCA, the U.K.'s crypto promotion and disclosure regime, and the stablecoin frameworks in Singapore and Hong Kong. If the Clarity Act is enacted and produces a clear classification and supervisory path, it could provide a much-needed "anchor standard" for global coordination. Conversely, if it stalls or fails in the Senate, it would deepen fragmentation and uncertainty in global digital-asset regulation.

## Comparison of Perspectives

**Core Senate Banking and Agriculture Committee Republicans** describe the "final draft" as a "historic advance" in digital-asset regulation, arguing that it strikes a pragmatic balance on classification, stablecoin reserves and supervisory allocation, while preserving U.S. leadership in global digital-asset competition.

**The White House Office of Legislative Affairs and the White House Counsel's Office** said Trump's acceptance of "most of the ethics provisions" was a necessary political compromise, but stressed that legislation should not impair normal channels of communication between the White House and the crypto industry, and called on Congress to preserve flexibility in subsequent drafting.

**Major crypto firms — Coinbase, Ripple, Circle** — broadly welcomed the draft, calling it a key step toward ending the long U.S. regulatory stalemate. Some, however, expressed reservations about the conflict-of-interest and revolving-door provisions, which they view as potentially too strict and disruptive to normal talent flow and policy engagement.

**CoinDesk and The Block** note that the draft's adjustments to digital-asset classification and stablecoin oversight mark the fruits of years of industry lobbying, but that the new ethics provisions reflect a more complex and multi-faceted Washington political environment toward crypto.

**The European Commission services commissioner** said the Commission is closely tracking U.S. legislative progress and will consider adjusting parts of MiCA in the 2027 midterm review to make the two jurisdictions' frameworks more coordinated and to avoid cross-border compliance conflicts and regulatory arbitrage.

**The Monetary Authority of Singapore and the Hong Kong Securities and Futures Commission** have, in different settings, said they are holding informal discussions with U.S. regulators on cross-border stablecoin coordination, and stressed that such coordination is a key issue requiring consensus among major economies on standards, reserve audits and cross-border data sharing.

**Some Democratic senators and consumer-advocacy groups** criticized the draft as overly tilted toward the crypto industry, arguing that it inadequately addresses investor protection, market-manipulation prevention and systemic-risk containment, and could exacerbate the transmission of crypto risks into the traditional financial system.

**Academic and policy-research institutions** broadly view the draft as an important milestone in U.S. digital-asset regulatory progress, but call for more attention in subsequent drafting to stablecoin systemic risk, spillover between crypto and traditional finance, and cross-border coordination.
