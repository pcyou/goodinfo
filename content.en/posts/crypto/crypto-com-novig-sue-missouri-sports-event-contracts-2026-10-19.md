## Key Facts

Sports-event prediction-market operators Crypto.com and Novig have filed suit against Missouri financial regulators to challenge the state's cease-and-desist orders on sports event contracts. Kalshi, Polymarket, Robinhood and Underdog, which received similar orders, have held off on litigation according to court records. The core legal question is whether sports-event contracts are sports betting under Missouri state law, or derivatives trades under the federal commodity-futures regulatory framework. The case is widely viewed as a marker in the broader US state-versus-federal clash over how to classify sports prediction markets.

## What We Know

On October 18, Crypto.com and Novig each filed suit in Missouri state or federal court against state financial regulators, seeking a ruling that the cease-and-desist orders on sports event contracts are invalid and requesting a temporary restraining order to block enforcement during the litigation. Filings from both platforms argue that their products qualify as event contracts under the federal Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) framework rather than as sports betting under Missouri state law, and should therefore be regulated at the federal level rather than by state gaming authorities.

Before the Crypto.com and Novig filings, Kalshi, Polymarket, Robinhood and Underdog had received similar cease-and-desist notices from Missouri regulators but had chosen a wait-and-see approach rather than file suit in the same window. Industry counsel say several platforms are watching for the next enforcement step from Missouri before deciding whether to bring parallel challenges in other states.

The broader backdrop is the long-standing US split over how to classify sports-event contracts. Some states treat them as sports betting that requires a state gaming licence; others lean toward treating them as derivatives regulated by the CFTC. Missouri's actions have sharpened that dispute, and the industry expects the resulting ruling to influence the regulatory trajectory in several other states.

Notably, both Crypto.com and Novig are seeking temporary injunctive relief to keep their platforms operating in Missouri during litigation — a step designed to avoid knock-on effects on user balances, open counterparty contracts and active event markets. The litigation is widely viewed as a marker event in the US state-versus-federal contest over sports prediction markets.

## Wider Perspective

### 1. The line between state and federal regulatory authority

Crypto.com and Novig's suits directly test the authority of state regulators over what they argue are federally regulated financial derivatives. In the two-year tug-of-war between the CFTC and state regulators over event contracts, this is the first clear, headline-platform-led challenge to a state-level prohibition, and the outcome could redraw the enforcement perimeter that state agencies are able to draw around similar products.

### 2. Reconstructing the legality framework for sports prediction markets

The ruling will shape product design, market access and consumer-protection standards for US sports prediction markets. Platforms want to operate under an event-contract framework that enables cross-state expansion without the cost of obtaining a gaming licence in every state; state regulators worry about consumer protection, underage access and problem gambling. The litigation will define the structure of the market for years to come.

### 3. Diverging compliance paths for crypto and traditional finance

Sports prediction markets are seen as a flagship route for the crypto industry to enter mainstream financial workflows. If the suits fail, more platforms may exit certain states or migrate to offshore structures; if they succeed, event-contract products could be more readily embraced by traditional brokerages and banks, accelerating the sector's compliance-led transformation.

### 4. Short-term impact on user assets and market liquidity

During the litigation, if state regulators take more aggressive enforcement steps, users in some states could face forced position closures, withdrawal delays or thinner liquidity. Platforms will need to balance litigation strategy with user communications to prevent a compliance fight from triggering a broader confidence shock.

## Viewpoints Compared

- **Crypto.com and Novig:** Argue that their products are federally regulated event contracts, that state regulators lack jurisdiction to treat them as gambling, and that a victory would give them a clear cross-state legal footing while avoiding the cost of acquiring a gaming licence in every state.
- **Missouri financial regulators:** Treat sports-event contracts as functionally equivalent to sports betting, contend that platforms offering such products without a Missouri gaming licence are violating state law, and emphasise the consumer-protection rationale for stopping unlicensed operators.
- **Kalshi, Polymarket, Robinhood and Underdog:** Received the same cease-and-desist notices but, as of now, have not filed suit in Missouri. They are working with outside counsel to time any litigation and may launch parallel actions in other states.
- **Consumer-protection groups:** Express concern over the speed at which sports prediction markets are expanding and want clear age verification, anti-addiction safeguards and early-intervention mechanisms for at-risk users regardless of how the litigation is resolved.
