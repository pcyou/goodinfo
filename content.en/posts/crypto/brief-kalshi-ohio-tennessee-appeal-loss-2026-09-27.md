## Core Summary

The Block reported on Friday that a federal appeals court ruled Kalshi has not adequately demonstrated that its sports event contracts qualify as swaps under the Commodity Exchange Act. The decision leaves the prediction market platform on the losing side of litigation against Ohio and Tennessee sports betting regulators and widens the split among US federal circuits on the regulatory status of event contracts.

## Event Details

The legal dispute centres on whether Kalshi's contracts on the outcome of sports events should be classified as swaps regulated under federal commodity law, or as sports betting products subject to state gambling regulators. Ohio and Tennessee argued that Kalshi's products are functionally sports betting and should fall under state jurisdiction. Kalshi contended that the contracts meet the statutory definition of swaps and should be governed primarily by the federal framework.

In its ruling, the appeals court concluded that Kalshi failed to provide sufficient legal argument on the key elements required to qualify the contracts as swaps, and therefore upheld the lower court's unfavourable judgment. The decision contrasts with a separate circuit court ruling in which Kalshi prevailed, intensifying the divergence among US federal appellate courts on the legal nature of prediction market products.

## Multi Party Viewpoint Comparison

Kalshi indicated it will evaluate further legal options, including potential recourse to a higher court. State level gaming regulators welcomed the ruling as an important confirmation of state regulatory authority. Other prediction market operators are monitoring the case closely, concerned that similar adverse precedents may spread to additional states. Academic and regulatory researchers noted that the widening circuit split may ultimately push Congress to intervene and clarify the regulatory classification of prediction markets through legislation.

## Panoramic Perspective

Viewed through a wider lens, the Kalshi case is symptomatic of a broader institutional struggle to define prediction markets. Whether event contracts are financial instruments or gambling products has divided the US regulatory system for two years, with proponents highlighting financial functions such as price discovery and risk hedging while critics warn of weakened consumer protection and anti addiction safeguards. The widening circuit split means that, in the absence of unified federal legislation, prediction market platforms must operate under conflicting legal regimes across states and circuits, raising compliance costs and strategic uncertainty for the industry as a whole.
