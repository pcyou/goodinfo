## Core Summary

The US Senate failed to clear a procedural vote on the Clarity Act, effectively killing what the crypto industry had called its "most important legislative priority" for this Congress. Industry leaders such as Coinbase and Andreessen Horowitz had poured millions of dollars into lobbying, but ultimately the bill collapsed under partisan infighting. The result means US crypto regulation will remain in its current gray zone, with deep implications for both near-term operations and long-term industry development.

## Event Details

### The Key Procedural Vote

According to The Washington Post, the Senate's procedural vote on the Clarity Act failed to secure the simple majority needed to advance the bill. The result effectively "kills" the legislation for this Congress; restarting the process will require waiting until at least the next Congress.

### The Industry's Failed Political Mobilization

The New York Times noted that Coinbase, Andreessen Horowitz, and other crypto leaders had invested millions of dollars in lobbying over the past months and organized coalitions like "Stand With Crypto" to leverage electoral pressure on lawmakers.

The final outcome proved that despite significant gains in political influence, the industry's policy clout in Washington remains insufficient to bridge fundamental partisan divides.

### The Three Dimensions of Partisan Division

According to outlets including The Hill, partisan disagreements around the Clarity Act centered on three main axes:

1. **Regulator**: Republicans prefer Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) oversight; Democrats insist on the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) retaining primacy.
2. **Consumer Protection**: Democrats demand stronger anti-fraud and investor-protection provisions; Republicans fear excessive regulation will crush innovation.
3. **Stablecoins**: The two sides were deeply split on stablecoin reserves, audit requirements, and issuance thresholds, which became the immediate trigger for the final breakdown.

### Industry Media's Inside Reconstruction

CoinDesk provided an in-depth reconstruction of the "last-minute political breakdown": core negotiators from both parties had reached principled consensus on several disputed provisions, but the sudden intervention of the White House and the Senate Banking Committee over the stablecoin clauses led to a total collapse.

## Panoramic Analysis

**First, the US crypto "policy vacuum" enters a new phase.** The Clarity Act was seen by the industry as the key legislation to end the "dual SEC vs CFTC oversight" deadlock. Its failure means the US crypto sector will continue to face overlapping jurisdiction and inconsistent enforcement standards between the two regulators. In the short term, the SEC may tighten enforcement against unregistered securities-like tokens; in the longer term, the absence of a clear framework will suppress large-scale institutional entry.

**Second, stablecoin oversight becomes the biggest lingering issue.** The largest sticking point was the stablecoin section. Stablecoins are the most important infrastructure in the crypto ecosystem, and issues around reserve transparency, audit standards, and cross-border circulation will not disappear with the bill's failure. The Treasury and SEC are expected to address them case by case through executive orders and regulatory guidance, but this fragmented approach will raise compliance costs and may drive stablecoin issuers to migrate across borders in search of regulatory arbitrage.

**Third, the crypto industry's political strategy needs fundamental rethinking.** The strategy of heavy political donations pursued by Coinbase and a16z did not deliver the expected result in this legislative cycle. The reason: crypto issues are deeply divisive within both parties—Republicans have a free-market wing versus a traditional-finance wing, while Democrats have a progressive wing versus a moderate wing. Simply throwing money cannot bridge these structural fissures.

**Fourth, the global regulatory competition is being reshaped.** The US legislative failure opens a window for the EU, Singapore, and the UAE to offer greater "regulatory attractiveness." Frameworks such as Singapore's Payment Services Act, the EU's MiCA, and Dubai's VARA are already attracting crypto companies to relocate headquarters or core operations outside the United States. This "regulatory competition" means the US could lose its global digital-asset leadership position over the next 5 to 10 years.

## Multiple Perspectives

**Crypto Industry Associations**: Coinbase's chief policy officer expressed "deep disappointment" but stressed that "the political power of the crypto voter has awakened; the 2028 election will be the industry's decisive battle." The Crypto Innovation Council called on the White House and SEC to proactively fill the legislative vacuum through administrative guidance.

**Democratic Senators**: Senior Democrats on the Senate Banking Committee accused Republicans of "sacrificing consumer protection to protect big business interests" and pledged to "relaunch a stricter crypto regulatory framework" in the next Congress.

**Republican Senators**: Some Republican lawmakers argued the bill's failure was "good news for innovators," stressing that "excessive securities regulation would crush US global competitiveness in digital assets." They advocated replacing the SEC-led model with a CFTC-led "light-touch" framework.

**Traditional Financial Institutions**: Executives from JPMorgan and Goldman Sachs had previously voiced support for the Clarity Act, arguing clear regulation is conducive to institutional capital entering crypto. After the bill's failure, some institutions may "pause large-scale crypto allocation plans" while waiting for regulatory clarity.

**International Regulators**: The European Systemic Risk Board (ESRB) and the Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) have both publicly stressed that "clear regulation is a key factor in attracting crypto companies." The US legislative failure will further strengthen Europe's and Asia's regulatory attractiveness.
