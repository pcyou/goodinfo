## Key Facts

Tether chief executive Paolo Ardoino said on Tuesday that the company holds about 150,000 Bitcoin, worth roughly $11.5 billion at current prices. The figure sits about 50,000 coins above what the company's own third-quarter reserve attestation implies, a gap close to $3.8 billion.

- **The statement**: speaking at the Plan ₿ Forum in Lugano, Ardoino said the company has "give or take 150,000 bitcoin ourselves. It's not much, but it's honest work." Tether has run a Bitcoin and stablecoin adoption programme in the Swiss city since 2022.
- **What the filing says**: the third-quarter attestation covers the assets backing USDT and lists $8.37 billion of Bitcoin valued at $83,621 per coin, implying roughly 100,060 coins.
- **What was not explained**: Ardoino said nothing about where the additional Bitcoin is held or when it was acquired, so the statement does not indicate a fresh purchase of that size. Tether has long maintained that its corporate Bitcoin sits separately from the reserves supporting USDT.
- **The wider balance sheet**: the quarterly report shows $191.45 billion of assets against $183.50 billion of liabilities, an excess cushion of $7.95 billion, close to double the $4.11 billion reported in June. United States Treasury bills remain the bulk of reserves at roughly $132.4 billion.
- **The profit behind it**: the company reported $1.5 billion in net operating profit in the third quarter alone and has posted billion-dollar quarterly profits throughout the year while USDT supply continued to grow.
- **Strategy**: in May 2023 Tether said it would funnel up to 15 percent of net realised operating profits into Bitcoin and hold the coins itself rather than through third-party custodians.
- **Scale**: Ardoino said the company now serves more than 730 million users and is adding roughly 30 million wallets a quarter.
- **Audit**: Tether began its first full financial audit with KPMG this year, a long-awaited step on the question of whether reserves are fully backed.

## Competing Views

**The company frames this as a scoping difference rather than a contradiction.** Corporate Bitcoin and stablecoin reserves are said to be separate pools, so the gap need not represent anything. The difficulty is that this distinction had previously appeared as a general company statement, whereas it now came from the chief executive with a specific number attached, which is what made it news.

**The market reaction has centred on the size of the gap, not on whether it exists.** Fifty thousand coins worth close to $3.8 billion is small against $191.45 billion of total assets, roughly two percent. What makes it consequential is the timing: it surfaced in the same quarter as the first full audit.

**The buffer provides a second reading.** The excess cushion nearly doubled from $4.11 billion in June to $7.95 billion. Read one way, that is improving asset quality. Read another, a company running a larger balance sheet outside its reserve line may need a larger cushion to absorb it.

**The audit thread matters most of all.** A first full audit is a different kind of exercise from a quarterly attestation. Attestation checks a moment in time; an audit examines historical periods. If the two conclusions diverge, the consequence will far exceed this particular discrepancy.

## Panoramic Analysis

A stablecoin issuer with assets in the hundreds of billions telling a conference room, in public, that it holds fifty thousand more Bitcoin than its own filing shows is not a discrepancy that can be waved aside as a matter of internal accounting convention.

The real subject is comparability. A quarterly reserve attestation is a document with a clearly stated scope: it covers assets backing USDT, and it carries no obligation to list Bitcoin the company holds on its own corporate account. That is clean accounting. It is also exactly why the attestation becomes the only factual baseline available to anyone trying to work out how much this company actually owns, and when two numbers appear side by side in public, the public does not make the technical distinction. It remembers the gap.

At roughly two percent of total assets, the gap does not threaten solvency. What deserves tracking runs across three levels. Whether the next attestation will bring corporate Bitcoin inside the disclosed scope, which would change what the document means, or leave the gap in place and therefore in the questions. What the first full audit concludes, and over what period. And whether the definition of a reserve itself tightens, because a concept that admits two readings will eventually be used in both ways at once, usually at a moment when nobody has time to be careful.

There is a wider structural point too. Every expansion Tether has made in recent years, from committing a slice of profits to Bitcoin, to self-custody, to this public statement, rests on an assumption that the company generates steady cash to support positions held outside its reserve line. Third-quarter operating profit of $1.5 billion validates that assumption today. But the earnings of a stablecoin issuer depend heavily on USDT supply and on short-term rates, and if either moves, the mismatch between corporate assets and reserves stops being an accounting question and becomes a reputational one, and reputational problems move considerably faster than accounting ones.

Three things follow from here. Whether other institutions press the same question after the Lugano forum. Whether the next quarterly report changes the disclosure structure. And when the KPMG audit concludes and what it finds. The first two determine how the market reads those fifty thousand coins. The third determines which line of the balance sheet they end up on.
