## Key Facts

- **The potential sale**: Nomura Holdings is reportedly weighing a sale of its cryptocurrency subsidiary Laser Digital, with talks held with potential buyers including Marex Group.
- **No decision yet**: The company has yet to make a final decision and could keep the business.
- **The earnings backdrop**: Nomura's European businesses posted a pretax loss of 7.4 billion yen in the quarter ended September 30, 2026, a fourth consecutive quarterly loss, while every other reporting region posted pretax profits over the same period.
- **What was not disclosed**: Nomura did not say whether Laser Digital, which is headquartered in Switzerland, contributed to the European result.
- **The timing**: The potential sale comes less than three months after Laser Digital Japan secured the country's first new crypto exchange license in four years, allowing it to expand institutional crypto trading services in Japan.

## Competing Views

**The loss is concentrated, not general.** Four straight losing quarters occurred in one reporting region while every other region turned a pretax profit, which says the problem is not a broad failure of a diversified financial group in digital assets but something specific to one region and one line. That concentration is what makes a sale logically coherent: it carves out an asset with clean boundaries that can be priced separately.

**License and sale point in opposite directions inside the same three months.** The subsidiary won the first new exchange license in Japan in four years, which opened institutional trading services, and the parent is now weighing disposal. Both facts are reported, and reading them together is more informative than either alone.

**The buyer's name suggests a second reading.** Marex Group is itself an active market maker, so if a deal proceeds, the operating business and the license are more likely to be repriced inside a different framework than the one the seller used.

**The non-disclosure is itself material.** Nomura did not say whether Laser Digital contributed to the European result, and that specific gap is the central information asymmetry in any sale negotiation. What the buyer is pricing is precisely the profit attribution.

## Wider Perspective

A license landing while the business is up for sale is a more telling contrast than the loss figure alone. The hard problem in institutional crypto has never been whether a regulator will grant permission. Licenses are scarce and act as a barrier to entry. The hard problem is whether compliance and operating costs can be spread over enough business once granted. Nomura winning Japan's first new license in four years shows the regulatory channel has opened. The parent weighing disposal at the same moment shows the economics behind that channel have not yet been proven.

Disclosures matter here beyond the headline number. Because the loss sits in one reporting region while every other region posted pretax profits, the asset has clear boundaries and can be priced in isolation, which is what makes a carve-out workable rather than a concession. And the fact that the company would not say whether the Swiss-headquartered crypto subsidiary contributed to the European result identifies the one fact a buyer most needs. If the sale completes, the actual price becomes a public reference point for what a traditional Asian financial institution will pay for a license whose economics have not been demonstrated, which extends well beyond the transaction itself.
