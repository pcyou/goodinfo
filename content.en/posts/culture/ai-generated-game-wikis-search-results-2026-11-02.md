## Key Facts

As generative site-building tools spread, a wave of fake wikis is crowding out search results, filled with invented companions, abilities and settings. These sites target precisely the games whose player base is too small to have formed a community, and therefore lack reliable reference material.

## Event Details

- **Trigger**: While writing about Nighthawks, a journalist searched for basic information about the game's companion characters and clicked the first result, a site called the Nighthawks Wiki.
- **Nature of the site**: It was not a wiki at all. Instead of being editable by anyone, the site invited corrections by email only, and it carried information that was plainly false, listing companions and abilities that do not exist.
- **Tracing the source**: Following the domain name led to a site-building service called Spaceship, which also offers an AI website builder. An email sent to the site bounced.
- **Scale**: A follow-up search turned up many more similar sites, some genuinely editable as wikis and others only calling themselves by that name.
- **Target category**: They typically go after indie games and works without large player bases, the sort of game people seek help with and cannot find a thriving community to provide it.
- **Publisher response**: Hooded Horse chief executive Tim Bender said that, unlike most publishers, the company creates wikis for its own games and works with fans to make sure they are well populated with useful information. Lately it has had to play whack-a-mole with AI-generated sites competing for search results and providing inaccurate information.
- **A concrete case**: One such site, which appeared for the game Corsair Cove, was actually part of a scattershot collection of more than twenty games operated by a site called GameDB, whose creator admitted to using generative tools.

## Competing Views

**The publisher side frames the issue as an asymmetry in the cost of supply**. Hooded Horse building wikis for its own games treats content accuracy as an extension of the publishing process, which costs labour, while the generative sites spend compute. When two kinds of site compete for the same search position, the second can cover the keywords of dozens of games overnight while the first maintains them one by one. That structural cost gap, not the lapse of any single site-building platform, is the reason the problem keeps recurring.

**The researcher's methodological stance deserves separate mention**. The reporting explicitly declines to accuse anyone of using generative AI, because the hallmarks are hard to pin down and are often misattributed to the model rather than to the humans doing the copying, with a taste for em-dashes being a common false lead. The restraint is justified, since writing an unproven accusation into a report would also taint the genuinely human-written sites. The alternative approach was verifiable: tracing the domain to the site-building service and finding the operator's own admission, which puts the conclusion on evidence rather than inference.

**The operator's admission pushes responsibility further upstream**. The relevant collection operator admitted using generative tools, which means the problem is not one small site owner's personal decision but the fact that the tool itself makes bulk production almost free. When one owner admits to using it, the same tool is very likely in use by hundreds of others at the same time, and single-site takedowns have almost no effect.

**The effect on players is currently mostly indirect**. What the reporting establishes is that once search results are displaced, players either remain on false information or dig deeper to find the genuine community. The point is not whether any single entry is right, but that the search entry habit itself fails. Once players stop trusting the first result, community-maintained wikis lose their main source of traffic, which lowers the willingness of players to contribute, producing a self-reinforcing downward cycle.

**An unresolved question is how responsibility should be allocated**. Whether the providers of generative tools should carry liability for downstream content quality is unsettled. The tools do not produce these sites, but without low-cost bulk generation the site owners would not have built them. The chain is broken here, with the benefits accruing to the provider and the risks distributed across every user whose search results are polluted and every content ecosystem caught in between. This mismatch between upstream gains and downstream costs will recur through this round of generative AI entering content production, and a fan wiki is a cheap and measurable sample of it.

## Panoramic Analysis

The surface story is a game wiki being displaced by a fake one, but what it exposes is more general. Generative tools are driving the marginal cost of content supply toward zero, while search retrieval still allocates positions according to the old logic of scarce content.

The essence of search ranking is the ordering of scarcity. When a category of content, whether game guides, recipes, fault-troubleshooting advice or introductory material on an obscure topic, stays scarce because there is never enough good supply, generative tools fill exactly that gap. That explains why the casualties are all works with small audiences. For popular games, reference material is already saturated and the model has nothing to work with, but for titles such as Nighthawks, players genuinely search and cannot find a community large enough to answer. The tool fills the steepest part of the supply curve with precision.

The consequences run deeper than a few false entries. The reference-wiki ecosystems around small games were always fragile, sustained by volunteers. Volunteer effort presupposes that the work will be seen. Once search entry points are occupied by bulk-generated sites, that effort stops paying back, supply contracts further, and the next round of generated sites covers even more ground. This is a positive feedback loop, and it runs downward.

The description of whack-a-mole is telling. Building a wiki for your own game is a long-term investment, but when a competitor can cover twenty games in a night, the content race takes on a peculiar shape: resources flow not to the works that most need accurate reference, but to the ones whose search traffic is easiest to take. That systematically pulls community attention away from where it is needed and toward where it is most replaceable.

That sharpens the question of who is responsible. The provider of a generative tool can reasonably say it supplied a tool and produced no sites, yet without cheap bulk generation the sites would not exist. The chain breaks at that point, with the profit at the top and the risk spread thin across every user and every content ecosystem downstream. This same structural mismatch between upstream gains and downstream costs has appeared repeatedly as generative AI has moved into content industries, and a fan wiki is simply a low-cost and measurable instance of it.

Three directions are worth watching: whether search platforms impose stricter admission and demotion rules on thin bulk-generated pages, whether site-building services restrict the use of their platforms for this pattern, and whether community content ecosystems can find an entry point that does not depend entirely on general search ranking. The third matters most, because technical measures can only slow the pace of replacement. Whether fragile content ecosystems survive depends on whether creators and readers can rebuild a connection that bypasses the search engine.
