## Key Facts

A satellite built by the German startup Orbital Matter 3D-printed a half-meter long beam out of a custom plastic material in the vacuum of space, in what the company called a first for a free-flying spacecraft in orbit and a step toward constructing large structures in space.

- **How it flew**: the company's small satellite, Replicator 2, launched on a rideshare mission this summer with four of its 3D printers onboard. Each printer creates a rigid cylindrical beam by extruding coils of resin that are cured by ultraviolet light, which the company analogises to cement and water.
- **Why it matters**: future spacecraft will need to be larger to generate more electrical power and radiate more heat into the vacuum, particularly if they are to serve as orbital data centers. They must still fit inside the tight confines of the fairing on the rocket carrying them. If spacecraft can construct their own structures in orbit, the power and heat-dissipation problems become considerably easier.
- **The company statement**: chief executive and co-founder Jakub Stojek said the demonstration proves "we can simply build the array in space, at whatever size the mission demands". The company partnered with the European Space Agency and Thales Alenia on Replicator 2.
- **What went wrong**: two of the printers were designed to deploy a flexible solar array, but an anomaly caused one to fail. The boom that was created successfully deployed its solar array, which remains an important proof of concept.
- **Scale**: boom length is limited by the resin carried onboard, and engineers believe 100 metre booms are feasible. For comparison, the solar arrays on SpaceX's Starlink V2 satellites are 20 metres long.
- **Next step**: another demonstration is scheduled for 2028 on a larger satellite, deploying a solar array capable of generating 5 kw of power, described as a stepping stone toward deployments of 100 kw or more per wing by 2030.

## Competing Views

**The company's emphasis is on scalability.** Stojek framed the result as proof that an array can be built in space at whatever size a mission requires, rather than as a one-off result.

**The comparison with current practice is the more revealing part.** Today's large space structures are origami projects, with large solar arrays or telescope mirrors folded up and deployed on orbit, which is a difficult mechanical undertaking. NASA's funding of the idea includes a mission with Redwire that 3D-printed structural beams in a vacuum chamber in 2023 but never got off the ground.

**The demonstration also left an unresolved question.** One printer failed in orbit while the other completed its solar array deployment, which shows the four-printer redundancy design absorbing a single point of failure and also shows that reliability on orbit remains far below the level required for scaled deployment.

## Wider Perspective

The value of manufacturing in orbit comes down to a dimensional constraint. Spacecraft are limited by fairing volume, while power generation and heat rejection both demand that they become as large as possible, and those two requirements are in permanent tension. Conventional deployment concentrates the complexity into a single high-risk mechanical event, where one failure can cost an entire satellite. Manufacturing in orbit converts a structure from a finished article that must fit inside a fairing into something produced on demand where it is needed.

If that approach holds, the shape of space infrastructure changes structurally. The scale of an orbital data centre would no longer be bounded by launch cost or fairing dimensions, but only by propellant, radiator area and money. New constraints would take their place, all of them manufacturing ones. Material has to be extruded and cured in vacuum. Fluid behaviour and cure characteristics differ in microgravity. Every printer has to run unattended and reliably for hundreds of days.

The most informative part of this demonstration was the failure. One printer stopped working while another completed its solar array deployment, which shows the redundancy design doing its job and simultaneously shows that orbital printing reliability is not yet close to what scaled deployment would require. The company's approach has been to learn in orbit and adapt the software rather than spend years testing every possibility on the ground, which is a reasonable posture for a fast-iterating technology, though it also means every launch carries real cost.

The 2028 mission is the true test. A larger satellite, a larger printer, a five-kilowatt solar array and, most importantly, a manufacturing process validated in a real orbital environment. If that step lands, the discussion of orbital data centres stops being a concept drawing and becomes a plan with an engineering timetable.
