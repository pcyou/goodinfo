## Key Facts

- **The permit**: The Nuclear Regulatory Commission approved the construction permit application for four of X-energy's Xe-100 reactors at a Dow chemical plant in Texas.
- **Per-unit output**: Each reactor can generate 80 megawatts of electricity and 200 megawatts of heat.
- **The claimed first**: X-energy says the approval is the first for an advanced nuclear reactor at an industrial site in the United States.
- **Industry reality**: The U.S. nuclear industry has pinned its hopes on small modular reactors, whose proponents argue that shrinking the reactor speeds construction and reduces costs, though those advantages have yet to be proven. No SMR is currently in operation in the country.
- **Timeline**: X-energy says the Dow reactors will not be operational until the early 2030s.
- **Design**: X-energy claims its reactor, which flows helium gas over uranium-containing fuel pellets, is safer than many existing reactors.
- **The safety objection**: Edwin Lyman, director of nuclear power safety at the Union of Concerned Scientists, said the NRC only accomplished this by pushing consideration of critical safety questions to a later date, issues the NRC should have fully resolved before green lighting construction, and questioned the speed of the approval.
- **Demand side**: Hyperscalers and data center developers are desperate for electricity. Amazon's Climate Pledge Fund led X-energy's $500 million Series C-1, and the company has committed to buy 5 gigawatts of capacity from the startup.
- **Market reaction**: The approval gave the stock a small jolt that was only enough to recover last week's losses. Shares trade at less than half their initial offering price, after an April IPO that raised $1 billion.

## Competing Views

**The bullish logic is that miniaturization is the direct answer to nuclear's two structural problems.** An 80-megawatt unit sits inside the demand range of industrial heat users, and a construction permit moves the project from paper to an actual approved build, which is more than most nuclear timelines ever deliver.

**The critical logic targets process rather than engineering.** Lyman's objection is that the commission separated granting the permit from fully resolving critical safety questions, deferring the hard part to a later date, and he questioned how quickly the approval was obtained.

**The industry has no answer yet on cost or schedule.** No SMR has ever operated in the United States, so the advantages the sector sells have never been demonstrated in a real plant, and the Dow units will not run until the early 2030s. The economics arrive more than a decade from now.

**The hyperscalers' hunger is the most durable support in the story.** Amazon both led the funding round and committed to buy 5 gigawatts, which places a buyer inside the capital structure of a reactor manufacturer in a way the nuclear industry has rarely seen.

**Investors have stopped paying for the concept.** The permit produced a rally just large enough to undo the prior week's losses, and the stock still trades at less than half its IPO price, meaning the market credits milestones and nothing more.

## Panoramic Analysis

The novelty here is not the number four but the location. This is the first advanced reactor approved at an industrial site, and that is a different proposition from every demonstration project that came before it. Nuclear power has historically existed as a generation asset, selling to a grid and earning money through baseload volume and long-term power purchase agreements. A reactor built inside a chemical park, delivering 80 megawatts of electricity and 200 megawatts of heat, sells something else: continuous industrial steam and high-temperature process heat that can be consumed without interruption. Those contracts are structurally different from baseload power. The business looks less like an electricity producer and more like a utility supplier, and the revenue comes from a continuous thermal load rather than from a price that moves with the market.

That shift explains both why Dow was the first site and why the units will not run until the early 2030s. Industrial customers need process heat at very high availability, which is precisely the metric a first-of-a-kind plant struggles to deliver. It also puts the modular route's central marketing claim back under a spotlight: the promised advantages of faster construction and lower cost are claims that can only be checked against an actual bill, and that bill will not arrive for more than a decade.

The procedural dispute may outlast the technical one. The concern from the Union of Concerned Scientists is that the commission separated the permit from the resolution of critical safety questions, deferring the latter. On a first industrial-site approval that objection carries unusual weight, because every subsequent application of the same type will cite this precedent. If granting first and reviewing later becomes the normal, it stops being a case-specific question and becomes standard practice. The operating data from the first plant, including any unplanned shutdown, will largely decide how that precedent is finally judged.

The demand argument is harder than any previous nuclear revival cycle. What is driving X-energy is not a clean-energy constituency but hyperscalers in acute need of power: Amazon both led the round and committed to 5 gigawatts of offtake, writing long-term demand directly into the capital structure of a reactor manufacturer. Data center electricity demand is turning nuclear from an option into a requirement, and that demand curve has no visible reason to turn down soon.

The market's response supplies the cold footnote. A rally just large enough to cover the previous week's losses, with the stock still below half its offering price, says investors no longer pay for the concept and will credit only milestones approaching delivery. For X-energy the real test is the first unit in the early 2030s and the cost and schedule figures disclosed after it. Every permit approved until then postpones the question rather than answering it.
