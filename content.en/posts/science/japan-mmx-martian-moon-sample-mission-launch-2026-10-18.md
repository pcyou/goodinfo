[Core Summary]

JAXA is scheduled to launch the Martian Moons eXploration (MMX) mission on October 20 from the Tanegashima Space Center, aiming to collect at least 10 grams of material from Phobos and return it to Earth within five years. The mission is the first sample-return effort ever directed at a Martian moon, and will also include the first close flyby of Deimos.

[Event Details]

- **Launch plan**: JAXA will launch MMX on an H3 rocket from Tanegashima Space Center in Kagoshima Prefecture in the early hours of October 20 Japan time. The launch window will remain open for several weeks to accommodate weather and technical conditions.
- **Mission objective**: MMX will spend roughly three years in the vicinity of Phobos, collecting at least 10 grams of surface and shallow subsurface material, before beginning the return journey. The probe is expected to re-enter Earth's atmosphere around 2031, releasing a small capsule containing the samples.
- **Extended mission**: Before reaching Phobos, MMX will perform the first-ever close flyby of the smaller Deimos, gathering data on its composition, surface features and gravity field to compare the two moons' formation hypotheses.
- **International cooperation**: The mission is led by JAXA, with scientific instruments and deep-space communications support from the European Space Agency, and a neutron and gamma-ray spectrometer plus navigation assistance from NASA. Research institutions in Germany, France and Switzerland contributed to several key instruments.
- **Key science question**: The mission's central debate is the origin of the Martian moons. The dominant hypothesis holds that Phobos and Deimos are captured asteroids, but more recent work suggests they could be fragments thrown off Mars by a giant impact. MMX will deliver the first direct evidence.
- **Technical lineage**: MMX is widely seen as the next step after JAXA's Hayabusa2 asteroid sample-return mission, which successfully returned material from asteroid Ryugu in 2020. JAXA's iterative investment in deep-space sampling and atmospheric reentry technology is the foundation that makes MMX possible.

[Multiple Perspectives]

- **JAXA and Japanese academia**: Mission scientists note that MMX's primary science target is not Mars itself but its two moons. Ryosuke Kawakatsu, professor at the University of Tokyo and MMX chief scientist, has said that even a 10-gram sample from Phobos would be enough to reshape humanity's understanding of the moons' origin, and indirectly affect the reconstruction of Mars' early impact history.
- **European Space Agency**: ESA Director of Science Carole Mundell said MMX will give European scientists systematic access to Martian moon material for the first time, and the Deimos flyby will provide data to inform more complex future Mars-system missions.
- **NASA**: The U.S. space agency's Mars exploration program scientists view MMX and future Mars sample-return efforts as complementary — one explores Mars' past, the other its moon family, and combining the two will help draw a complete picture of Mars system evolution.
- **Critics and skeptics**: Some planetary scientists caution that the mission's success depends heavily on whether Phobos' surface will allow safe landing and sampling. If the regolith is too loose or fine, the sampling mechanism may fail to acquire a sufficient quantity, narrowing the scientific return.

[Full Perspective]

1. **A long-standing mystery about the Martian moons may finally be settled**

For decades, scientists have accumulated orbital and spectral data on Phobos and Deimos, but no direct evidence exists to settle whether they are captured asteroids or fragments from a Mars-impact. Once MMX's samples are analyzed for isotopes and mineralogy, researchers can directly compare their composition with Martian meteorites and outer-belt asteroids, potentially resolving a multi-decade puzzle of solar system history by the early 2030s.

2. **From Hayabusa2 to MMX: Japan's deep-space sampling system matures**

MMX is JAXA's second major deep-space sampling mission after Hayabusa2. The two share critical technology — ion engine propulsion, deep-space communications and atmospheric reentry capsules. Japan's strategy of "continually refining one class of technology and steadily taking on one class of mission" contrasts with the U.S. and Europe's emphasis on large flagship projects, giving JAXA a distinctive position in medium-budget deep-space sampling.

3. **A new template for international cooperation**

In MMX, ESA provides scientific payloads and deep-space communications, NASA contributes instruments and navigation, Japan supplies the platform, launch and sampling, and universities in France, Germany and Switzerland play key roles in instrument development. This "platform country plus multi-country payloads" model eases budget pressure on any single nation while distributing the scientific return widely, and may become a template for complex planetary missions in the 2030s.

4. **Indirect value for crewed Mars exploration**

Even though MMX's science output focuses on the Martian moons, the engineering data — long-duration deep-space cruise, autonomous landing on a low-gravity body, atmospheric reentry — is directly relevant to crewed Mars exploration. NASA and ESA both treat MMX as a pathfinder for crewed Mars missions planned for the late 2030s and early 2040s.
