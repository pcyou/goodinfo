## Super El Niño Could Cause 451,000 Excess Deaths, Researchers Warn

**[Core Summary]** On 23 September 2026, Reuters and other outlets reported new research warning that the developing super El Niño is projected to cause roughly 451,000 excess deaths between winter 2026 and February 2027, primarily driven by extreme heat. The researchers emphasised that the event has already broken multiple historical temperature records before reaching its peak, with intensity and pace of development far exceeding any previous El Niño, posing a serious challenge to global public health and climate adaptation systems.

According to the research team's projection model, excess mortality will be driven primarily by extreme heat events, compounded by prolonged heatwaves, stubbornly high overnight temperatures and unusually high humidity. The study covers multiple low- and mid-latitude countries, with South Asia, Southeast Asia, sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Latin America expected to be the most severely affected regions. The researchers stressed that these figures are initial estimates based on the best available data, and actual outcomes will vary depending on the public-health response capacity of each country.

**[Panoramic Perspective]** The excess-mortality risk posed by a super El Niño extends far beyond a purely meteorological event — it has profound implications for global public-health systems, urban infrastructure and economic development patterns. From the standpoint of climate adaptation capacity, the extreme heat associated with El Niño disproportionately affects older adults, people with chronic conditions, outdoor workers and low-income groups. These populations often lack effective air conditioning, medical resources and the institutional arrangements that would allow flexible adjustment of outdoor working hours, leaving them considerably more vulnerable during heatwaves than the general population.

From an urban-infrastructure perspective, many cities in developing regions were not designed with adequate shelter capacity for extreme heat scenarios. The urban heat-island effect amplifies the health impact of El Niño events, leaving already heat-stressed cities under even greater pressure during the event window. This highlights the importance of mainstreaming climate adaptation into urban planning and infrastructure investment.

From a global food-security perspective, El Niño events are typically accompanied by widespread drought, flooding and reduced crop yields worldwide. Because the current super El Niño has already broken multiple temperature records before reaching its peak, the subsequent shock to global food production may well exceed historical experience. This poses new challenges for global food trade patterns, strategic reserves and humanitarian assistance mechanisms.

**[Multiple Perspectives]** Those stressing the urgency of climate action are concentrated among climate scientists, environmental organisations and parts of the public-health community. They argue that the research findings once again underscore the importance of global temperature targets and that governments need to accelerate delivery of emission-reduction commitments and step up investment in climate-adaptation infrastructure. This camp also calls for stronger international cooperation and more effective early-warning systems and extreme-weather response coordination.

More cautious views on the methodology come mainly from some statisticians and meteorologists, who broadly agree with the direction of the research but caution that long-horizon projection models of this kind carry substantial inherent uncertainty. They argue that the headline figures should be treated as reference ranges rather than precise predictions, and that the scientific community should interpret the results with appropriate caution to avoid placing undue weight on a single number and obscuring the broader systemic risks.

Voices attentive to social-equity dimensions come predominantly from the development-research community, who emphasise that the excess mortality from El Niño is unevenly distributed and that low-income countries and vulnerable groups bear a disproportionate share of the health burden. They argue that the international community should adopt more targeted approaches when providing climate assistance, avoiding one-size-fits-all adaptation measures that risk failing to reach the most vulnerable populations.
