## Core Summary

The BBC reported on September 16 that the latest satellite data shows the Earth has lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland and Antarctica over the past 47 years, a staggering figure highlighting the profound impact of climate change on polar ice sheets. Scientists warn that at current melt rates, global sea levels could rise several meters by the end of the century, threatening the survival of coastal cities and island nations.

## Event Details

According to satellite observation data disclosed by the BBC, from 1976 to 2023, Greenland and Antarctica combined have lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice over 47 years. Among them, Greenland loses about 270 billion tons of ice per year on average, while Antarctica loses about 150 billion tons per year. The data is derived from comprehensive measurements by multiple observation platforms including the European Space Agency's Sentinel satellites and the US ICESat-2 satellite. This data further corroborates the projections of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) that polar ice sheet melt is the primary driver of sea level rise.

## Panoramic Analysis

Polar ice sheet melt is not just a scientific issue but a major geopolitical concern. Sea level rise will threaten the survival space of coastal cities and low-lying island nations, potentially triggering a new wave of 'climate refugees.' At the same time, ice sheet melt will release massive amounts of fresh water, affecting the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation and thereby changing global climate patterns. The release of this satellite data may inject new urgency into the upcoming climate summit.
