## Scientists to Resurrect Woolly Mammoth With the Help of AI

### Key Summary

The New York Post reported on Saturday that a research coalition combining a major biotech startup with several university laboratories has announced a key breakthrough. By using artificial intelligence models to design gene editing pathways, the team has cultivated living cells that carry critical mammoth cold-tolerance gene traits in the laboratory, and expects to produce a modified cell line suitable for embryo transfer within three to five years. The advance is being hailed as a major milestone for the de-extinction field, even as ethical and ecological debates intensify.

### Event Details

The project is led by a startup called Colossal Biosciences, in partnership with Harvard Medical School, the University of Cambridge, and several institutions focused on Ice Age paleobiology. The team first performed whole-genome sequencing on mammoth remains recovered from Siberian permafrost, then trained a deep learning model to predict the gene loci that control key mammoth traits, including long fur, adipose metabolism, and cold-tolerant hemoglobin. The resulting model was used to design a precise editing strategy for the Asian elephant genome, the mammoth's closest living relative.

In a preprint released this week, the team reported that by editing more than twenty key loci simultaneously, they have successfully introduced mammoth-characteristic allele combinations into cells cultured in vitro, with notable changes in the expression patterns of genes linked to cold-adapted metabolism. The next step is to use artificial womb technology to grow edited embryos and evaluate whether traits such as fur density and subcutaneous fat depth can be faithfully recapitulated through development.

The company has pointed out that the same technology has clear medical applications, especially in treating metabolic disorders rooted in genetic defects. Several venture capital firms have announced follow-on investments, with cumulative funding for the project on track to exceed USD 300 million this year. However, ethicists and conservation biologists quickly raised objections, questioning whether spending huge sums to revive an extinct species is reasonable when conservation of living endangered species, including Asian elephants, remains underfunded.

### Full Scope Analysis

Since the woolly mammoth revival project was first floated in 2021, it has been regarded as a stress test for combining gene editing with artificial intelligence. The real scientific value of the project lies less in whether a living mammoth can be produced in a lab, and more in whether AI can precisely model the relationship between complex traits and multi-gene combinations, dramatically shortening the cycle of traditional directed breeding. Once the approach matures, the spillover effects could reach livestock breeding, endangered species rescue, and even the treatment of human genetic diseases.

At a broader level, the de-extinction research race is accelerating in several countries. Japanese and Korean institutions have launched projects to evaluate the feasibility of reviving the passenger pigeon and the Korean tiger, while Russia has been collaborating with international partners on Pleistocene permafrost ecosystem reconstruction. Taken together, these initiatives are coalescing into an emerging interdisciplinary field, sometimes called de-extinction biology, whose core is to use modern biotechnology to artificially restore lost ecological functions.

However, reviving a species is not the same as restoring an ecosystem. Even if a living mammoth is eventually produced, scientists still face a long list of practical questions: whether the animal can adapt to present-day Arctic grassland conditions, whether suitable habitat can be secured, and whether it can form a stable food web with existing species. Some conservation biologists worry that high-profile star projects like this attract disproportionate funding and media attention at the expense of frontline efforts to save species that are still alive today.

### Multiple Perspectives

Project supporters argue that reviving the woolly mammoth will significantly advance gene editing, AI-driven biological design, and artificial womb technologies, while in the longer term helping to repair Arctic permafrost ecosystems and slow the carbon release triggered by permafrost thaw, giving the work a clear, applied horizon.

Conservation organizations and ethicists push back. They argue that comparable funding directed toward conserving living endangered species, such as Asian elephants, rhinoceroses, and red wolves, would save more species. They also warn that pushing de-extinction projects before fully assessing ecological and social risks could open a slippery slope: once the technology is mature, it could be misused to revive species that are harmful to modern ecosystems.

Neutral research institutions stress that in science communication, the wide gap between achieving a cellular breakthrough and producing a living animal should be made explicit, to avoid excessive public optimism about project timelines. They also call on academic and industry players across countries to establish transparent ethics review and data-sharing mechanisms so that highly sensitive research remains under public scrutiny.
