## Key Facts

The current Ebola haemorrhagic fever outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo has spread to an eighth province, marking what health officials describe as one of the most serious outbreaks in the past decade and an acceleration rather than a containment. WHO and Africa CDC data indicate that approximately one quarter of confirmed deaths are children, underscoring the disproportionate burden on vulnerable groups. Multiple medical teams, aid agencies and local health authorities are running vaccination, contact tracing and community engagement in parallel, while insecurity, weak infrastructure and cross-border population movement remain major obstacles.

## What We Know

On October 18, AP, Reuters and CBC — citing the Democratic Republic of Congo's Ministry of Health, the World Health Organization and the Africa CDC — confirmed that the outbreak has now expanded to an eighth province, the largest geographic footprint since the current wave began. The outbreak has been identified as the Zaire ebolavirus strain, which carries a high case-fatality rate and has predominantly affected eastern and central provinces that overlap with active armed-conflict zones.

Multiple international aid agencies have deployed medical teams to support the response, focusing on contact tracing, the construction of isolation and treatment units, and the targeted deployment of Ebola vaccines. However, persistent armed conflict in eastern Congo, security risks for healthcare workers and supply convoys, and chronically weak local health infrastructure have all slowed the response. Africa CDC has issued alerts on cross-border transmission, urging neighbouring countries to strengthen screening at border crossings and community-level surveillance.

A particularly striking feature of this outbreak is the disproportionate toll on children. WHO and partner agencies have noted that roughly one quarter of confirmed deaths are minors — a pattern that reflects local family structures, prior immunisation histories and weak baseline immunity in the affected zones. Experts emphasise that reducing paediatric mortality will be a key performance indicator in the next phase of the response.

## Wider Perspective

### 1. Cross-border spread and pressure on regional public health systems

Reaching eight provinces materially increases the risk that cases will spill across borders. Neighbouring Uganda, Rwanda, Burundi and Tanzania have limited border-screening capacity, and a confirmed cross-border case would trigger a wider secondary response. The episode is pushing Africa CDC and WHO to accelerate joint contingency planning, cross-border epidemiological investigations and shared surveillance-data protocols at the regional level.

### 2. Inequitable access to vaccines and therapeutics

Although effective vaccines against Zaire ebolavirus now exist, real-world access in outbreak zones is limited by cold-chain logistics, security conditions and the availability of trained medical staff. The current outbreak is likely to revive the long-running global push for more reliable stockpiles and localised production capacity, and to strengthen mechanisms for fair allocation of vaccines and monoclonal therapies.

### 3. Difficulty of health responses in conflict zones

The overlap between outbreak geography and active conflict zones adds a security premium to every part of the response — from the deployment of healthcare workers and supplies to the construction and operation of treatment units. International agencies will need to combine sustained engagement with armed actors, more flexible security protocols and stronger community trust mechanisms, a recurring lesson from the past two years of Ebola response work.

### 4. Why children are so heavily affected

The roughly one-in-four share of paediatric deaths reflects long-standing gaps in baseline immunisation, family-level health literacy and nutrition in the affected areas. The next phase of the response will need to integrate paediatric bed capacity, antibody therapy and community-level maternal-and-child health education into the core agenda, not just emergency ring vaccination.

## Viewpoints Compared

- **WHO and Africa CDC:** Stress that the outbreak is still evolving. Priorities are scaling up vaccination, accelerating cross-border coordination and protecting healthcare workers, with calls for additional international financing and personnel.
- **DRC Ministry of Health:** Has announced intensified contact tracing and isolation in outbreak zones, is coordinating with WHO and NGOs to expand laboratory testing capacity, and has launched rapid-response teams into the newly affected province.
- **International aid organisations (MSF, ICRC and others):** Flag the safety risks facing healthcare workers and supply convoys in conflict-affected zones and urge all parties to the conflict to guarantee humanitarian access.
- **Outbreak-zone communities and parents' associations:** Demand dedicated paediatric care capacity — including paediatric beds, family education and psychosocial support — and call for more equitable and transparent allocation of aid supplies.
