## [Brief] CDC Survey Finds Nearly Half of US GLP-1 Users Alter How They Take Their Medication, Mostly Over Cost

## Key Facts

- **Study**: Researchers at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention broke down survey data on the use of GLP-1 medications collected between July and August 2025, publishing the results in the agency's Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report.
- **Sample**: More than 8,000 United States adults responded.
- **Prevalence**: The researchers estimate that about one in ten US adults, or 9.8 percent, used a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist medication in 2025, which works out to roughly 27 million adults.
- **Deviation from prescription**: Among users, 44 percent reported using their medication in a way different from how it was prescribed, including skipping doses, delaying refills and using smaller doses.
- **Primary reason**: More than half of the people modifying their use cited cost or availability as the reason.
- **Age gap**: Among adults aged 14 to 39 who modified their use, 58 percent cited cost as a barrier, compared with just 20 percent of adults aged 65 and over.
- **Indications**: These peptide-based medications are widely used for diabetes, weight loss and a growing range of connected chronic conditions, including cardiovascular disease and obstructive sleep apnea.

## Event Details

A study published this week by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimates that about 27 million American adults used a GLP-1 medication in 2025, roughly one in ten of the adult population. Among those users, 44 percent said they were taking the medication differently from what was prescribed, by skipping doses, delaying refills or using smaller doses than directed.

The reason matters more than the behaviour. More than half of the people who changed how they used the medication pointed to cost or availability, and the pattern is sharply age-dependent: 58 percent of users between 14 and 39 who altered their use cited cost, against 20 percent of users aged 65 and above.

The finding arrives at a moment when these drugs have moved well beyond diabetes and weight management into cardiovascular disease and obstructive sleep apnea, which widens the population exposed to the behaviour and makes the gap between prescribed and actual use more consequential than it would be for a single indication.

## Competing Views

**One view reads the deviation as an access problem rather than a compliance problem.** Putting cost and availability first in the respondents' own explanations means the people in the sample were facing a practical constraint rather than a simple absence of willingness to follow the prescription.

**A second view is concerned with what altered dosing does to benefit and safety.** Reduced dosing may affect glycaemic control and the pace of weight loss, and for patients on additional medicines, frequent dose changes also widen the information gap between what they are taking and what their prescriber believes they are taking.

**A third view notes the limits of the data.** The study relies on self-reported survey responses from people who reported medication use, so the representativeness of the findings for the wider medication-taking population still needs to be tested against other sources.

## Panoramic Analysis

The value of this study is not about the medication itself. It is that it quantifies something widely assumed but rarely measured: once a compound that was once promoted for obesity and a range of chronic conditions becomes the daily medicine of tens of millions of people, what determines the result is frequently not the prescription but the price and the payment arrangement. The age distribution of the finding makes a second point: price sensitivity is not spread evenly across the population but tracks closely with age, which is why a single narrative about affordability cannot explain what is happening.

The second layer concerns how well the health system can respond to this. The clinical benefit of these medications is built on regulated use, and with more than four in ten users deviating, any management path built around titration is resting on an unreliable premise. What is more difficult is that people reducing doses on their own do not necessarily tell their doctors, which means the data available to a prescriber is already skewed before any conversation about the deviation takes place.

The third layer is what the finding implies for public policy. If price and availability are the principal reasons for deviation, then simply widening eligibility or insurance coverage without reducing what patients actually pay out of pocket will deliver less than expected, because the obstacle is not access itself but the cost of access.

Four indicators are worth watching: how prices and patient cost-sharing change after the next round of negotiation; how medication behaviour differs across insurance types; whether subsequent research cross-checks these self-reported figures against actual pharmacy dispensing records; and whether specific clinical guidance is issued for the population that alters dosing.
