## Core Summary

U.S. President Trump has signed an executive order requiring the measles, mumps, and rubella combination vaccine (MMR) to be split into separate injections, attempting to override the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) vaccination schedule. The American Academy of Pediatrics strongly opposes this move, calling it a "dangerous recommendation" that could leave millions of children unprotected against serious infectious diseases.

## Event Details

According to BBC and The Guardian reporting, the executive order signed by President Trump requires health departments to reassess current childhood vaccination schedules, specifically splitting the long-standing combined MMR vaccine into three separate vaccines.

This decision directly challenges CDC's vaccination recommendations spanning decades. CDC's Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP), based on extensive scientific research, has consistently recommended combining measles, mumps, and rubella vaccines into a single injection to improve vaccination coverage and reduce missed doses.

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) quickly issued a statement strongly opposing the executive order. The organization pointed out that splitting the MMR vaccine will increase the number of injections required, potentially reducing parental compliance and leading to decreased vaccination rates. AAP called this a "dangerous recommendation," warning it could trigger outbreaks of highly contagious diseases like measles.

The New York Times fact-checking report noted that several of Trump's claims about childhood vaccines lack scientific basis. Public health experts worry this policy change could further erode public confidence in vaccination.

## Panoramic Perspective

This executive order reflects the deep contradictions currently facing U.S. public health policy: the tension between scientific consensus and political intervention is intensifying.

From a scientific perspective, combined MMR vaccination is best practice verified over decades. Combining three vaccines into one injection not only improves vaccination efficiency but also reduces children's doctor visits and parents' transportation and time costs. Historical experience shows that complicating vaccination schedules often leads to decreased coverage. In 1998, Britain faced similar controversy when false research triggered MMR vaccine panic, causing vaccination rates to plummet from 92% to below 80%, followed by a significant increase in measles cases.

From a policy-making perspective, the Trump administration's attempt to bypass CDC's professional judgment and directly intervene in vaccination schedules raises concerns about scientific independence. CDC's vaccination recommendations are based on large-scale clinical trials and epidemiological data, not political considerations. The executive order's override of professional agency recommendations may set a dangerous precedent, undermining the scientific foundation of the public health system.

More broadly, this controversy reflects the ongoing influence of the anti-vaccine movement globally. After the COVID-19 pandemic, vaccine hesitancy has increased in many countries. As an important global public health leader, changes in U.S. vaccine policy may have demonstration effects on vaccination confidence in other countries.

Public health experts warn that if MMR vaccination rates decline, the United States may face renewed epidemics of diseases like measles and mumps, which are especially dangerous for children and adults with weakened immune systems.

**Trump Administration**: Claims this move provides parents with more choices and questions combination vaccine safety, but failed to provide scientific evidence supporting its claims.

**Public Health Experts**: Warn this policy could undermine public confidence in vaccination and potentially trigger outbreaks of preventable diseases.

**Parent Groups**: Reactions are mixed; some parents support more choice, but many say they trust CDC's scientific recommendations and worry policy changes will affect their children's health.
