## Core Summary

According to Reuters, the latest report from the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) shows that global funding for HIV prevention, testing, treatment, and care dropped 18% over the past year. This marks the first significant funding decline in many years, breaking a long-term trend of steady increases. The report warns that the funding shortfall could lead to a resurgence of the HIV pandemic worldwide, particularly in heavily affected regions such as sub-Saharan Africa. International organizations are calling on governments and international bodies to recommit financial support for HIV prevention.

## Event Details

The UNAIDS global HIV report indicates that total global spending on HIV prevention, testing, treatment, and care fell 18% in 2025 compared to the previous year. This is the largest funding decline since 2010, breaking the trend of steadily increasing AIDS funding over many years.

The report identifies several key reasons for the funding reduction: traditional donor countries facing domestic fiscal pressures and cutting foreign aid budgets; uneven global economic recovery post-COVID with many nations prioritizing domestic reconstruction; and geopolitical tensions causing an overall contraction in international development aid.

Sub-Saharan Africa, home to nearly two-thirds of all people living with HIV globally, is the most severely affected region. Its HIV programs are highly dependent on international aid, making the funding cuts particularly devastating. Multiple countries have already been forced to scale back testing and treatment programs, with some testing sites closing entirely.

The UNAIDS Executive Director stated that funding cuts will directly prevent millions from accessing essential HIV testing and treatment services. This will not only increase new infections but also worsen conditions for those already infected, leading to more AIDS-related deaths.

The report also notes that reduced funding could seriously undermine long-term progress in HIV prevention. Over the past two decades, the international community has achieved remarkable results, with significant declines in both new infections and AIDS-related deaths. But if funding remains insufficient, these gains could be reversed.

## Panoramic Perspective

The decline in global HIV funding reflects deeper shifts in the international development aid landscape. Traditional donor countries face domestic fiscal pressures and political shifts, leading to tighter foreign aid budgets. Meanwhile, emerging economies have not yet fully integrated into traditional aid systems, creating a gap in global public health funding.

From a public health perspective, reduced HIV funding could trigger cascading effects. HIV is closely linked to tuberculosis, malaria, and other infectious diseases. Weakening HIV prevention systems undermines the effectiveness of the entire public health infrastructure. Additionally, funding cuts could slow HIV research progress, affecting the development of new drugs and treatments.

At the socioeconomic level, an HIV resurgence would negatively impact economic development in affected countries. HIV primarily affects working-age populations, and a resurgence would lead to reduced labor force, lower productivity, and increased medical burdens. World Bank research shows HIV has a significant negative impact on GDP growth in affected countries.

From a global health governance perspective, this funding crisis exposes the vulnerability of international public health financing mechanisms. Current mechanisms overly rely on a few donor countries, lacking diversified funding sources and sustainable financing models. The international community needs to explore innovative financing mechanisms, such as global taxes and financial transaction taxes, to ensure stable public health funding.

## Multiple Perspectives

UNAIDS and international health organizations strongly urge governments to increase HIV funding, emphasizing this is a necessary investment in global health security. They note that every dollar invested in HIV prevention yields multiple times the economic and social return.

Some traditional donor governments state they must reprioritize spending amid domestic fiscal pressures, but emphasize they continue supporting HIV prevention, potentially shifting from direct financial aid to technical support and capacity building.

Affected country governments and civil society organizations express serious concern about funding cuts. They note that reduced international aid places enormous pressure on national budgets, making it difficult to bear the full cost of HIV prevention independently. They call on the international community to honor commitments and not undo years of progress.

Public health experts emphasize that HIV prevention is not just a health issue but also a human rights and social justice issue. Funding cuts will worsen health inequalities and affect the health rights of the most vulnerable populations. They call for integrating HIV prevention into universal health coverage frameworks to ensure everyone can access necessary services.
